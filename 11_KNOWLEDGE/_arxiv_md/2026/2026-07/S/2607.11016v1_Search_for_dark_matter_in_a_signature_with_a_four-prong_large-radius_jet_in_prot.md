Search for dark matter in a signature with a four-prong

The CMS Collaboration*
Abstract
A search for a pair of nonprompt dark matter (DM) candidates produced in associ-
ation with an initial-state radiation jet, in a signature containing a four-prong large-
radius jet, is presented. The signal model contains a heavy vector or axial-vector
mediator, which produces long-lived dark-sector particles that decay to a stable DM
particle and a light boson, which decays to quarks. The analysis is based on data
collected in the years 2016–2018 with the CMS detector at the LHC in proton-proton
collisions at √s = 13 TeV, corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 138 fb−1. Sig-
nal candidates feature large-radius jets, which are identified using a jet substructure
tagger based on a graph neural network. The large-radius jet aims to reconstruct the
decay of light DM mediators into four quarks, which are produced in association with
two stable DM particles. The standard model background contributions are estimated
from data using dedicated control regions. The missing transverse momentum spec-
trum is probed for a potential signal over the expected background. No significant
excess over the standard model expectation is observed. Upper limits at 95% confi-
dence level are set on the signal strength as functions of either the mediator mass or
the relevant coupling. This is the first search for a pair of nonprompt DM candidates
in the Lorentz-boosted topology, characterized by a large-radius jet and large missing
transverse momentum.

Introduction
While the nature of dark matter (DM) remains unknown, many extensions of the standard
model (SM) predict a richer dark sector containing additional, potentially long-lived particles
that can be produced at colliders and decay at macroscopic distances within the detectors. This
paper investigates nonprompt DM signatures, which can be regarded as an extension of the
simplified DM models [1], as outlined in Ref. [2]. The model used in this paper incorporates
displaced secondary vertices characterized by the mass and lifetime of long-lived particles.
Feynman diagrams of the studied process are shown in Fig. 1. A pair of Dirac fermions χ2
is produced via a vector or axial-vector mediator Y1. Each χ2 particle decays into a stable
fermion χ1 and a scalar particle Y0. The Y0 boson is assumed to subsequently decay into a
pair of SM quarks. Depending on the choice of the model parameters, the χ2 particle may be
long-lived, resulting in a nonprompt decay with respect to the primary interaction point. In
this model, DM production proceeds via a heavy mediator that couples the SM to a dark sector
containing additional particles. The characteristic signature arises from the production of long-
lived particles that decay nonpromptly within the detector (“displaced decays”), giving rise
to displaced hadronic activity accompanied by missing transverse momentum (pmiss

In this search we investigate the topology in which the mediator Y1 is produced in association
with a jet from initial-state radiation (ISR) with high transverse momentum pT. This require-
ment enables efficient selection of candidate signal events, despite the relatively high thresh-
olds of jet-based triggers in the CMS experiment at the CERN LHC, and enables the exploration
of regions with low mediator mass. In all benchmark scenarios considered in this analysis, the
Y0 boson mass is fixed 1 GeV. At this mass, the Y0 boson is assumed to decay promptly and
predominantly into light-flavor quark pairs. Because of the large Lorentz boost of the Y1 sys-
tem and the small masses of the particles in the decay chain, the two χ2 decays are expected to
be collimated. The analysis therefore targets events in which the four quarks from the two Y0
decays are reconstructed as a single large-radius jet with a four-prong substructure.
Because of their large Lorentz boost, the decay products of the two Y0 mediators are recon-
structed as a single large-radius jet. For long χ2 lifetimes, the decay products of the two χ2 par-
ticles may originate from spatially separated displaced vertices; in this case, the large-radius
jet reconstruction remains sensitive to the boosted topology, but the reconstruction of the jet
substructure and subjet directions becomes less efficient. A jet tagger based on a graph neural
network (GNN) is employed to identify signal-like jets based on their substructure. The tagger
output is used to define the signal region (SR) and background-enriched control regions (CRs).
The CRs are used to estimate the dominant background contributions from events composed
uniquely of jets produced through the strong interaction, referred to as quantum chromody-

2
The CMS detector
The CMS apparatus [4, 5] is a multipurpose, nearly hermetic detector, designed to trigger on [6–
8] and identify electrons, muons, photons, and (charged and neutral) hadrons [9–11]. Its central
feature is a superconducting solenoid of 6 m internal diameter, providing a magnetic field of
3.8 T. Within the solenoid volume are a silicon pixel and strip tracker, a lead tungstate crystal
electromagnetic calorimeter (ECAL), and a brass and scintillator hadron calorimeter (HCAL),
each composed of a barrel and two endcap sections. Forward calorimeters extend the pseu-
dorapidity coverage provided by the barrel and endcap detectors. Muons are reconstructed
using gas-ionization detectors interleaved with the layers of the steel flux-return yoke outside
the solenoid. More detailed descriptions of the CMS detector, together with a definition of the
coordinate system used and the relevant kinematic variables, can be found in Refs. [4, 5].
Events of interest are selected using a two-tiered trigger system. The first level, composed of
custom hardware processors, uses information from the calorimeters and muon detectors to
select events at a rate of around 100 kHz within a fixed latency of 4 µs [6]. The second level,
known as the high-level trigger, consists of a farm of processors running a version of the full
event reconstruction software optimized for fast processing, and reduces the event rate to a few
kHz before data storage [7, 8].
3
Event simulation
Simulated samples of signal and background events are generated using various MC genera-
tors, and passed through a detailed simulation of the CMS detector implemented in GEANT4 [12].
They are processed with the same version of the CMS event reconstruction software used for
actual collision data samples. Due to the changes in the accelerator and detector configurations
and conditions, separate samples are produced for each data-taking year.
Samples of signal events are simulated at leading order (LO) using MADGRAPH5 aMC@NLO
2.6.5 [13]. The main parameters of the signal model are the coupling strength gχ2χ1Y0 of the
χ2χ1Y0 vertex, as well as the masses of the particles, all the other coupling strengths are set
to 1. In this search, we consider a range of values for that coupling strength and different
combinations of particle masses, as listed in Table 1. The coupling strength affects the lifetime
of the χ2 particle and therefore impacts the displacement of its decay vertex with respect to
the main interaction vertex. The mass of the Y1 boson affects the kinematic properties of the
signal, in particular the opening angle between the two χ2 particles from the Y1 decay and,
consequently, the angular separation among the final-state light-flavour quarks from the two
subsequent Y0 decays.

The benchmark scenarios considered in this analysis are not intended to provide an exhaustive
exploration of the full five-dimensional parameter space of the model, but rather to demon-

scans in order to isolate the impact of the displacement from that of the Lorentz boost on the
reconstruction and tagging performance. This analysis represents a first study of this experi-

as representative proof-of-concept scenarios.

Event reconstruction and selection
The global event reconstruction, also called particle-flow (PF) event reconstruction [23], aims to
reconstruct and identify each individual particle in an event, with an optimized combination
of all subdetector information. In this process, the identification of the particle type (photon,
electron, muon, charged hadron, neutral hadron) plays an important role in the determination
of the particle direction and energy. Photons are identified as ECAL energy clusters not linked
to the extrapolation of any charged-particle trajectory to the ECAL. Electrons are identified as
a primary charged-particle track and potentially many ECAL energy clusters corresponding to
this track’s extrapolation to the ECAL and to possible bremsstrahlung photons emitted along


## Page 6


4
the way through the tracker material. Muons are identified as tracks in the central tracker
consistent with either a track or several hits in the muon system, and associated with cal-
orimeter deposits compatible with the muon hypothesis. Charged hadrons are identified as
charged-particle tracks not linked to electrons or muons. Finally, neutral hadrons are identified
as HCAL energy clusters not linked to any charged-hadron trajectory, or as a combined ECAL
and HCAL energy excess with respect to the expected charged-hadron energy deposit.
The energy of photons is obtained from the ECAL measurement. The energy of electrons is
determined from a combination of the track momentum at the main interaction vertex, the cor-
responding ECAL cluster energy, and the energy sum of all bremsstrahlung photons attached
to the track. The energy of muons is obtained from the corresponding track momentum. The
energy of charged hadrons is determined from a combination of the track momentum and the
corresponding ECAL and HCAL energies, corrected for the response function of the calorime-
ters to hadronic showers. Finally, the energy of neutral hadrons is obtained from the corre-
sponding corrected ECAL and HCAL energies.
Hadronic jets are clustered from the PF candidates using the infrared- and collinear-safe anti-kT
algorithm [24, 25] with a distance parameter of 0.4 or 0.8 (AK4 or AK8 jets, respectively). The
pileup-per-particle identification (PUPPI) algorithm [26, 27] is used to mitigate pileup effects at
the reconstructed-particle level. Jet energy corrections are derived from simulation to bring the
measured response of jets to that of particle-level jets on average. In situ measurements of the
momentum balance in dijet, photon+jet, Z+jet, and multijet events are used to account for any
residual differences in the jet energy scale (JES) between data and simulation [28]. Additional
selection criteria are applied to each jet to remove jets potentially dominated by anomalous
contributions from various subdetector components or reconstruction failures. Small-radius
AK4 jets are only used in the online trigger reconstruction for the calculation of the scalar
transverse momentum sum of jets.

A combination of triggers is employed requiring events to have high values of the scalar pT
sum of all AK4 jets in an event or AK8 jets with high pT or trimmed mass [30]. The AK8 jets
reconstructed offline are required to have pT > 200 GeV and |η| < 2.5, and pass the tight jet
identification requirement [31]. In this search, we require the presence of at least two AK8 jets
satisfying the aforementioned criteria to select events compatible with the expected signature
of one ISR jet and the decay of a Lorentz-boosted Y1 boson into a χ2χ2 pair. The pT-leading AK8
jet in the event is additionally required to have pT > 500 GeV to ensure high trigger efficiency.
A GNN-based classifier is used to identify the signal AK8 jets based on their substructure.
The interaction network architecture [32] is utilized: a graph is formed to represent the pro-
cess, where the objects of interest—namely the jet constituents (particles) characterized by their
four-momenta—are represented by nodes and are connected to each other via edges. The in-
teractions and correlations between the nodes are taken into account by the classifier (tagger)
trained to identify the signal jet.
The graph is constructed using the reconstructed PF candidates associated with the AK8 jet,

supplemented with secondary vertex information to enhance sensitivity to displaced topolo-
gies. Each node is characterized by kinematic and tracking-related observables, while the graph
edges encode pairwise relations between constituents. A fully connected graph topology is em-
ployed, allowing the interaction network to learn nonlocal correlations among jet constituents.
The relational information is embedded through a sequence of fully connected layers acting
on the node and edge representations, followed by aggregation operations used to construct a
jet-level representation and classification score.
The tagger is trained using simulated signal jets and QCD multijet background jets, which con-
stitute the dominant background process in the analysis. The signal samples used for training
are presented in Table 1 and span a wide range of mediator masses and lifetimes, ensuring
sensitivity across different displacement regimes and kinematic configurations. Background
jets are selected to match the kinematic properties of the signal jets, in particular in transverse
momentum and pseudorapidity, to avoid biases in the classifier response.
Compared to alternative architectures, such as PARTICLENET [33], the interaction network is
chosen for its flexibility in explicitly modeling relational information between particles and
incorporating heterogeneous inputs, such as secondary vertices. This is especially relevant
for the present analysis, where displaced topologies introduce nontrivial correlations between
constituents that are not optimally captured by architectures relying primarily on local neigh-
borhood structures.
The hyperparameters of the network architecture and training procedure were optimized us-
ing the expected signal-to-background discrimination performance on statistically indepen-
dent validation samples. The training performance was validated by comparing the classifier
response in independent simulated samples and in background-enriched CR in data.
Overall, the GNN tagger exploits both kinematic and topological information of jet constituents
to enhance the discrimination between signal-like jets, characterized by multiprong and poten-
tially displaced substructure, and background jets arising from SM processes.

the candidate AK8 jet. This jet is further required to have an ungroomed PUPPI AK8 jet mass
exceeding 40 GeV and a tagger score sGNN > 0.8. The trimmed mass requirement is only used
in some of the online trigger paths and is not used in the offline jet mass selection.

Systematic uncertainties
Systematic uncertainties arise from imperfect knowledge of detector effects, theoretical model-
ing, and corrections derived from data. These can affect both the normalization and the shape
of the discriminating observables used in the maximum likelihood fits described in Section 7.
Each source of uncertainty is modeled as a nuisance parameter: uncertainties affecting only the
rate of a specific process use log-normal constraints, while shape-dependent ones are imple-
mented via a polynomial interpolation with Gaussian prior distributions. Correlations between
years of data taking and processes are imposed, as appropriate.
The integrated luminosities for the 2016, 2017, and 2018 data-taking years have 1.2–2.5% in-
dividual uncertainties [35–37], while the overall uncertainty for the 2016–2018 period is 1.6%.

The efficiency of the GNN-based jet tagger is determined using a reweighting method based
on the Lund jet plane (LJP) [38]. This approach corrects the substructure modeling of mul-
tiprong, displaced large-radius jets by applying per-prong corrections based on the ratio of
LJP densities in data and simulation. The LJP reweighting method has previously been used
mainly for prompt multiprong jet signatures; in this analysis it is extended for the first time to
displaced large-radius jets. The displacement of the (χ2) decay can affect the reconstructed jet
substructure through changes in the PF-candidate composition, track association, secondary-
vertex reconstruction, and subjet-to-parton matching. The applicability of the correction is
therefore validated in simulated signal samples with different lifetimes, and uncertainties are
assigned to cover the residual limitations of applying the method to displaced topologies. The
resulting per-event scale factors are applied to signal samples and do not affect the shape of the

The total uncertainty in the tagger efficiency is dominated by subjet matching effects in the LJP
correction procedure. These are grouped into three categories: jets with generator-level quarks
near the geometrical AK8 jet boundary, unmatched subjets, or ambiguous subjet-quark asso-
ciations. Because of the highly boosted topology of low-mass mediator signals, conservative
uncertainties between 50% and 70% are applied. These large uncertainty values arise from the
limited ability to model highly collimated substructure, where overlapping prongs hinder re-
liable subjet matching in the LJP correction procedure; this effect is most pronounced at low
mediator masses because of the stronger Lorentz boost.

independent uncertainty, allowing the TF for that bin to fluctuate within its statistical uncer-
tainty. In addition to these per-bin statistical components, experimental uncertainties related
to the JES and JER are propagated to the TFs. These variations are treated as fully correlated
across all bins, as they arise from global detector effects.

A binned maximum likelihood fit to the pmiss
T
distribution in the SR is performed to test for
the presence of a signal. The results are interpreted using the modified frequentist CLs [39, 40]
criterion with the profile likelihood ratio as the test statistic, and upper limits at 95% confidence
level (CL) are set on the signal strength µ = σ/σtheory using the asymptotic approximation [41],
as implemented in the CMS statistical analysis tool COMBINE [42].
Separate distributions for each year of data taking are fitted simultaneously to obtain the final
results, taking into account possible correlations between the systematic uncertainties, as de-
scribed in Section 6. The QCD multijet, W + jets, and Z + jets contributions are estimated from
dedicated CRs described in detail in Section 5. All other backgrounds are estimated directly
from simulation. No significant excess over the SM expectation is observed.
Figure 3 shows the distributions of pmiss
T
in the SR and CRs after a background-only fit to the
data and combining all data-taking periods for illustration. The prefit signal distributions for
the different mass and coupling hypotheses are also shown in the SR to illustrate the expected
signal shapes, given that no significant excess is observed. Upper limits at 95% CL on gχ2χ1Y0
and the mass of the Y1 mediator particle are derived for the signal parameter configurations
given in Table 1, and are shown in Fig. 4 (left and right, respectively). The observed and ex-
pected upper limits agree within two standard deviations. The good postfit agreement ob-
served in the CRs is expected because the dominant QCD, W + jets, and Z + jets backgrounds
are constrained directly from data in the simultaneous fit through dedicated CRs, with their
normalizations and transfer factors determined in situ. The observed limits in Fig. 4 are ob-
tained from the full profile likelihood fit, including the SR and CRs, all bins and data-taking
years, correlations, and systematic uncertainties. Consequently, small residual differences be-
tween the data and the postfit background prediction in the SR, even when below one standard
deviation per bin, can shift the observed limit with respect to the median expected limit if they
occur coherently in bins with high signal sensitivity. These results constrain the parameter
space of the model, excluding at 95% CL the signal hypotheses corresponding to the tested
values of the mediator mass and coupling strength shown in Fig. 4.
