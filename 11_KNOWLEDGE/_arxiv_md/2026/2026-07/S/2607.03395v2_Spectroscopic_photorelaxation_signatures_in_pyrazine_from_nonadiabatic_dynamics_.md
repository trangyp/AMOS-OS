Abstract
Despite extensive theoretical and experimental efforts, the mechanisms underly-
ing the ultrafast relaxation of pyrazine after photoexcitation remain challenging
to disentangle. Recently, theoretical investigations have been converging towards
a three-state mechanism, with an ultrafast decay of the bright 1B2u state followed
by beats in the populations of the low-lying 1B3u and 1Au states. However, a clear
agreement between the experimental results and the corresponding theoretical
predictions remains elusive. Here, we present a high-level simulation of the ultra-
fast excited states dynamics of pyrazine using coupled cluster theory with single
and double excitations and ab initio multiple spawning, together with predictions
of the time-resolved photoelectron spectrum and X-ray absorption spectra at the
nitrogen and carbon edges. This is made possible by using a newly developed
multistate implementation of similarity constrained coupled cluster theory. We
find quantitative agreement with the experimental signature of the 1B2u decay in
the photoelectron spectrum, and qualitative agreement with the available exper-
imental X-ray absorption spectra. Moreover, we detail spectroscopic signatures

1 Introduction
The ultrafast internal conversion in pyrazine has long attracted the interest of experi-
mental and theoretical chemists, becoming one of the prototypical benchmark systems
for simulations and experimental investigations of nonadiabatic processes. Neverthe-
less, the decay of gas-phase pyrazine following photoexcitation to its bright 1B2u state
(ππ∗) has been extensively debated. Here, the Franck-Condon region is characterized
by strong vibronic coupling between different electronic states, making experimental
investigations difficult to interpret and theoretical studies highly dependent on the
approximations introduced for the applied methods.
A large body of work has therefore been devoted to simulating the photophysical
decay mechanisms of the molecule1–24. Due to the high dimensionality of the system,
full quantum dynamics simulations including all 24 internal degrees of freedom have
been difficult to carry out until recently25,26, and the mechanisms proposed, in terms
of the states and the normal modes involved in the relaxation, were found to depend
on the vibrational degrees of freedom included in the simulations5–7. While this was
partly overcome with the development of reliable low-dimensionality models1,2, as
well as the advent of on-the-fly dynamics methods, simulations relying on different
treatments of the electronic structure and of the nuclear dynamics still produced
inconsistent predictions1–3. Over the past decade, the consensus on the mechanisms
has been shifting from the initial two-state picture, with a fast 1B2u →1B3u decay,
to a three-state mechanism involving the dark 1Au state as well. Lately, theoretical
simulations have predicted the presence of beats in the populations of the 1B3u and
1Au states with a period varying between 30 and 60 fs1,2,4,11–13,18,20,21, and revivals
of the 1B2u state after its ultrafast (< 40 fs) decay have also been reported, with a
major revival predicted after around 80 fs by several simulations1,4,11–13,18,20,21.
At the same time, simulations of spectroscopic signatures related to this process
have been proposed12–18,27, and their sensitivity to the applied level of theory recog-
nized13,14,18,22. Ultimately, signatures of the beats in the 1B3u and 1Au populations
were predicted for the X-ray absorption spectrum (XAS) at the nitrogen-edge (N-
edge), while the
1B2u decay was found to be more visible for the XAS at the
carbon-edge (C-edge)14,18. Recent simulations of the photoelectron spectra (PES),
instead, have predicted strongly overlapping 1B3u and 1Au signals13, while a clear
signal associated with the 1B2u state allowed for studies of its decay23.
Experimentally, the investigation of this photorelaxation, in which both the 1B2u decay
and the transfers between 1B3u and 1Au have been predicted to occur on the order of a
few tens of femtoseconds, is limited by the extremely high time resolution required to
track the molecular dynamics. However, photoelectron spectroscopy and photoelectron

imaging have provided some evidence of oscillations in the signals associated with the
1B3u and 1Au states, capturing quantum beats on the order of 60 fs and in some
cases hinting at 30 fs beats, and have also provided estimates of the 1B2u decay time
below 30 fs28–31. Time-resolved X-ray absorption spectra have also been recorded,
with evidence of the involvement of the 1Au state at the C-edge21,32, and evidence of
the beats in the 1B3u and 1Au signals at the N-edge21. Overall, the current state of
the evidence gives a partial but incomplete picture of the decay channels.
In the present study, we aim to close the chapter on the theory side by providing
high-level predictions of the excited-state molecular dynamics and of the time-resolved
photoelectron and X-ray absorption spectra. We achieve this by simulating the wave
packet dynamics in the first 200 fs after photoexcitation using ab initio multiple
spawning (AIMS)33,34 and coupled cluster theory with a complete description of sin-
gle and double excitations and including similarity-constraints to ensure correctly
described conical intersections (CCSD/SCCSD)35,36. To make the simulation possi-
ble, we have developed a multistate implementation of similarity constrained coupled
cluster theory that allows us to treat more than two electronic states in the dynam-
ics simulation at the CCSD/SCCSD level. Based on the molecular dynamics data, we
simulate time-resolved spectra and identify spectroscopic signatures that can be used
to experimentally verify the predicted photophysical channels.
2 Results and discussion
The ultrafast time evolution of pyrazine in its excited states can be followed by pho-
toelectron spectroscopy. In Fig. 1, we report the simulated photoelectron spectrum
in the region of binding energies lower than 8.0 eV, together with the experimental
results reported in Ref. 30. In both cases, we also show the integration of signals in
selected energy windows. As can be seen from Fig. 1B and C, the simulated time-
resolved spectrum shows good agreement with the experimental measurements. In
the energy region reported, two main features appear: a broad, long lasting feature
between 5-6.8 eV, and a short-lived one between 6.9-7.9 eV. In the experimental
spectrum, in the range between 7.6-8.0 eV a higher-lying feature becomes visible,
which is not modeled in our simulations. From static calculations at critical points
(see Fig. 1A), we can associate the short-lived feature in the 6.9-7.4 eV window with
the signal of the 1B2u state, while the broad, long lasting feature at lower binding
energies is given by the interplay of the 1B2u, 1B3u, and 1Au signals. A subdivision
of the simulated time-resolved spectrum based on the diabatic character of the states
involved is reported in Sec. S1 and Fig. S6 in the Supplementary Information and
confirms these assignments. To analyze the photorelaxation mechanism, in Fig. 1D

Finally, we focus on the mechanism of the photophysical channels involved in the
photorelaxation as directly predicted by our simulation of the molecular dynamics. We
start by analyzing the populations of the diabatic states over time reported in Fig. 4A.
Details about the diabatization procedure can be found in the Supporting Information.
In this figure, we can see the rapid decay of the population of the 1B2u state, with an
almost complete transfer to the other states within the first 50 fs, and no significant
revivals at later times, in contrast with other simulations1,4,11,20,21. By performing an
exponential fit of the 1B2u population, we find a decay constant of 34 fs. Both the
overall trend and the decay constant are in agreement with the 1B2u signals extracted
from our simulation of the PES spectrum in Fig. 1E and of the C-edge XAS in Fig.
3D. After the first 40-50 fs, the interplay between the 1B3u and 1Au states becomes
dominant. Here, we can see clear beats in the populations of these two states with
a period of approximately 34 fs, with a slight increase in the duration of the period
in the last simulated beats. The simulated 1Au/1B3u population beats are broadly
consistent with several previous theoretical studies, although the precise strength and
frequency of the beats vary significantly (30–60 fs) across simulations1,2,4,11,18,20. The
observed frequency of the beats (34 fs) is furthermore in agreement with the Fourier
transform of the experimental PES, which for the 1Au and 1B3u signals shows two
main frequencies at around 600 cm−1 and 1000 cm−1, corresponding to a period of 56
fs and 33 fs, respectively, where the 1000 cm−1 peak disappears after 110 fs30.

the mode with the highest coupling between the two states, as suggested by other
studies1,2 and confirmed in our simulations (see Fig. S20). By investigating the adia-

dynamics becomes prominent, the only adiabatic state significantly populated is S1,
and almost no transfer of population to S2 occurs after this time. This shows that
the beats in the 1B3u/1Au populations are a result of movement of the nuclear wave
packet across regions of the potential energy surface of S1 with different diabatic char-
acter. On this adiabatic state, we find two stationary points, namely a local minimum
with B3u character and a transition state with Au character, as shown in Fig. 4B and
C. Both stationary points preserve D2h symmetry, and the coordinate connecting the
two (QAu→B3u) is a combination of the totally symmetric modes. On the other hand,
moving from the Au transition state geometry, the energy of S1 is lowered along a
combination of the B3g normal modes, which couple the 1B3u and 1Au states (Q‡, see
Fig. S19).
While all totally symmetric modes play an important role in the dynamics, with the
displacements from the Franck-Condon geometry along ν1 and ν9a exhibiting clear
oscillations with a period of approximately 30 fs (see Fig. S21), an analysis of the
displacements along ν6a and ν8a provides some insight into the relaxation mechanism.
These modes have also been implicated in several previous studies1,12,18,21,23. In Fig.
4D and E, we report the average displacement of the nuclear wavepacket from the
Franck-Condon geometry along these modes. Here, a pronounced movement along ν6a
can be observed in the first 40 fs of the dynamics, probably related to the fast 1B2u
decay. After this time, the displacement along this mode stabilizes on average around
negative values, with slow and less definite beats at around 90 fs, 125 fs and 170 fs. In
this second part of the dynamics, on the other hand, the average displacement along
ν8a seems to directly correspond to the 1Au diabatic population, with the maxima of
the average displacement matching the maxima of the 1Au population (reported in
Fig. 4 as green vertical lines), and the slower pattern at 140-170 fs reproducing the
trend visible in Fig. 4A. This, together with the change in diabatic character of the
S1 adiabatic state when moving along ν8a from the Franck-Condon geometry (see Fig.
4F), suggests that the mode plays a fundamental role in the interplay between the 1B3u
and 1Au states. Finally, from Fig. 4D and E and S23 we can see that the maxima of the
1B3u and 1Au populations coincide with a reduced distance of the nuclear wave packet
from the determined stationary points with B3u and Au character. This suggests a
mechanism where the nuclear wave packet oscillates on the S1 potential energy surface
between regions in the proximity of the B3u minimum and of the Au transition state
and along the QAu→B3u and Q‡ modes (see Fig. S24), causing a change in the diabatic
character and a transfer of the population between the diabatic states.

3 Conclusion
The photorelaxation of pyrazine remains challenging to both simulate and investigate
experimentally. Here, we have provided a high-level simulation of the first 200 fs of
the wave packet dynamics, using ab initio multiple spawning and coupled cluster the-
ory with a full description of single and double excitations. This was made possible
by the development of a multistate implementation of similarity constrained coupled
cluster theory, which allowed us to describe more than two electronic states at the
CCSD/SCCSD level in the dynamics simulation. In this paper, we simulated the pho-
toinduced relaxation and explicitly computed the experimental TR-PES and N/C-edge
XAS signals. These signals were directly compared to the experimentally measured
observables and the resulting agreement validates our dynamics simulations. In par-
ticular, our simulation of the photoelectron spectrum agrees well with the available
experimental measurements29–31. Moreover, it confirms the absence of a revival in the
1B2u population observed in the experimental data30. In our simulation of the X-ray
absorption spectra, we also find qualitative agreement with the experimental N-edge
and C-edge XAS spectra21,32, consistent with previous predictions of the characteris-
tic 1B3u/1Au signatures and 1B2u decay14,18. Nevertheless, the detection of quantum
1B3u/1Au beats predicted on the order of 30 fs remains difficult to identify from cur-
rent experimental data given the extremely high requirements on energy and time
resolution. However, as the present work demonstrates, high-level theory predicts clear
signatures of the photorelaxation in pyrazine that could become visible with higher
experimental resolution.
4 Methods
The nonadiabatic dynamics simulation was performed using CCSD/SCCSD35,36 with
the cc-pVDZ basis set to describe the electronic structure and ab initio multiple
spawning (AIMS)33,34,37 for the time evolution of the nuclear wavepacket. Electronic
structure calculations were performed using a development version of the eT pro-
gram38, while the AIMS dynamics was performed using FMS90. The interface of
the two programs extends the existing one39 by employing CCSD for regions where
the potential energy surfaces are well separated in energy, and SCCSD with the
natural projection36 in regions of quasi-degeneracy (see the Supplementary Informa-
tion for a detailed description of the implementation). The threshold to determine
quasi-degenerate states was set to 0.01 eV.
Twenty initial conditions were sampled from a 0 K Wigner distribution (in the
harmonic approximation), based on the S0 minimum and corresponding harmonic fre-
quencies computed with CCSD/cc-pVDZ. These initial conditions were propagated
within the independent first generation approximation starting from S2 and S3. Only
samples with vertical excitation energies in the energy window 4.56-5.13 eV were
propagated. All results were weighted according to the oscillator strength of every ini-
tial condition to account for the brightness of the adiabatic state. Further details of
this procedure can be found in Sec. S8 in the Supplementary Information. Coupling
11


## Page 12


elements were evaluated with the derivative operator acting on the right vector and
without normalization36,40,41. Couplings where the derivative operator acts on the bra
vector were enforced to be equal to the negative of the couplings with the derivative
acting on the ket. The spawning criterion was the norm of the coupling vector and
the threshold was set to 20 a.u.. The propagation time step was 20 a.u. (≈5 fs) and
reduced to 5 a.u. (≈0.5 fs) in the coupling region. The dynamics was simulated for
8000 a.u. (≈200 fs). During the simulation, a total of 445 trajectory basis functions
(TBFs) were produced.
All simulated spectra are based on the incoherent approximation, where the spectra
are calculated at the center of each TBF. Only TBFs with an amplitude > 0.01 were
included in the simulated spectra.
Photoelectron spectra were simulated at the CCSD/cc-pVDZ level, using a develop-
ment version of the eT program. Dyson intensities were implemented based on the
implementation in Ref. 42. Further details on the PES spectra can be found in the
Supplementary Information.
In the X-ray absorption spectra, core and valence excited states were described at
the CC3/cc-pVDZ and CCSD/cc-pVDZ levels, respectively. This allowed an accurate
description of the core states while avoiding ambiguities in the assignment of the
valence states used in the dynamics. Further details on this procedure can be found
in Ref. 39. All calculations used a development version of the eT program. Further
details on the XAS spectra can be found in the Supporting Information.

energies) are calculated using the existing implementation for ionized states in eT ,1
and the peak intensities are obtained as Dyson intensities. These intensities were imple-
mented at the EOM-CCSD level in a development version of eT . The implementation
generalizes the ground state implementation in Ref. 2.

approximately constant shift for all the peaks towards higher binding energies.

S2 Additional details on the XAS spectra
All X-ray absorption spectra have been calculated using a development version of the
eT program1. In order to be consistent with the level of theory used in the dynam-
ics, the valence states were described using CCSD/cc-pVDZ. Core excited states
were determined with CC3/cc-pVDZ using the core-valence separation.5 Differences
in the ground state energies at the two different levels of theory were accounted for
as described in Ref. 6. To simulate each spectra, excitation energies and oscillator
strengths were determined within the incoherent approximation for every TBF with a
weight larger than 0.01 at time steps of 200 au (≈4.84 fs). For TBFs that were killed
during the simulation due their small amplitude, the last available geometry was used.
Four geometries in the N-edge and five in the C-edge were excluded from the spec-
tra because of convergence problems of the higher lying CC3 core excited states. To
simulate different experimental time and energy resolutions, a two-dimensional Gaus-
sian broadening was applied to all spectra with different full-widths at half maximum
(FWHM) in the time and energy directions.
S2.1 N-edge
At the N-edge, we focus on the region of the spectrum below 397 eV, where the core
excited states involved have single excitation character and are thus well-described at
the CC3 level. For this region of the spectrum, in order to simulate different exper-
imental time and energy resolutions, we report in Figs. S9, S10, S11, we report the
time-resolved spectrum at the N-edge with FWHM of the two-dimensional broaden-
ing set to 5 fs, 0.1 eV; 20 fs, 0.3 eV; 30 fs, 0.4 eV, respectively. In Fig. S12, we report
the spectrum subdivided in contributions from the 1B2u, 1B3u and 1Au states in blue,
red and green, respectively, using the RGB scheme to highlight the overlap of the dif-
ferent features.
The upper part of the spectrum is characterized by double-dominated peaks, for which
an accurate description would require the inclusion of higher order excitations. In Fig.
S13 , we report a simulation of the N-edge spectrum where the doubles dominated
peaks have been shifted by -1.31 eV to reproduce the average shift of the double-
dominated peaks when calculated with CCSDT instead of CC3. This reference value
was found as the average of the energy difference of the different double-dominated
core excited states at the CCSDT/cc-pVDZ and CC3/cc-pVDZ levels. As can be
seen from the figure, and comparing with the experimental results reported in Ref.
7, the excitation energies of these spectroscopic signatures are still overestimated at
the CCSDT level, which suggests that a further improvement of the level of theory,
including perhaps quadruple excitations, is necessary for an accurate description of
that region of the spectrum.

S5 Adiabatic populations
In Fig. S25, we show the populations of S1, S2 and S3 over time. Since in the vicinity
of the Franck-Condon region both S2 and S3 are bright, at time t = 0 both states
are populated. However, both states quickly decay and transfer their population to
S1 in the first 50 fs, with only about 12% of the population in S2 at the end of the
simulation. Since S1 is the only state with a considerable population after 50 fs, we
can conclude that the beats in the 1B3u and 1Au populations are a consequence of
the change in diabatic character of S1, and non-adiabatic transitions to S2 are not
significantly involved in this mechanism. Finally, the residual population in S3 at the
end of the propagation is likely related to the threshold of a minimum population of
5% for a TBF to spawn used in the simulation.

from the first row of F . To ensure an orthogonal transformation from adiabats to
diabats, we orthonormalize this vector against the 1B2u coefficient vector. Finally, to
obtain the 1Au coefficients, we calculate the cross product between the 1B2u and 1B3u
vectors. The matrix containing these three vectors defines the orthogonal adiabatic-
to-diabatic transformation, and the square of each element provides the contribution
of each diabatic state to the adiabatic one (and vice versa).

S8 Selection of the initial conditions and other
details about the dynamics
The initial conditions were sampled from a harmonic Wigner distribution at 0 K based
on the ground state equilibrium geometry determined at the CCSD/cc-pVDZ level.
The ground state geometry, together with the harmonic frequencies, are reported in
Table S1, S2. The Wigner sampling was performed using a development version of
the eT program1, which calculates the numerical Hessian using central differences
(dx=10−4 Bohr) of the CCSD analytical gradients calculated at displaced geome-
tries from the equilibrium geometry. A Wigner sample of 100 initial geometries and
momenta was generated and used to simulate the UV-Vis absorption spectrum (see
Fig. S26). Of these, 20 initial geometries and momenta were used in the nonadiabatic
dynamics simulation. In order to simulate the dynamics following excitation within
the excitation window 4.56 −5.13 eV, corresponding to the excitation to the 1B2u
state, and since at the Franck-Condon geometry S2 and S3 have excitation energies
E2 = 5.203 eV and E3 = 5.206 eV (CCSD/cc-pVDZ), a simulation was started (within
the independent first generation approximation) for every pair of initial geometry and
momenta y from either S2, S3, or both, depending on whether the excitation energy
of each state falls within the excitation window.
The results were weighted within
the incoherent approximation by the oscillator strength of the transition to the ini-
tial state of each initial condition, normalized by the sum of the weights of all initial
conditions included in the simulation. The spawning threshold was set to 20 a.u. and
the spawning criterion was the norm of the coupling vector, the minimum popula-
tion to spawn was set to 0.05. The default propagation time step was set to 20 a.u.
(≈0.5 fs), and reduced to 5 a.u. in the coupling regions. The dynamics was simulated
for 8000 a.u. (≈200 fs). During the simulation, a total of 445 TBFs were produced.
The continuity of the coupling elements was ensured by analyzing the overlap matrix
Sij = ⟨Li|Rj⟩(see also Sec. S10). In some cases, TBFs or centroids encountered a
region where higher lying states had complex energies . In these cases, since the states
encountering the numerical artifacts are not coupled to the adiabatic state of the TBF
(or centroid), we have not enforced further similarity constraints.

S10 Adaptive interface
The nonadiabatic dynamics was simulated using the FMS90 program for the nuclear
dynamics and the eT program for the electronic structure. Building on the existing
FMS90-eT interface,6 which can perform nonadiabatic dynamics simulations with an
arbitrary number of CCSD states but only on two SCCSD states, we have developed
an adaptive interface that can handle an arbitrary number of SCCSD states.
The SCCSD method avoids the well-known numerical artifacts of coupled cluster the-
ory at conical intersections between excited states by constraining the two intersecting
states to be orthogonal, and thus, to have real energies.10,11 Then, in order to include
in the dynamics more than two states, and within the assumption that three-state
intersections are not going to be encountered, the two states to be constrained are
selected adaptively. This is possible because the energy correction that SCCSD gives
compared to CCSD is small and typically becomes negligible away from intersection
regions.11,12 Consequently, a threshold on the energy gap between two states can be
selected such that, whenever two states are closer in energy than the threshold, they
are constrained to be orthogonal, and their energies, nuclear gradients and nonadia-
batic couplings are calculated using SCCSD. In all other regions, where the states are
well separated in energy, their energies, gradients, and nonadiabatic couplings are cal-
culated using CCSD. Although this adaptive CCSD/SCCSD method implies a sudden
change when the states become constrained, the CCSD and SCCSD energies are so
close in practice that the effect on energy conservation is negligible. We discuss this
aspect in more detail in Section S11.
During the time propagation, the dynamics program requests electronic structure
information at the center position of trajectory basis functions and at centroids
between pairs of basis functions. For a given geometry, and given an energy threshold
τ to detect quasi-degenerate states, the interface performs the following sequence of
operations:
1. Determine CCSD energies and evaluate ∆Eij = Ei −Ej for states i, j
2. If all ∆Eij > τ, evaluate energies/gradients/couplings at the CCSD level of theory
3. If any ∆Eij < τ, constrain i and j and evaluate energies/gradients/couplings at
the SCCSD level of theory
Note that if 3. is true, then the program may request energies, gradients, and couplings
that involve constrained or unconstrained states. Consequently, the evaluation is either
of type unconstrained-to-unconstrained, constrained-to-constrained, or unconstrained-
to-constrained, depending on i and j and the states the dynamics program is requesting
the electronic structure information about. This requires careful changes to the SCCSD
analytical nuclear derivatives and is discussed in Section S12.
Other aspects of the interface, in particular, the orbital diabatization and the main-
taining of continuous coupling elements, remain largely unchanged. We refer to the
original interface for details.6 Two minor modifications have been added to improve
the stability of the interface. First, the continuity of the equation of motion states

is now maintained by analyzing the left-right overlap matrix Sij = ⟨Li|Rj⟩, which
strictly ensures Sij →δij for ∆t →0 by biorthonormality (unlike the right-right
overlap matrix ⟨Ri|Rj⟩). Second, the solution algorithm for the SCCSD equations
now includes an improved displacement approach to converge the equations. In this
approach, if the direct inversion (DIIS) algorithm11 fails to converge, which sometimes
occurs close to intersections, the program will take several small steps away from the
intersection region (by moving an atom) until convergence becomes possible. Then it
will take steps back towards the original geometry, using restart at each step to ensure
convergence. Once it has returned to the initial geometry, the program evaluates the
required energies and nuclear derivatives and returns the information to the dynamics
program.
S11 Energy conservation
As we are using an adaptive algorithm where a similarity constraint between two
states i, j is activated when ∆Eij drops below some value τ, we must carefully consider
potential artifacts in the dynamics caused by the instantaneous change from CCSD
to SCCSD. We have therefore made some additional checks in addition to the default
checks that are performed by the FMS program (where per step deviation exceeding
0.006 Hartree or cumulative drift exceeding 0.02 Hartree cause the program to declare
numerical failure).
For each TBF, we compute the maximum deviation of the classical energy with the
initial energy to investigate possible energy drifts caused by the change from CCSD
to SCCSD in the adaptive algorithm. The observed deviations are given in Figure S35
and show that drifts generally remain small over the course of the simulation.
Issues with numerical integration can also occur when we exchange CCSD energies and
coupling elements with their SCCSD corrections. Considering the maximum deviation
of the wave packet norm from 1.0 for all the initial conditions, we find that no deviation
exceeds 10−3, indicating no obvious issues with numerical integration.
The lack of observable artifacts is a reflection of the small magnitude of the SCCSD
correction for pyrazine. An example comparing CCSD, SCCSD, and adaptive CCS-
D/SCCSD, for one of the initial conditions (IC 3) in the simulation, is shown in Figures
S35. This examples illustrates the similar dynamics predicted by CCSD, SCCSD, and
adaptive SCCSD/CCSD, the small magnitude of the SCCSD energy correction, as well
as the small extent of the numerical artifact that causes the CCSD dynamics to ter-
minate at around 7.7 fs. We should note that the magnitude of the SCCSD correction
is generally system-dependent and may be different in other systems.
