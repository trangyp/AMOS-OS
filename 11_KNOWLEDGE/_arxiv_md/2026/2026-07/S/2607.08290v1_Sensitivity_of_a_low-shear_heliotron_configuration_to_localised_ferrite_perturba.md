Abstract.
The influence of ferritic steel on low-shear stellarator/heliotron magnetic
configurations is investigated for the Heliotron J device using a point dipole
magnetisation model. By numerically evaluating ferritic steel plates assumed at several
locations inside the Heliotron J vacuum vessel, the changes in the rotational transform
and magnetic island width are shown to be sensitive to the installation location. This
sensitivity arises from the coupling between the background nonaxisymmetric field
and ferrite perturbation, rather than being determined solely by the perturbation
amplitude. Ferritic steel plates placed on the outer side of a straight section produce the
most significant changes in the magnetic topology and exhibit the highest sensitivity
to violations of the M = 4 toroidal periodicity. Additionally, we show that appropriate
arrangements of passive magnetic dipoles can reduce the effective helical ripple while
preserving the vacuum magnetic well depth in Heliotron J, and can induce stellarator-
asymmetric boundary perturbation in low-field experiments near the ferrite saturation
threshold.

1. Introduction
Structural materials for in-vessel components must satisfy the demanding engineering
and nuclear requirements of future fusion reactors. In addition to resistance to neutron
irradiation, high-temperature strength, and weldability, they must exhibit low-activation
characteristics [1,2] to facilitate reactor maintenance and radioactive waste management.
Reduced-activation ferritic/martensitic (RAFM) steels [3, 4], such as F82H [5] and
EUROFER-97 [6], are among the leading candidate materials for blanket systems and
other in-vessel components [7,8].
For the practical application of RAFM steels, it is essential to quantify the influence
of ferritic magnetisation on plasma confinement.
In present experimental devices,
magnetic materials are primarily treated as sources of error fields. However, tokamak
experiments have suggested that magnetic materials can provide an additional degree
of freedom for magnetic-field control [9–12]. In JFT-2M [13, 14] and JT-60U [15], for
example, ferritic steel tiles were installed to reduce the toroidal field ripple, which was
shown experimentally and numerically to affect energetic-particle confinement [15, 16]
and plasma rotation [17, 18].
These studies also demonstrated that normal plasma
operation is possible with ferritic steel, provided that appropriate wall conditioning
procedures are applied [19].
The use of RAFM steels is also anticipated in stellarator/heliotron reactors [20–22].
Because stellarators generate rotational transform using external coils and possess
intrinsically three-dimensional magnetic field structures, the influence of magnetic
materials is nontrivial. Previous studies have indicated that magnetic perturbations
from ferritic materials can lead to the formation of sizable magnetic islands [20].
From an engineering perspective, the limited space and complex geometry inside the
vacuum vessel require consideration of practical installation constraints in its design.
These considerations motivate the present investigation of ferrite effects in an operating
stellarator/heliotron device, providing physical insight relevant to future reactor design.
In this study, the Heliotron J device [23,24] is used to investigate the influence of
magnetic materials on stellarator/heliotron configurations. Heliotron J is a helical-axis
heliotron device operating at Kyoto University, which is characterised by low magnetic
shear and a vacuum magnetic well.
These characteristics are shared with modular
quasi-isodynamic stellarators, including W7-X [25]. We numerically evaluate the effects
of ferritic perturbations using a point dipole magnetisation model, focusing on two
complementary aspects: first, as a source of unintended error fields that can break
magnetic surfaces, and second, as a beneficial passive element for tailoring the magnetic
configuration.
In tokamaks, error fields are known to lock magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) modes to
a specific phase and can ultimately be a cause of plasma disruptions [26]. In stellarators,
the impact on confinement is more direct, as these perturbations can break magnetic
surfaces of the vacuum equilibrium configuration. The requirements for coil installation
accuracy during the design stage [27–29] and the precision of the actual configuration

the installation location than on the magnetic perturbation amplitude.
Another aspect of ferritic materials is their potential for passive configuration
control. In particular, in present-day experiments operated at relatively low magnetic
fields (e.g., around 0.4 T), the magnetisation of ferritic steel, such as F82H, is close
to saturation, whereas the field produced by ferrite can become comparable to the
equilibrium field. Under such specific conditions, magnetic materials can provide an
additional degree of freedom for tailoring three-dimensional magnetic configurations.
In light of recent studies that explore localised magnetic elements such as permanent
magnets [38–40], non-plasma-surrounding coils [41,42], and passive superconductors [43]
for constructing and improving stellarator configurations [44–46], we also examine the
possibility of improving the effective helical ripple and deforming the plasma boundary
shape in Heliotron J using ferritic materials.

Section 2 introduces the

2. Simulation methods
2.1. Heliotron J configuration
Heliotron J [23, 24] is a heliotron device with a helical magnetic axis, major radius
of R0 = 1.2 m, and average minor radius of a = 0.13 m. The coil system consists
of a continuous helical field coil with poloidal and toroidal period numbers of L = 1
and M = 4, respectively, complemented by three pairs of vertical field coils and 16
toroidal field coils of two types (TA and TB coils). While the magnetic field strength on
the magnetic axis is typically operated at B0 = 1.25 T for electron cyclotron heating,
operation at lower field strengths is also possible. In this study, we consider magnetic
configurations with B0 down to 0.42 T to examine regimes where the ferritic magnetic
field constitutes a larger fraction of the equilibrium magnetic field.
Heliotron J was designed [23] with a particular emphasis on the formation of
a vacuum magnetic well through the selection of the helical coil modulation.
As a
consequence, toroidicity on the order of the geometrical inverse aspect ratio remains,

Heliotron J shares several key characteristics with modern quasi-isodynamic stellarators

The TA and TB coils generate a toroidal mirror field with

the “standard configuration,” while the TA:TB = 5:1 configuration enhances the toroidal
mirror component and modifies the trapped-particle confinement properties [49]. Unless
otherwise noted, the analyses in section 3 focus on the standard configuration. The
TA:TB = 5:1 (“high-bumpiness”) configuration will be considered in section 4.
In this study, the vacuum magnetic field generated by the coil system of Heliotron J
was calculated using KMAG [50]. In KMAG, coils are discretised based on a finite-cross-
section model [51], and the magnetic field is calculated by numerical integration based on
the Biot-Savart law. The KMAG code searches for the magnetic axis and the last closed
magnetic surface while evaluating the rotational transform profile and Poincar´e plots.
The vacuum field calculated by KMAG is used as the external magnetic field, Bext, and
the magnetic response to the ferritic steel installation is analysed by superposing the
ferritic field onto this external field. For the analyses presented in section 4, the vacuum
magnetic field calculated by KMAG is further transformed into Boozer coordinates using
VMEC [52] and BOOZXFORM [53].

3.2. Spatial structure of localised ferrite perturbation
Figure 3 illustrates the magnetic field generated by the ferritic plate installed on the
inner side of the straight section at B0 = 1.25 T. Here the ferritic field components
are displayed as contour plots projected onto the three-dimensional surface representing
the plasma boundary, which is determined for the unperturbed vacuum magnetic field
configuration in the absence of ferritic steel plates.
Although the ferritic plate is
modelled in KFERRITE as an assembly of numerous saturated dipoles, the resulting
magnetic field exhibits the characteristic structure of a macroscopic dipole field whose
scale size is as large as the ferritic plate. Opposite magnetic poles are formed near the
two ends of the plate, and the dipole axis is slightly tilted from the geometrical toroidal
direction, reflecting the local pitch of the helical magnetic field.
Panels (a)–(c) show contour plots of the ferritic magnetic field projected onto the
surface-normal, binormal (geodesic), and field-aligned (parallel) directions on the plasma
surface, respectively. While the amplitudes of all three components remain of the same
order, the radial (normal) component is found to be the strongest (δB ≃0.05 T).
The radial perturbation Bferrite · ∇s appears as a pair of positive and negative lobes
associated with the two magnetic poles of the ferritic plate, a structure expected to
produce a localised wavy distortion as field lines pass over the plate. A similar positive-
negative pair structure is observed in the binormal component. This symmetry suggests
that these components produce only localised distortions associated with a magnetic
dipole.
The parallel component exhibits a different behaviour in figure 3(c). Over most of
the area facing the ferritic plate, the ferritic magnetic field is directed opposite to the
local equilibrium magnetic field, indicating that the ferritic plate weakens the parallel
magnetic field. Although regions where the ferritic field strengthens the equilibrium
field exist near the magnetic poles, their peak amplitude is about half of that in the
antiparallel region near the center of the plate (δB ∼0.03 T). The reduction in magnetic
field strength in front of the ferritic plates is interpreted as the magnetic material
naturally drawing the surrounding magnetic flux within the magnetic circuit. As shown
below, such perturbations result in a displacement of the magnetic surfaces toward the
ferritic plates.

3.4. Vacuum magnetic islands induced by ferritic steel perturbations
Figures 6 and 7 show the Poincar´e plots and rotational-transform profiles for
configurations in which a ferritic plate is installed at only one toroidal location at
B0 = 1.25 and 0.42 T, respectively. Because the plate is not repeated with the intrinsic
M = 4 periodicity of Heliotron J, the ferritic perturbation introduces a broad spectrum
of toroidal Fourier components. Among these, the n = 1 component explicitly breaks
toroidal periodicity. The coupling between this n = 1 perturbation and the M = 4
equilibrium field further generates sideband components, including n = 5, which can
resonate with the n/m = 5/9 rational surface.
A strong dependence on the plate location is again observed.
Although the
rotational-transform profile intersects the n/m = 5/9 resonance in all cases, a large
magnetic island is formed only when the plate is located on the outer side of the straight
section (S-O), as seen in figure 6(d). In the case of B0 = 0.42 T, the outer magnetic
surfaces are broken and the effective confinement volume even shrinks [figure 7(d)] To
quantify the resonant drive, the Fourier components of Bferrite ·∇s/Bext were evaluated.
Remarkably, the amplitudes of the n = 1 and n = 5 components are of the order of 10−3
and show no strong dependence on the plate location. This indicates that the island
response cannot be characterised solely by the magnitude of the resonant perturbation,
but is instead governed by its spatial coupling to the equilibrium magnetic field geometry.
The correlation between the island width and ∆ι suggests that the coupling between
the equilibrium magnetic field and the ferritic perturbation plays an important role.
Numerical sensitivity tests further reveal that the magnetic island width scales with
the square root of the ferritic plate thickness.
A substantial magnetic island width
persists at the S-O location, even when the thickness is reduced to as little as 2 mm.
Consequently, because the island width decreases more gradually than the perturbation
amplitude, even a minimal ferritic thickness is sufficient to have a sizable island width.
As expected, these results have emphasised that preserving the intrinsic toroidal
periodicity is crucial for avoiding large vacuum islands in the presence of ferritic
materials.
In practice, however, perfect symmetry is difficult to achieve because of
installation errors, port access, and engineering constraints. To assess the sensitivity
to such asymmetries, Figs. 8 and 9 show the effect of vertically displacing one of the
four periodic plates by 1.6 cm at B0 = 1.25 and 0.42 T, respectively. Consistent with
the single-plate results, the configuration is most vulnerable at the S-O location. While
the other three locations remain robust even for this centimetre-scale displacement,
distortions of the peripheral magnetic surfaces are observed at the S-O location for
displacements as small as 4 mm. In particular, when installed at the corner section, no
large magnetic islands are observed, even with a weak magnetic field of 0.42 T and the
presence of misalignment. These results point out that installation tolerances should be
evaluated individually based on the local magnetic sensitivity of each location, rather
than applying a uniform standard.

4. Application: passive magnetic-configuration modification using ferritic
steel plates
4.1. Passive field modification
The magnetic field generated by ferritic steel is intrinsically limited by the saturation
magnetisation of the material (Ms ≈2 T). Consequently, in reactor-scale devices where
the equilibrium magnetic field is much stronger, ferritic perturbations are expected
to remain relatively small. However, the installation of ferritic steel plates offers an
opportunity to provide a means of configuration control for present-day experimental
devices, especially when operated at relatively low magnetic fields such that the field
produced by the ferrite can become comparable to the equilibrium magnetic field.
Under such conditions, ferritic steel plates can be utilised as passive elements to
smooth out local field non-uniformities, analogously to the reduction of the toroidal field
ripple in tokamak devices. As discussed above, ferritic materials naturally draw the
surrounding magnetic flux toward themselves, also enabling the localised modification
of the plasma boundary shape without the need for additional power supplies or
complex coil systems.
Such passive tailoring provides an interesting experimental
method for investigating the effect of localised magnetic elements on three-dimensional
configurations. Below we show two numerical examples for applications in Heliotron J.

4.3. Boundary shape modification using magnetic dipoles
Figure 12 shows the plasma boundary shape of the magnetic configuration corresponding
to the ferrite insert case of figure 10. It is seen that ferritic steel plates modify not only
the magnetic-field-strength distribution and rotational transform but also the geometry
of magnetic surfaces in this weak equilibrium field regime. Figure 12 illustrates that the
outer magnetic surfaces are displaced toward the ferritic steel plates. This behaviour
is consistent with the ferritic material drawing the surrounding magnetic flux toward
itself.
An intriguing extension of this concept is to produce magnetic configurations that
break the stellarator symmetry [60].
By arranging ferritic elements asymmetrically,
localised perturbations can be introduced while keeping the external coil system
itself perfectly symmetric.
Such configurations offer a unique way to investigate
the consequences of symmetry breaking in stellarator/heliotron plasmas, similar to
research in tokamaks where up-down asymmetry is known to influence intrinsic rotation
and momentum transport [61, 62]. Figure 13 illustrates an example in the standard
configuration of Heliotron J, where a single ferritic plate is placed asymmetrically
on the upper inboard side [top-left in figure 13] in the straight section.
The
resulting perturbation remains localised near the plate location and does not adhere
to the intrinsic stellarator symmetry of the configuration.
Such a capability in the
weak equilibrium field regime (∼0.4 T) provides a unique experimental scheme to
experimentally investigate the effect of plasma boundary shaping in existing devices.

5. Conclusion
In this study, motivated by the possible use of RAFM steel in a present experimental
device and future fusion reactors, we have numerically investigated the magnetic
response of a low-shear heliotron configuration to localised ferritic perturbations. The
KFERRITE code was developed and benchmarked against experimental data from JT-
60U.
The main conclusion is that the magnetic response in a stellarator/heliotron is
governed not only by the amplitude of the ferritic magnetic field but also by its spatial

coupling to the background magnetic field.
While asymmetry only in the poloidal
direction is expected for tokamaks [34], the sensitivity is highly non-uniform in both
the poloidal and toroidal directions in the stellarator/heliotron device. The outer side
of the straight sections (S-O) was identified as the most sensitive location in Heliotron J.
At this position, even a weak ferritic perturbation can produce significant changes in the
rotational transform and trigger the formation of (m, n) = (9, 5) magnetic island chains.
Numerical sensitivity tests have shown that the magnetic island width scales with the
square root of the ferritic plate thickness. This scaling explains that significant island
chains can still be generated even by relatively thin ferritic components when the ferritic
plates are installed at the specific locations. While some locations are robust even to
centimetre-scale displacements, the S-O location requires millimetre-scale precision to
avoid breaking the field periodicity, resulting in large vacuum magnetic islands. While
the magnetic field errors associated with the ferrite field can affect the optimum coil
configuration in future fusion reactors [22], by identifying and avoiding the placement
of ferritic materials at highly sensitive locations, one can maintain the integrity of the
magnetic field with minimal modification to the external coil system. This is also related
to exploring the stellarator coil optimization with higher engineering tolerances [58].
A more quantitative analysis of such localised sensitivity across different installation
locations, such as extending the method of [36], remains a task for future work.
Beyond its role as a source of error fields, this study has also shown that ferritic
steel can provide a passive means for magnetic configuration tailoring in present-day
low-field experiments. The ferritic material naturally draws the surrounding magnetic
flux in a magnetic circuit, providing a means of locally shaping the magnetic field.
We successfully illustrated an application where an appropriate arrangement of ferritic
plates reduced the effective helical ripple while preserving the vacuum magnetic well
depth.
The localised boundary perturbation can also be used to break stellarator
symmetry, which may be of interest for studying intrinsic rotation and momentum
transport. These results demonstrate that ferritic materials placed at different toroidal
and poloidal locations can be used as passive “knobs” to selectively tune magnetic
configurations.
