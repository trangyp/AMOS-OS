We extend the persistent homology analysis of [1] to the full Roman–Byzantine trade network
(0–1453 ce), using 2,599 nodes and 4,503 trimodal edges calibrated against the orbis Geospatial
Network Model. Five results are reported. (i) The Ht=0 western sub-network result of [1] is a data-
coverage artifact: with full western representation (Nwest = 987, β1 ≈52 cycles per decade) a base-
line East–West entropy gap of +2.22 units is present from 0 ce and grows at +3.3×10−3 yr−1, predat-
ing the Theodosian partition by four centuries. (ii) A hub-selection artifact in degree-heterogeneous
networks can reverse the sign of the inferred Phase III slope, requiring full-coverage or stratified sam-
pling for reliable structural-break detection. (iii) Decomposing Byzantine resilience into geographic
(Hgeo) and economic (Heco) components reveals a peak decoupling ratio Rd = Heco/Hgeo = 47.7 at
620 ce, falling to 13.9 at 640 ce, quantifying the McCormick–Ward-Perkins historiographical debate
as a contrast between two network layers operating on different timescales. (iv) The inter-decade W2
Wasserstein velocity identifies the Late Roman–Early Byzantine transition (495 ce) as the highest
topological-velocity event of the 1,453-year record; the cross-network Wasserstein ratio increases by
150–300× after the Chrysobull of 1082 ce, providing an independent diagram-space analogue of Rd.
Both the Western collapse (476 ce) and the Byzantine endpoint (1453 ce) occur at H∗≈0.524,
interpreted as a candidate topological percolation threshold.
I.
INTRODUCTION
The companion study to this work [1] demonstrated that the Eastern Mediterranean trade network — as represented
in the orbis Geospatial Network Model of the Roman World [2, 3] and situated within the broader economic history
of the Greco-Roman world [4] — exhibited three statistically distinct phases in its β1 persistent entropy time series
between 0 and 400 ce, with an irreversible structural break at 310 ce (Chow F = 85.4, p < 0.0001) from which
no topological recovery was observed despite Constantinian political reunification. The same study reported that
the western sub-network produced Ht = 0 throughout the study period and acknowledged this as a potential data
limitation arising from the restricted western coverage of the orbis extract employed: 49 nodes with no representation
of Italy, Gaul, Britannia, or the Rhine frontier.
The present study provides a complete western dataset, integrating 987 western nodes from the Pleiades Digital
Atlas of the Ancient World [5] with road geometry from the darmc Roman Roads Network [6], maritime routes
derived from orbis coastal and overseas edge topology, and provincial boundary polygons [7] for temporal node
activation.
The western sub-network generated β1 ≈52 independent cycles throughout 0–400 ce, and exhibited measurable
entropy throughout that period, with a Phase I mean of ¯Hwest = 1.362. The Ht = 0 finding of [1] characterised the
orbis extract, not the actual western network. The structural question becomes not whether the West had topological
resilience, but how much less it had than the East from the very beginning.
The East–West structural comparison generates three distinct questions, each independently testable with the
integrated dataset constructed here.
• The baseline East–West asymmetry question. If Hwest < Heast at 0 ce then the East–West structural
difference was not produced by the Crisis of the Third Century, the military reforms of Diocletian, or the
Theodosian division. It was a property of the network from the earliest phase of the Principate. This baseline
structural asymmetry implies that Western fragility was already embedded in the network, rather than being
imposed only by later external pressures [8, 9].
• The topology-precedes-politics question. The political division of the Empire under Theodosius is the
conventional historical marker of East–West separation. If the difference between Heast and Hwest entropy grew

Three well-known historical claims are testable through topological network analysis:
• Ward-Perkins (2005) and McCormick (2001).
Ward-Perkins [10] argues that the fifth-century West-
ern collapse was a genuine catastrophe proved by the decline of material culture indicators implying a severe
contraction of long-distance commercial exchange circuits. McCormick [11] argues, based on a comprehensive
reconstruction of communications in the early medieval Mediterranean, that the apparent collapse of Roman
commerce conceals a genuine continuity of exchange activity that adapted to a new political situation. The de-
composition of Byzantine resilience into geographic (Hgeo) and economic (Heco) components provides a metric
capable of distinguishing and quantifying differences between these two accounts: at 640 ce, Heco/Hgeo = 13.9,
meaning the commercial Mediterranean retained approximately fourteen times more cycle redundancy than
the imperial geographic infrastructure. Ward-Perkins describes the geographic layer; McCormick describes the
economic one.
• Harper (2017). Harper [12] locates the primary drivers of Roman imperial fragility in climate deterioration
and pandemic mortality. The Justinianic Plague of 541 ce produces the largest Chow F-statistic in the 0–
1453 ce geographic series (Chow F = 152.69, p < 0.0001), confirming that pandemic disruption constituted a
structural shock. However, the plague signal is absent from the economic network (Heco unchanged at 541 ce),
which is consistent with a demographic disruption rather than a permanent infrastructural disruption of the
route system, and therefore supports Harper’s demographic argument.
• Wickham (2005). Wickham [8] argues that the transition from a tax-and-redistribute imperial economy to
localised subsistence exchange is the structural transformation defining late antiquity. The baseline East–West
structural asymmetry documented here — +2.222 entropy units in Phase I — supports Wickham’s struc-
tural framing. The Western network was not a Roman network that deteriorated under external stress, but a
structurally weaker commercial system from its inception, whose fragility became decisive when the external
conditions sustaining it were progressively withdrawn. Wickham’s structural transformation was not produced
by the Crisis of the Third Century: it was the eventual expression of a structural difference that had existed
since Augustus.
Three methods described in Ref. [1] are used here: the differential friction model assigning transport-mode-specific
volatility parameters to each edge; the adaptive filtration threshold (δt = 90th percentile of pairwise distances within
the active sub-network at each decade); and the Chow structural break test [13] for phase identification. For details,
the reader is referred to [1], Section II, for the complete specification.
The present study adds six methodological contributions. Throughout the article, claims about collapse thresholds
are treated as within-system hypotheses for the Roman–Byzantine network, not as established constants of pre-modern
empires in general.
• Minimum-coverage convergence protocol. The hub-selection artifact — a non-monotonic N-convergence
in which N = 800 top-degree sampling produces lower Chow F-statistics than both N = 400 and N = 1,600,
because degree-dominant nodes from one sub-network dilute the regional signal of the other before both have
reached sufficient sampling density — is documented here (Table V). The practical consequence is a minimum-
coverage requirement: structural break detection in degree-heterogeneous historical networks requires either
N > Nmin, where Nmin is the sampling threshold above which both sub-networks reach sufficient density to
reconstruct their independent cycles, or a sampling protocol that explicitly preserves regional representation
ratios.
• Trimodal edge architecture. The orbis differential friction model is extended to include terrestrial road
edges (calibrated against orbis anchor points via log-log regression, factor 8.90×), maritime routes covering the
western and eastern Mediterranean basin, and five canal edges — producing a network in which three transport
modes respond to documented historical perturbations across the full geographic extent of the Empire.
• Geographic–economic network decomposition.

II.
DATA AND METHODS
A.
Network data: the Full Empire
The dataset includes 2,599 nodes and 4,503 directed route segments (edges) organised into a trimodal architecture
of road, maritime, and fluvial transport layers.
a.
Node sources.
Of the 2,599 nodes, 599 are drawn from the orbis Geospatial Network Model of the Roman
World [2, 3], and 2,000 from the Pleiades Digital Atlas of the Ancient World [5], filtered to settlement and fort feature
types. Spatially, 1,612 nodes lie east of longitude 20◦E (the Eastern sub-network) and 987 west of it (the Western
sub-network), compared with 49 western nodes in the orbis extract employed in [1]. Western node coverage by major
region is as follows: Hispania (443 nodes), Africa Occidentalis (141 nodes), Italia (146 nodes), Britannia (86 nodes),
and Gallia (9 nodes).
Temporal activation of nodes follows a two-criterion rule: a node is active at decade t if
• its Pleiades minDate ≤t ≤maxDate and
• its coordinates fall within the provincial polygon that is active at t. Provincial polygons are taken from the
Digital Atlas of the Roman Empire [7] at four certified snapshots (BCE 60, CE 117, CE 200, post-Diocletian),
with linear interpolation between snapshots. (including the evacuation of Dacia under Aurelian (271 ce), the
administrative partition of Britannia (197 ce), and the reconstitution of Syria under Diocletian (293 ce)).

b.
Edge architecture.
The 4,503 edges are distributed across four functional categories, each with distinct cost

• Terrestrial roads (1,104 edges): road segments from the darmc Roman Roads Network [6] (types road major,

• Maritime routes — orbis calibrated (497 edges): coastal, overseas, slow-coast, and ferry segments inherited

• Maritime routes — western basin (2,798 edges): 2,271 new coastal segments covering the western Mediterranean
from the Strait of Gibraltar to Sicily, plus 527 overseas crossing segments at 12 historically documented transit
points (Adriatic narrows, Strait of Sicily, Aegean crossings, Tyrrhenian passages). Base costs are set proportional
to segment length using the orbis median coastal cost ratio (0.03 denarii km−1; Table I of [1]).
• Fluvial and canal routes (104 edges): 99 upstream, downstream, and fast-current river segments from orbis
plus five canal edges from a supplementary GeoJSON layer, covering the Nile, Euphrates, Rhine, Danube, and

C.
Byzantine temporal dataset (400–1453 CE)
For the period 400–1453 ce we construct a dedicated temporal dataset, Full empire byzantina, from Full Empire Nodes v2
by assigning each node a byzantine end date derived from nine documented historical events and shocks affecting
imperial control or network operation.
The 1,938 nodes active at 400 ce contract to 67 by 1453 ce — a 96.5 % reduction — concentrated in discrete jumps
(as given in Table II).
The Byzantine edge dataset (2,492 edges) keeps the trimodal architecture of the Full Empire: 1,609 maritime

hinterland when the Mediterranean sea-lanes constituted the remaining coherence link of the imperial network.

D.
Macroeconomic Friction Index: Byzantine extension
The Macroeconomic Friction Index of [1] is extended from 400 ce to 1450 ce in the dataset
macroeconomic friction byzance.csv (1,238 rows; 22 columns). Two independent proxies of economic activity are
used:
a.
Lead proxy.
Sub-annual Greenland ice-core lead concentrations from McConnell et al. [14] are used as a proxy
for metallurgical activity over −50 to 800 ce. Concentrations are decade-averaged and normalised to the Antonine
baseline of 3.411 pg g−1 (118 ce, Trajanic peak). For 810–1450 ce, where no continuous ice-core record is available, six
literature-calibrated control points anchor a cubic-spline reconstruction: Macedonian recovery (950 ce, 2.2 pg g−1),
Komnenian peak (1100 ce, 2.8 pg g−1), Palaeologan contraction (1350 ce, 1.4 pg g−1), and pre-fall minimum (1440 ce,
0.9 pg g−1). Uncertainty is propagated as a symmetric 1σ band using the McConnell et al. uncertainty for the observed
tier and a conservative ±30 % for the reconstructed tier.
b.
Hoard proxy.
As a sign of economic instability, coin-hoard frequencies from the Coin Hoards of the Roman
Republic and Empire (chrr) database (4,175 records; [17]) are used, following the broader historical and numismatic
use of hoard evidence in Roman economic analysis [18]: a hoard not recovered implies that its depositor did not
return. For 0–450 ce, observed regional hoard counts from chrr are used. For 451–1450 ce, documented monetary
debasements of the nomisma are used: the Nikephoros II reform (963 ce), the Alexios I recoinage (1092 ce), and
the Palaeologan debasement sequence (1261–1350 ce) each produce step increases in the hoard friction factor
proportional to the documented silver content reduction.
c.
Shock multipliers.
Four historically documented fiscal and structural shocks are encoded as discrete multipliers
on the commercial-capture parameter α(t): the Justinianic Plague (τ = 541 ce, reco = 1.00; geographic shock only),
Arab Conquests (τ = 647 ce, r = 0.59), Chrysobull of Venice (τ = 1082 ce, r = 0.44), and Palaeologan-Genoese
transfer (τ = 1261 ce, r = 0.55). The shock parameters τ are fixed from historical sources.

The combined resilience index Hcombined.
Hgeo and Heco answer two separate questions, but neither
answers the question most relevant to collapse forecasting: how much of the commercial network’s cycle redundancy
can the Byzantine state actually mobilise for defence, administration, and fiscal extraction? Heco includes routes under

resilience. On the other hand, Hgeo counts only territorially controlled routes.

capture value from routes it no longer controls. It is the metric that, when it falls to H∗, signals that the state can no
longer sustain the minimum routing redundancy required to respond to systemic shocks — the topological condition
we identify with political collapse.

A.
Baseline East–West asymmetry
The central result of the Full Empire analysis is that the topological asymmetry between the Eastern and Western
sub-networks is not a product of the Crisis of the Third Century, nor of any identifiable political or military event
within the study period: it is present at the first decade of the series (0 ce) and grows monotonically thereafter.
Figure 2 shows the β1 persistent entropy time series for the Eastern (Heast), Western (Hwest), and Full Empire
(Hfull) networks at each decade from 0 to 400 ce. The Phase I mean values are ¯Heast = 3.584, ¯Hfull = 3.802, and
¯Hwest = 1.362, yielding a baseline structural gap of ∆H0 = 3.584 −1.362 = +2.222 entropy units at Phase I. The
western network is not simply less topologically rich than the eastern one: it operates in a qualitatively different
regime. The β1 cycle count of β1 ≈52 per decade in the western network (vs. the Ht = 0 artifact reported in [1]
for the 49-node orbis extract) confirms that western commercial exchange was structured into redundant circuits
— primarily maritime arcs connecting Hispania, Africa Occidentalis, Italia, and Britannia — but that these circuits
were substantially fewer, shorter, and less interconnected than those of the Eastern core.
The gap is not narrowing. A linear fit to ∆H(t) = Heast(t)−Hwest(t) yields a slope of d(∆H)/dt = +0.0033 entropy
units yr−1 (R2 = 0.41, p = 0.003; Fig. 2b), meaning the Eastern network was gaining topological advantage over the
Western one at a rate of +0.033 entropy units per decade throughout 0–400 ce. The Theodosian political division of
395 ce did not create the East–West structural difference: it formalised a divergence that had been accumulating for
four centuries.

Crisis by 260 years, external pressure cannot be the primary explanation for western fragility. The asymmetry was
endogenous to the network from the Augustan period.
D.
Hub-selection artifact: methodological validation
The N-convergence protocol described in § II G produces a methodological result of independent significance. A
researcher using the standard top-degree heuristic at N = 800 would conclude that the Eastern Mediterranean network
was gaining topological coherence during 310–400 ce (see Table V).
At N = 400, the sample consists mainly of eastern degree-hubs (Alexandria, Antioch, Caesarea Maritima, Ephesus,
Thessalonike), which form a dense, high-entropy subgraph that represents Eastern Phase III decline. At N = 800, the
first western hubs enter the sample (Carthago, Carthago Nova, Mediolanum, Burdigala, Londinium). Their addition
dilutes the inter-regional cycle structure without contributing sufficient western cycles to compensate. The measured
β1 entropy drops artificially between N = 400 and N = 800 during Phase III, producing a spurious positive slope.
Any historical network with geographically skewed degree distributions is susceptible to the hub-selection artifact
when tda is applied with top-degree sub-sampling. The minimum-coverage convergence protocol (§ II G) should be
applied as a standard validity check before reporting topological break-test results on historical networks.

C.

shock: it alters the state’s capacity to capture value from Mediterranean trade without physically removing the
commercial route infrastructure.

East–West structural divergence, the commercial network (Heco) was much more resilient than the geographic network

The crucial additional finding is the western contrast: Rwest

it, because the western commercial system lacked the independent maritime circuit structure that allowed the eastern

b.
Harper: climate and pandemic as demographic, not structural, shocks.
Harper [12]’s thesis is that
climate deterioration (the Late Antique Little Ice Age, 536–660 ce, as reconstructed by B¨untgen et al. [24]) and pan-
demic mortality (Justinianic Plague, 541 ce; for a recent reassessment of its macrohistorical impact, see Mordechai
et al. [25]) were the primary structural drivers of Roman imperial fragility. The present results support the demo-
graphic component of Harper’s argument but challenge the structural component.
The Justinianic Plague of 541 ce produces the largest Chow F-statistic in the 0–1453 ce geographic series (F =
152.69), larger than the Phase III break of [1] (F310 = 85.4). The plague was a genuine structural shock to the
geographic network, consistent with Harper’s emphasis on its severity. However, the plague produces no detectable
break in Heco (∆Heco(541) = −0.04, within the bootstrap 95 % CI). The commercial network shows no detectable
short-term topological break at the plague, indicating that the disruption was demographic without destroying the
infrastructure.
If the plague was a demographic shock, commercial recovery could occur as population recovered, consistent with
the evidence for late sixth-century commercial revival before the Arab conquests.
The Heco series supports this
interpretation.
The climate signal is not directly testable with our network methodology, which does not incorporate meteorological
data.
c.
Wickham: structural transformation confirmed and antedated.
Wickham’s [8] structural transforma-
tion argument — that the dissolution of the tax-and-redistribute imperial economy into localised subsistence exchange
is the defining transition of late antiquity — receives its strongest quantitative support from the congenital asymmetry
result.
The topological evidence places the onset of divergence before 0 ce: the gap was already +2.22 entropy units at
the beginning of the Principate, and growing at +0.0033 entropy units per year throughout 0–400 ce. The Crisis of
the Third Century accelerated the divergence at 260 ce (Chow F = 29.41) but did not initiate it.
The western commercial system was subordinate to the eastern one from the Augustan settlement: the transfor-
mation was a property of the initial network configuration.
B.
Eastern-Western asymmetry and dynamical indicators
In the Roman–Byzantine case, Rd grows from ≈1.09 at the western collapse (476 ce) to a maximum of Rd =
47.7 at 620 ce (Hgeo = 0.075), and Rd = 13.9 at 640 ce, reflecting the progressive institutionalisation of trade in
church networks, diaspora merchant communities, and bilateral treaty arrangements that did not require Roman
administrative infrastructure.
If Rd is around 1, it means that geographic and economic collapse are coupled, and that the failure of one triggers
the failure of the other.
The western network’s Rd ≈1.09 at collapse indicates a fully coupled system with no
adaptive buffer; the eastern network’s Rd = 13.9 at the Arab Conquest peak indicates a decoupled system with
adaptive capacity. The difference in collapse trajectories — western terminal in one century, eastern resilient for ten
centuries — may be explained in part by this difference in decoupling ratio.
The Wasserstein analyses add independent quantitative support for this interpretation. The cross-network ratio
ρ(t) = Wcross(Admin, Geo)/Wcross(Admin, Trade)
equals 1.0 before 640 ce, then rises discontinuously to ∼155 at 700 ce and permanently stabilises at 100–300 after
the Chrysobull of 1082 ce (Table XII). This trajectory in diagram-space precisely recapitulates the Rd(t) trajectory
in entropy-space, providing a fully model-free confirmation of the decoupling result. The western network’s ρ ≈1.0
throughout its period confirms the absence of any decoupling buffer: both the administrative and commercial diagrams
deformed at the same rate up to the end.

VII.
CONCLUSIONS
We have extended the persistent homology formalism of Reference [1] to the Roman–Byzantine trade network (0–
1453,ce), using an integrated dataset of 2,599 nodes and 4,503 trimodal edges, a Byzantine temporal sub-dataset of
1,938 nodes and 2,492 edges, and a Macroeconomic Friction Index extended from the Greenland ice-core lead proxy of
McConnell et al. [14] to 1450,ce. We further introduced two independent dynamical observables: topological velocity
( ˙W2) and the Integrated Criticality Threshold (ict). Our main results are
• The Ht = 0 artifact and the baseline East–West asymmetry. The western sub-network result of [1]
is a data-coverage artifact of the 49-node orbis extract. With full western coverage (Nwest = 987, β1 ≈52
independent cycles per decade), the western network exhibits ¯H ∗westI = 1.362, against ¯H ∗eastI = 3.584: a
baseline structural asymmetry of +2.222 entropy units present at 0,ce and growing at +0.0033 entropy units
per year.[26]. The Theodosian partition formalised a structural difference accumulating since the Augustan
settlement; the Crisis of the Third Century accelerated it (Chow F = 29.41 at 260,ce) but did not initiate it.
• The hub-selection artifact in TDA of historical networks. Top-degree heuristic sampling at N = 800
produces a spurious positive Phase III slope (+1.69 × 10−3,yr−1) for the Eastern sub-network. Full-coverage or
a specific sampling protocol is required for reliable structural break detection in historical networks with skewed
degree distributions.
• Geographic–economic decoupling and the resolution of the Ward-Perkins–McCormick debate.
The decomposition of Byzantine resilience yields a maximum decoupling ratio Rmax
d
= 47.7 at 634 ce. The
contrast with the western collapse (Rd = 1.09 at 476 ce) defines the topological distinction between imperial
transformation and coupled geographic–economic collapse.
In the late eleventh century, Manzikert and the
Chrysobull act through different primary mechanisms. Manzikert produces territorial compaction and an ap-
parent increase in Hgeo by reorganising the surviving Byzantine network around a smaller Aegean–Balkan core
with shorter local cycles; this is not interpreted as a genuine recovery of large-scale geographic resilience. The
Chrysobull of 1082 ce produces an independent fiscal break in Heco (Chow F = 3.84, p = 0.025). The concurrent
break in Hgeo (F = 17.35, p < 0.0001) is methodologically dependent on the Manzikert territorial contraction
of 1071 ce and does not represent an autonomous geographic shock at 1082 ce.
• The collapse threshold H∗= 0.524. Both the Western Roman collapse (476,ce) and the Byzantine collapse
(1453,ce) occur at H∗= 0.524 ± 0.031 (bootstrap 95% CI).
• Topological velocity: the Late Roman transition is the highest-velocity event of the 1,453-year series. The
W2 Wasserstein velocity ˙W ∗2, sm, computed between consecutive decadal persistence diagrams, identifies the
Late Roman–Early Byzantine structural transition (495,ce,
˙W ∗2, sm = 0.177) as the largest diagram-space
displacement in the entire 0–1453,ce record, exceeding the Justinianic Plague (541,ce, 0.101) and the Arab
Conquests (641,ce, 0.094). For the Roman period (0–400,ce), the admin network peaks at ˙W ∗2, sm = 54.4,yr−1
(405,ce), and the cross-network divergence W ∗cross(Admin, Trade) increases by 13.9× from Phase I to 470,ce.
• Cross-network Wasserstein decoupling confirms the late eleventh-century bifurcation. The ratio
ρ = Wcross(Admin, Geo)/Wcross(Admin, Trade) = 1
before 640,ce, rises to ∼155 at 700,ce, and stabilises at 100–300× after 1082,ce. This result provides in-
dependent confirmation of the entropy-level Rd decoupling, showing that the geographic and administrative–
commercial persistence diagrams evolved under different dynamics after the economic restructuring of the late
eleventh century.
These results establish that persistent homology applied to layered, temporally-resolved trade networks — supple-
mented by topological velocity and criticality indicators derived from the full persistence diagram geometry — provides
structural insights not accessible to conventional historical or network-analytic methods: the identification of baseline
structural asymmetries, the decomposition of civilisational resilience into geographic and economic components, the
model-free detection of commercial restructuring events in diagram space, and early-warning signals.
