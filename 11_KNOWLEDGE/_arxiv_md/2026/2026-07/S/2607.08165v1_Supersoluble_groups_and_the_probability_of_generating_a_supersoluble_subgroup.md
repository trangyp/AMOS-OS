SUPERSOLUBLE GROUPS AND THE PROBABILITY OF
GENERATING A SUPERSOLUBLE SUBGROUP
ANDREA LUCCHINI
Abstract. Let G be a finite group and let PU(G) denote the probability that
two randomly chosen elements of G generate a supersoluble subgroup.
We
prove that if PU(G) ≥16/25 then G is supersoluble, and that the bound
16/25 is sharp, being attained by the group G = (C5 × C5) ⋊Q8, where Q8
acts faithfully and irreducibly on C5 × C5.
1. Introduction
Let C be a class of finite groups. We say that C is recognizable if, whenever all
2-generated subgroups of a finite group G belong to C, then G itself belongs to C. It
is well known for example that the class of finite soluble groups is recognizable (see
Thompson [10] and Flavell [4]). On the other hand the class of finite metabelian
groups is not recognizable (see [2, Example 7.1]). More quantitatively, for 0 < η < 1,
we say that C is η-recognizable if, whenever the probability that a 2-generated
subgroup of G belongs to C is greater than η, then G ∈C. Formally, letting
PC(G) = |{(x, y) ∈G × G | ⟨x, y⟩∈C}|
|G|2
,
we say that C is η-recognizable if PC(G) > η implies G ∈C.
Clearly, every η-recognizable class is recognizable. It is natural to ask whether
every recognizable class is η-recognizable for some η, and what the optimal value
of η is.
Guralnick and Wilson [7] proved that for any class C closed under taking sub-
groups, quotients and extensions, C is η-recognizable for some η > 0 (combining [9,
Proposition 5] with [9, Theorem 1.1], one may deduce that η can be taken to be 37
90).
In particular they proved that the class of finite soluble groups is 11
30-recognizable
and that this bound is sharp.
Earlier, Gustafson [6] had proved that the class of finite abelian groups is 5
8-
recognizable, while Guralnick and Wilson in [7] also prove that the class of finite
nilpotent groups is 1
2-recognizable. The next natural candidate to investigate is the
class U of supersoluble groups, which is not closed under taking extension and lies
strictly between the classes of nilpotent and soluble groups. It is known that U
is recognizable (see for instance [1, Example 1]). Our main result determines the
optimal threshold for η-recognizability.
Theorem 1. The class of supersoluble groups is 16
25-recognizable. More precisely,
if G is a finite group with PU(G) ≥
16
25, then G is supersoluble. Moreover, the
bound 16
25 is sharp, being attained by the group G = (C5 × C5) ⋊Q8, where Q8 acts
faithfully and irreducibly on C5 × C5.

We note that it remains an open problem whether every recognizable class is
η-recognizable for some η > 0. Natural candidates for further investigation are the
class of groups with nilpotent derived subgroup and, more generally, the class of
groups of Fitting length at most k, for a fixed positive integer k. These classes are
known to be recognizable [1, Examples 2 and 3], but whether they are η-recognizable
for some η > 0 remains open.
The results of Guralnick and Wilson easily reduce the proof of Theorem 1 to
a detailed analysis of primitive soluble groups with supersoluble point-stabilizers.
Recall that a finite group is primitive if it has a faithful primitive permutation
representation; equivalently, it has a core-free maximal subgroup.
A primitive
soluble group has the form G = V ⋊H, where V = Soc(G) is elementary abelian
and H acts faithfully and irreducibly on V .
The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we establish the key lemmas on
the module-theoretic structure of primitive soluble groups. In Section 3 we prove
the main theorem.

3.2. Reduction to the primitive case. We now prove Theorem 1 by induction
on |G|.
Proof of Theorem 1. Let G be a finite group with PU(G) ≥16/25. We proceed by
induction on |G|.
Step 1: All proper quotients of G are supersoluble. Let N be a non-trivial normal
subgroup of G.
Since every 2-generated subgroup of G/N is the image of a 2-
generated subgroup of G, we have PU(G/N) ≥PU(G) ≥16/25. By induction,
G/N is supersoluble.
Step 2: G has a unique minimal normal subgroup. Suppose G has two distinct
minimal normal subgroups N1 and N2.
Then N1 ∩N2 = 1, so G embeds into
G/N1 × G/N2. Since both G/N1 and G/N2 are supersoluble by Step 1, and the
class of supersoluble groups is closed under taking subgroups and direct products,
G is supersoluble.
Step 3: G is soluble. The probability that two random elements of G generate a
soluble subgroup is at least PU(G) ≥16/25 > 11/30. By a theorem of Guralnick
and Wilson [7, Theorem A], the class of finite soluble groups is 11
30-recognizable,
hence G is soluble.
Step 4: G is primitive soluble. By Steps 1 and 3, G is soluble with a unique minimal
normal subgroup N. If N is contained in the Frattini subgroup Φ(G) of G, then
G/Φ(G) is a quotient of G/N, hence supersoluble. Since supersolubility is inherited
from G/Φ(G) to G [8, VI Satz 8.6], G would be supersoluble, a contradiction.
Therefore N ̸≤Φ(G), so there exists a maximal subgroup M of G with NM = G
and N ∩M = 1.
Since N is the unique minimal normal subgroup of G and
CoreG(M) ≤N ∩M = 1, the subgroup M is core-free, and G is a primitive soluble
group with Soc(G) = N.
Step 5: Conclusion. Write G = V ⋊H where V = N is elementary abelian of
order pn and H ∼= M is the point stabilizer. Since G/N ∼= H is supersoluble by
Step 1, and PU(G) ≥16/25, Lemma 4 implies that G is supersoluble, completing
the induction.
Sharpness. The group G = (C5×C5)⋊Q8, where Q8 acts faithfully and irreducibly
on C5 × C5, is not supersoluble, since its unique minimal normal subgroup C5 × C5
has order 25 = 52, which is not prime. However, all proper subgroups of G are
supersoluble: indeed, since 5 ≡1 (mod 4), the field F5 contains a primitive fourth
root of unity, so every proper subgroup of Q8 acts reducibly on C5 × C5, and hence
every proper subgroup of G is supersoluble. Since the probability that an ordered
pair of elements generates G is 9/25, it follows that
PU(G) = 1 −9/25 = 16/25.
This shows that the bound 16/25 is sharp.
□
