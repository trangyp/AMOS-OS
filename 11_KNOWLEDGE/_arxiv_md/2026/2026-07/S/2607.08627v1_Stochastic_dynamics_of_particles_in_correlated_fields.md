Abstract.
The effective dynamics of a colloidal particle immersed in a complex
medium at equilibrium is usually described in terms of a linear overdamped Langevin
equation, possibly with memory.
However, numerical simulations and experiments
have shown that this linear model fails, suggesting that the effective dynamics of the
probe is actually nonlinear. Focusing on the case in which the medium is described
by a fluctuating and correlated Gaussian field, linearly coupled to the colloid, we
derive this effective dynamics and discuss its various consequences, including those on
the stochastic thermodynamics of a driven particle. When the field is generated by
the particle itself, with negligible fluctuations, the resulting self-chemotactic dynamics
turns out to display anomalous diffusion and run-and-tumble motion in low spatial
dimension, which we characterise analytically.

of interest are provided by near-critical liquid media: upon approaching their critical
point, a collective fluctuating field naturally emerges, which is the order parameter ϕ

space and in time, and the spatial correlation length ξ and corresponding correlation
time might become so large as to challenge the separation of time scales, which is usually
behind the idea of deriving a Langevin equation. Moreover, in these critical media, it
was theoretically predicted and experimentally demonstrated that when the correlation
length ξ becomes comparable with their distance, two particles are subject to a mutual
fluctuation-induced force, known as critical Casimir forces (see, e.g., Refs. [11, 12] for
reviews) which were also mentioned in the motivation for the award to Mehran Kardar of
the 2025 Boltzmann Medal. In the remaining part of this work, we briefly review some
recent progresses in understanding the stochastic dynamics of particles in correlated
fields, which we contributed to. In particular, in Sec. 2 we introduce a simple model
of a particle in interaction with a fluctuating Gaussian field ϕ, both of which are in
contact with a bath at thermal equilibrium. We present the effective dynamics of this
particle and the fluctuation-dissipation theorem that it satisfies. Then, in Sec. 3 we
discuss the features of this effective dynamics, focusing on the power spectral density of
the fluctuations and of their non-Gaussian statistics. In Sec. 4, the particle is driven out
of equilibrium by a moving trap. Similarly to the case of viscoelastic media discussed
above, it turns out that the relaxation of the particle after a displacement occurs via
damped oscillations. In this non-equilibrium setting and due to the presence of spatial
correlations, the stochastic thermodynamics of the system needs to be formulated in
terms of the fields associated with work and heat. Depending on the correlation length
ξ, for example, it turns out that the rate at which the field ϕ extracts heat from the
thermal bath acquires an unexpected spatial structure. In Sec. 5 we consider a non-
reciprocal extension of the model introduced in Sec. 2 which actually describes the
behaviour of self-chemotactic particles. In particular, we focus on how the mean square
displacement at long times depends on the dimensionality of the problem and on the
possible attractive or repulsive character of the self chemotaxis. Finally, in Sec. 6 we
present our summary and outlook.

3. Dynamics at equilibrium
Having established the effective equation of motion for the particle in interaction with
the field, one can now explore the observable consequences of having both a non-linear
and non-Markovian evolution. As we discussed in Sec. 1, the power spectral density
S(ω) (i.e., the Fourier transform of the two-time correlation function ⟨X(t)X(t′)⟩in
the stationary state) provides useful information about the dynamics. In the absence
of the interaction between the particle and the field, i.e., for λ = 0, this is given by
the Lorentzian form discussed in Sec. 1 (with ω0 = κ/γ∞) and shown as a red line
in the left panel of Fig. 2. Upon setting λ ̸= 0, the evolution equation, being non-
linear, can no longer be solved exactly. A viable approach (which can be tested against
numerical simulations) is to resort to a perturbative expansion in λ, which turns out to
involve only even power of the coupling, due to the symmetry {ϕ, λ} ↔{−ϕ, −λ} of
the problem [18,20]. The resulting spectral density S(ω), including the first non-trivial
correction at order λ2, is also shown in the left panel of Fig. 2 in spatial dimension
d = 3 and for various values of the distance r from the critical point (located at r = 0).
One observes that, upon approaching the critical point, S(ω) develops an algebraic
singularity S(ω) ∝ω−1+d/4 as ω →0 (we assume d < 4).
This behaviour differs
qualitatively from the one due to the hydrodynamic memory discussed in Sec. 1, which
takes the form of a resonance occurring at a frequency determined by the properties of
the medium. Here, instead, the modification occurs at low frequencies, as heuristically
expected whenever a critical behaviour is involved. In particular, this implies that the
effective noise becomes coloured due to the emerging memory.

4. Driving the particle
In the previous section, we discussed the case in which the particle and the field are
both in contact with an equilibrium thermal bath, focussing on various aspects of the
resulting effective dynamics of the particle. However, the simple model introduced in
Sec. 2 can be easily generalised to encompass the relevant case in which the particle is
driven through the medium by a moving trap, e.g., a moving optical tweezer (such as
that experimentally realised in, e.g., Ref. [7]). Differently from the previous case, the
system constituted by the particle and the field will eventually settle in a non-equilibrium
steady state, displaying the novel behaviours discussed below, some of which resemble
those mentioned in Sec. 1, though in a different physical setting.

5. Making the particle active
The model presented in Sec. 2 lends itself to an interesting generalisation out of
equilibrium, which might even render the particle active [26]. In the previous Sections,
the interaction between the particle and the field ϕ was reciprocal — as it derived from
an interaction in the common Hamiltonian H — and the noises satisfied the fluctuation-
dissipation relations. A possible way of violating the latter conditions is to assume, for
example, that no fluctuations affect the evolution of the field ϕ (i.e., that the noise η
in Eq. (2) vanishes). Moreover, one can also break the reciprocity of the interaction
between the field ϕ and the particle, by fixing, in their equations of motion (8), the
corresponding interaction strengths (which, in equilibrium, are both given by λ) to
different values in the two equations. As a result, these interactions can no longer be
derived by a common interaction Hamiltonian Hint. When this is done starting from the
model in Eq. (2), with the field evolving at criticality with model A dynamics instead
of the model B considered there, and with a point-like interaction U(x) = δd(x), one

of the particle, it is convenient to focus on the mean square displacement MSD =

If the chemical emitted by the colloid decays in time (which corresponds to adding
a term −κϕ on the r.h.s. of the evolution equation for ϕ in Eq. (18)), the particle
quickly loses memory of its past motion: velocity correlations decay rapidly and the
long-time dynamics is diffusive, with a renormalized diffusion coefficient [31, 32]. In
contrast, for the chemically persistent species produced by most chemotactic particles,
the self-generated chemical landscape retains a record of the particle’s trajectory, leading
to strong nonlinear memory effects in the dynamics.
While previous studies were
primarily concerned with determining effective diffusion coefficients in this case (see,
e.g., Refs. [33, 34]), here we focus on the long-time behaviour of the MSD. It actually
turns out [26] that a sort of upper critical dimensionality dc emerges, with dc = 2, such
that normal diffusion with MSD ∝t as t →∞is recovered at long times for d > dc,
independently of whether the chemotaxis is repulsive or attractive (i.e., independently
of the sign of λ). For d = dc, the MSD develops, as expected, logarithmic corrections
as a function of time. For d < dc and, in particular in d = 1, the dynamics becomes
superdiffusive with MSD ∝t4/3 for λ < 0, while the dependence on time is logarithmic
MSD ∝log t for λ > 0 because the trail of the particle hinders its motion by attracting
it. In the repulsive case λ < 0, the superdiffusive behavior is due to the fact that, as
anticipated, the motion of the particle resembles a run-and-tumble motion, where the
run occurs approximately with the self-propulsion velocity v, while the noise determines
a tumbling process with a strong memory given by the field that the particle generates
while moving.
The predictions described above can be derived analytically, in part
with suitable scaling arguments and, for the repulsive case in d = 1, by determining
from Eq. (18) an effective run-and-tumble process with correlated tumbles which, in
turn, can be solved analytically. These predictions were also confirmed by numerical
simulations [26]. We also mention that the repulsive chemotaxis in d = 1 turns out to
be related to the so-called true self-avoiding random walk (TSAW), which was studied
long ago in Refs. [35–37].
6. Conclusions and perspectives
The simple model introduced in Sec. 2 lends itself to a number of interesting and relevant
extensions, that may also bring it closer to realistic experimental systems. Among them,
we mention:
(1) Explore additional and observable consequences of the memory emerging in the
correlated medium. For example, Ref. [38] demonstrated that the driven particle


## Page 17


Stochastic dynamics of particles in correlated fields
17
discussed in Sec. 4 may feature a memory-induced recoil after switching off the drive,
analogously to what was observed in viscoelastic media [39]. Similarly, motivated by
recent investigations in this kind of media [40–43], it would be interesting to study
the effects of correlations on, e.g., microrheology (partly addressed in Ref. [23]) or
the influence on barrier crossing etc.
(2) Account for the self-interaction of the field ϕ, e.g., ∝ϕ4 is certainly necessary in
order to be able to explore the connection with actual critical systems, where it is
well-known that the fluctuations of the order parameter field ϕ are non-Gaussian. In
this direction, it would be important to consider models of field dynamics which are
more realistic for actual fluids such as, e.g., model H [19]. Similarly, the extension
to the case in which the coupling between the particle and the field is also non-linear
in the field might be or relevance for certain applications.
(3) Considering the simultaneous presence of more than one particle within the medium
opens up the possibility to explore the effects of correlation on the dynamics of the
particles and on the possible structure that they may form. This is particularly
relevant for understanding, e.g., the dynamics of fluctuation-induced forces beyond
mean-field theory [44] or the dynamics of polymers [45] in such media. In this
direction, Ref. [46] has recently studied the model of Sec. 2 in which the field ϕ is
constrained to vanish at a flat boundary, reminding the classical setting in which
Casimir-like forces emerge [11]. The particle then interacts with ϕ as in Eq. (7)
and, because of the constraint on ϕ, it turns out to be subject to a repulsive force
from the boundary, with a range set by the correlation length ξ.
(4) Considering the case in which the particle is active in a passive medium at
equilibrium or the complementary case in which the particle is passive but the
underlying medium is active is also of practical and conceptual interest.
