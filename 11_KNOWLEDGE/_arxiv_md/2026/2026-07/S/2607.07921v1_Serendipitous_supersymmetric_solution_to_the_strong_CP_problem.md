Abstract
The Minimal Supersymmetric Standard Model (MSSM) has several problems: 1.
its
µ term must be forbidden, then regenerated at the weak scale, 2. it allows for R-parity
violating superpotential terms which lead to rapid proton decay, 3. it allows for dimension-
5 proton decay operators. The usual imposition of R- or matter parity PM solves only
the second of these, whereas anomaly-free discrete ZR
n symmetries (consistent with grand
unification) address all of them. Once the µ-term is forbidden, the MSSM develops an
accidental global U(1)PQ symmetry. By coupling the Higgs fields to PQ-charged gauge
singlet fields X, Y (in the Kim-Nilles mechanism), and imposing SUSY breaking, one
regenerates µ at the weak scale whilst breaking the discrete ZR
n and the U(1)PQ. The
broken global U(1)PQ develops a pseudo-Goldstone boson, the DFSZ axion, thus (perhaps
inadvertently) solving the strong CP problem. In this setting, SUSY develops a dark
matter candidate, the SUSY DFSZ axion, and possibly, though not necessarily, a WIMP
dark matter candidate as well, depending on the order of the induced R-parity violating
operators.

The Standard Model (SM) is beset with three finetuning problems (if we include gravity in
the mix): 1. the cosmological constant (CC) problem, 2. the gauge hierarchy problem (GHP,
due to Higgs mass instability under radiative corrections) and 3. the strong CP problem (why
is the QCD ¯θ parameter so tiny, ¯θ ≡θ + arg(det(M)) ≲10−10, as required by measurements of
the neutron EDM [1]?). The first of these, the CC problem, does not seem to have a satisfactory
solution, although anthropic selection of tiny Λ could occur in the context of a multiverse as
expected from the string landscape [2]. Many avenues for the GHP have been explored, but the
most compelling is that of weak scale supersymmetry (SUSY), which is also experimentally well-
supported by a variety of precision measurements when compared to radiative corrections [3].
The most convincing solution to the third of these, the strong CP problem, seems to be
the presence of a spontaneously broken global U(1)PQ symmetry [4–7] which gives rise to
a very weakly coupled axion field which allows the offending CP-violating term in QCD to
dynamically relax to (near) zero [8,9]. The axion solution to strong CP and the SUSY solution
to the GHP are usually thought to be independent entities, and lead to different dark matter
candidates: the axion field produced as cold dark matter in the early universe via coherent field
oscillations [10] (and other mechanisms [11]) and the lightest SUSY particle (LSP) (provided
R-parity is conserved), expected to be the lightest neutralino, a weakly interacting massive
particle (WIMP) candidate [12].
It is not well-appreciated that a solution to the strong CP problem actually emerges from
the second of these via a proper implementation of the Minimal Supersymmetric Standard
Model (MSSM). The MSSM begins with the SM gauge symmetry SU(3)C × SU(2)L × U(1)Y
with fields now elevated to superfields, and where two Higgs doublets are now required so that
higgsino contributions to triangle anomalies will cancel [13]. The superpotential is given by

Constructing the MSSM: role of R-parity
This usual procedure for constructing the MSSM actually side-steps two other important issues:

• The SUSY µ problem which arises because the superpotential Higgs bilinear term WMSSM ⊃
µHuHd is actually SUSY-conserving. The associated mass scale µ would be expected at
mP rather than the phenomenologically required value of msoft. In order to solve the
SUSY µ problem [16], one must first forbid it from the superpotential, and then regen-
erate it at the weak scale. Three ways to regenerate it include 1. the Kim-Nilles (KN)
mechanism [17] where µ arises from non-renormalizable terms in the superpotential, 2.
the Giudice-Masiero (GM) mechanism [18] where µ arises from non-renormalizable terms
in the K¨ahler potential and 3. where µ arises from the Higgs bilinear coupling to a SM
gauge singlet superfield as in the NMSSM [19].
• Dangerous dimension-5 proton decay operators [20] in W5 need suppressed coefficients

While the invocation of RPC forbids B and L violation in the superpotential, it allows for the
µ term and the W5 proton decay terms.
Ibanez and Ross classified all anomaly-free discrete gauge symmetries of order 2 and 3 [21];
they then proposed baryon triality B3 as an alternative. Under B3, B violating superpotential
terms are forbidden while L-violating terms are allowed, thus saving the proton from dim-4
decay operators. B3 also forbids the W5 p-decay operators, but allows for the µ term. Dreiner,
Luhn and Thormeier examined discrete gauge symmetries at higher order N, and proposed
proton hexality P6 as an alternative to RPC [22]. Under P6, all RPV superpotential terms are
forbidden as are the W5 p-decay operators. The µ term is still allowed under P6. Furthermore,
the P6 charge assignments conflict with unified matter representations in SU(5) and SO(10)
GUTs. This is of concern even in the more attractive modern day rendition of local GUTs where
matter representations appear at various locales in compactified extra-dimensions as found in
string constructs [23–25].

anomaly-free (up to a universal Green-Schwarz term [27]) and with charge values consistent
with SU(5) or SO(10) unification.

3
Accidental, approximate global PQ
At this point, we have constructed the MSSM as usual except that we have replaced the
imposition of RPC by the imposition of one of the anomaly-free ZR
n symmetries. This improves
upon the usual MSSM construction in that it 1. forbids the µ term, 2. forbids the various RPV
terms and 3. forbids the dangerous dim-5 p-decay operators all the while 4. allowing for the
usual superpotential Yukawa terms and terms needed for imposing the neutrino see-saw. The
µ term is regenerated as a consequence of SUSY breaking.
Now a wonderful thing happens to the model before SUSY breaking occurs. In this case, it is
easy to check that the MSSM, augmented by any of the four base models, enjoys a global U(1)PQ
phase invariance where superfields transform as Φ →eiαqΦ (where Φ is a generic left-chiral
superfield), q is the charge and α is the global phase. The superfield PQ charge assignments for
each of the base models are listed in Table 3 (normalized so that q(Hu,d) = −1). For invariance
under the global U(1)PQ, it is straightforward to check that for each of the allowed MSSM
superpotential terms, the PQ charges sum to zero.
It is important to note here that the global U(1) emerges as an accidental global phase
invariance, and occurs as a consequence of the structure of each base model, in much the
same way as global B and L conservation emerge accidentally in the SM as a consequence of
gauge invariance. This is most propitious since global symmetries are well-known to be not
fundamental, and are not respected by quantum gravity effects (black-hole no-hair arguments
and, more broadly, the expectation that exact global symmetries are absent in quantum gravity)
[37,38].
Upon imposing SUSY breaking, the X and Y fields develop intermediate-scale vevs which
break the ZR
n symmetry and generate a weak scale µ term. But now the global U(1)PQ is also
broken since X and Y carry PQ charges. By Goldstone’s theorem, a massless Goldstone boson
(a combination of the X and Y field phases) must develop which is identified as the QCD axion.
In our base models, it is realized that these are just supersymmetrized versions of the DFSZ
axion model [39,40], and so the axion is of SUSY DFSZ type. The axion develops a tiny mass
due to non-perturbative effects and so becomes a pseudo-Goldstone boson [41].

Some phenomenological consequences
We have focused here on the SUSY DFSZ axion emerging from the MSSM construction which
incorporates a KN solution to the µ problem. The DFSZ axion has a domain wall number
NDW = 6, so there is a danger that domain walls may form in the early universe [42] leading
to, among other things, overproduction of dark matter. We assume throughout that the PQ
symmetry is broken during inflation and not restored afterwards (fa ≳max(TR, HI/2π)), so
any domain wall structures are inflated away.
In the model considered here, a SUSY DFSZ axion always appears and so would be one of
the constituents of dark matter. We remind that the imposition of a ZR
n symmetry forbids the
several R-parity violating terms from the superpotential, so one might expect RPC and the
LSP to provide an additional DM candidate. In Ref. [34,43], it is noted that higher dimensional
operators of the form

Conclusions
In this paper, we have pointed out that the axion solution to the strong CP problem emerges
accidentally and serendipitously as a byproduct of solving the SUSY µ problem in the MSSM.
N = 1 spacetime SUSY can emerge from string compactifications on Calabi-Yau manifolds, and
its breaking at the weak scale is supported by solving the gauge hierarchy problem. Discrete R
symmetries can arise as discrete remnants from compactification of 10-d Lorentzian spacetime
to 4-dimensions, and their imposition provides the first step in solving the SUSY µ problem:
namely, forbidding the appearance of µ at scales far beyond the weak scale. The coupling of
the Higgs bilinear HuHd to R-charged fields X and Y via non-renormalizable operators is in
accord with the ZR
n symmetries, but in this guise the theory develops an accidental global U(1)
symmetry which can be identified as U(1)PQ. Then under SUSY breaking, the X and Y fields
develop ZR
n and PQ breaking vevs at an intermediate scale (the cosmologically-favored sweet
spot fa ∼√mweakmP ∼1010 −1012 GeV) in the KN mechanism. The spontaneously broken
global PQ then develops an axion as the associated Goldstone boson.
The U(1)PQ is also
broken via anomalies, so the axion develops its mass at scale ma ∼Λ2
QCD/fa, and its coupling
to gluons allows the CP-violating Lagrangian terms that contribute to the neutron EDM to
relax towards zero, thus solving the strong CP problem. In this sense, the axion solution to the
strong CP problem is a derived consequence of supersymmetrizing the SM, and stabilizing its
µ term via one of the anomaly-free ZR
n symmetries, and then regenerating it via a Kim-Nilles
operator. This result may well be known to a number of researchers [17,26,50], but it seems
to be hidden in the literature and certainly not appreciated by the wider community.
