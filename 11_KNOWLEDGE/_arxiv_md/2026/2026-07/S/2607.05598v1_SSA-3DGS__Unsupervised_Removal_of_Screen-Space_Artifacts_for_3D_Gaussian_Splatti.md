Abstract
Novel View Synthesis (NVS) methods, such as 3D Gaus-
sian Splatting (3DGS), rely heavily on the assumption of
clean, multi-view consistent, posed input images.
Real-
world captures can violate this assumption due to screen-
space artifacts—static occlusions fixed to the 2D image
plane rather than to the 3D world. Common examples in-
clude physical sensor defects, environmental obstructions
(such as rain or mud on the lens enclosure), capture ob-
structions (such as a thumb over the camera sensor or a
dashboard visible in dashcam footage), and digital overlays
(such as watermarks or UI elements). When present, they
are erroneously baked into the 3D geometry as “floaters” or
near-camera artifacts, degrading the quality of novel-view
rendering. In this work, we propose SSA-3DGS, an unsu-
pervised framework that jointly optimizes a 3D scene and
a learnable 2D overlay to recover a clean 3D scene and
the corrupting artifacts. By exploiting geometric consen-
sus across views, our method effectively disentangles static
artifacts from the 3D scene geometry without supervision
or manual input. Across diverse synthetic corruptions and
a self-captured real-world dataset, SSA-3DGS improves re-
construction fidelity by up to 9 dB PSNR over 3DGS trained
on the same corrupted inputs, while faithfully preserving the
corrupting artifact.
1. Introduction
The goal of Novel View Synthesis (NVS) is to capture the
visual essence of a real-world scene from multiple view-
points and render it from arbitrary novel angles. This field
has witnessed a paradigm shift with the advent of Neural
Radiance Fields (NeRF) [13] and, more recently, 3D Gaus-
sian Splatting (3DGS) [9]. 3DGS enables high-fidelity, real-
time rendering by representing scenes as a collection of
explicit 3D Gaussians rather than implicit neural weights,
making it a prime candidate for real-time applications and
3D digital content creation.
However, the reconstruction quality of novel-view syn-
thesis algorithms relies heavily on a fundamental assump-

tion in Multi-View Stereo (MVS): that the input images pro-
vide a multi-view-consistent representation of the underly-
ing scene geometry. While recent research has effectively
modeled world-space inconsistencies such as illumination
changes [15] or transient moving elements like pedestrians
[12, 16, 26, 27], these approaches struggle to handle screen-

facts). Distinct from world-space objects, these artifacts are
static relative to the camera sensor, manifesting as physi-
cal defects (dead pixels or dust), environmental effects (rain
or mud spatters on the lens enclosure), capture obstructions
(visible fingers, or car dashboards in dashcam footage), or
digital overlays (watermarks, timestamps, or UI elements).
Because these elements are fixed to the image plane, they
effectively “move” relative to the world geometry as the
camera moves. Consequently, standard novel view synthe-
sis and even approaches focusing on world-space inconsis-
tencies often fail to reject them, erroneously baking the ar-
tifacts into the 3D scene as near-camera “floaters” or view-
dependent effects.

Addressing this issue is non-trivial.
Single-view im-
age restoration and inpainting techniques [20, 21, 24] op-
erate on a per-image basis. They lack knowledge of the 3D
scene structure and hallucinate textures that differ frame-to-
frame, leading to temporal flickering and geometric incon-
sistencies when used as a multi-view pre-processing step.
Watermark-removal models, moreover, often require man-
ual annotations, which is not scalable when processing hun-
dreds of input frames.
In this work, we propose SSA-3DGS, an unsupervised
framework built upon 3DGS that disentangles 3D scene
geometry from 2D screen-space artifacts.
Our method
achieves this by formulating a simultaneous dual objec-
tive: (1) accurately separating and segmenting the 2D arti-
facts from the scene, and (2) reconstructing a high-fidelity,
artifact-free 3D asset. The key to making this joint opti-
mization work is leveraging motion parallax. As the cam-
era moves through the world, screen-space artifacts re-
main static relative to the image plane, allowing the two
signals to be decoupled without supervision.
We model
screen-space artifacts as learnable, semi-transparent over-
lays shared across all training views, jointly optimized with
the 3DGS parameters. During training, the gradients of the
reconstruction loss are distributed between the 3D model
and the 2D overlay. To prevent the overlay from absorbing
valid scene details or altering the scene’s exposure, we in-
troduce a regularization scheme that combines sparsity and
total-variation constraints.
We evaluate SSA-3DGS on diverse synthetic corrup-
tions of real-world images and on a self-captured real-
world dataset containing genuine, physically induced ar-
tifacts.
These experiments demonstrate that our method
learns clean, artifact-free 3D scenes from heavily corrupted
inputs.
In summary, our contributions are:
1. An unsupervised method to, given a multi-view record-
ing corrupted by screen-space artifacts, learn a clean
3DGS scene and the screen-space artifact via a shared
learnable 2D overlay composited with the 3DGS render.
2. A regularization scheme (sparsity and total variation)
that biases the optimization toward explaining pixels
through 3D geometry, only using the artifact layer if
needed.
3. A real-world, self-captured dataset on which we bench-
mark our proposed algorithm.
2. Related Work
2.1. Novel View Synthesis and 3D Gaussian Splat-
ting
NeRF [13], which represents scenes as continuous volumet-
ric functions optimized via differentiable ray marching, has
revolutionized the field of NVS. While NeRF achieves pho-
torealistic quality, its implicit nature results in slow train-
ing and rendering; subsequent approaches accelerate it us-
ing voxel grids [4] or multi-resolution hash encodings [14].
Most notably, Kerbl et al. introduced 3D Gaussian Splatting
(3DGS) [9], representing the scene as a set of anisotropic
3D Gaussians rasterized onto the image plane. 3DGS com-
bines state-of-the-art quality with real-time performance.
All these methods rely heavily on multi-view consistent in-
puts to avoid floating artifacts and optimize towards correct
ground-truth 3D geometry using differentiable rendering.
2.2. Robustness in 3D Reconstruction
A core challenge in photogrammetry is handling data that
violates multi-view consistency.
Pioneering work like
NeRF-W [12] introduced transient embeddings to handle
photometric variations and moving objects (e.g., pedestri-
ans) in unconstrained photo collections. In contrast, Ro-
bustNeRF [17] employs robust loss functions to ignore pix-
els that do not adhere to epipolar geometry. Recent works
adapt these ideas to 3DGS: SpotlessSplats [16] and Wild-
GS [27] use feature-based masking and robust losses to
prune Gaussians associated with transient objects, and De-
2

Splat [26] explicitly separates “distractor” Gaussians from
scene Gaussians. A complementary line of work targets
structural floaters and directly optimizes distractors. Some
approaches eliminate floating artifacts by prioritizing low-
frequency optimization [25], while others introduce robust
mechanisms to synthesize novel views in the presence of
unconstrained visual distractors [23]. Finally, some meth-
ods model camera-specific degradations such as rolling
shutter or motion blur [19], and RestorGS [15] extends
3DGS to degraded scenes (haze, underwater) by decoupling
“clear” and “degraded” Gaussians.
Crucially, all of these methods target world-space
transients, structural optimization errors, moving objects,
or volumetric degradations—phenomena that vary in 3D
space.
In contrast, our problem formulation addresses
screen-space artifacts that are static relative to the camera
but effectively “move” relative to the world as the camera
trajectory changes.
Standard robust estimators often fail
to reject these, leading to floaters baked into the 3D scene.
Modeling occlusions attached to the sensor plane, therefore,
requires a fundamental departure from world-space geome-
try modeling.
2.3. Single-Image and Multi-View Restoration
Removing unwanted occlusions is a long-standing problem
in image processing. Significant research has focused on re-
moving rain, dirt, and reflections from single images using
deep learning: Eigen et al. [3] train CNNs to remove dirt
and rain, others utilize attention mechanisms [11] or syn-
thetic physics-based datasets [6] to hallucinate clean back-
grounds behind raindrops, and single-image reflection re-
moval [24, 31] separates background and reflection layers.
State-of-the-art inpainters such as LaMa [21] and super-
vised watermark-removal models [7, 20] hallucinate miss-
ing textures, but require training a separate model on large
paired datasets. While powerful, these 2D methods lack
knowledge of 3D scene structure, so applying them inde-
pendently to NVS input frames as a pre-processing step re-
sults in temporal flickering and geometric inconsistencies
that break multi-view stereo assumptions.
When multiple views are available, geometric con-
straints can facilitate separation. Early works utilized stereo
disparity to detect and remove adherent water drops [22]—
exploiting the fixed disparity of the noise layer between
stereo images—or exploited the motion difference between
layers to separate reflections in video sequences [5]. Multi-
view inpainting within 3DGS also exists, but typically
requires manually annotating the objects to be removed,
which usually lie in the scene [8, 30]. Our method shares
the spirit of these multi-view approaches—leveraging mo-
tion parallax to distinguish signal from noise—but inte-
grates this logic directly into the 3DGS optimization, using
the 3D reconstruction task itself as the supervision signal to
achieve simultaneous separation and scene recovery with-
out 2D pre-processing, paired training data, or ground-truth
masks.
3. Methodology
Our goal is to reconstruct a high-fidelity 3D scene from a
set of images I = {I1, . . . , IN} captured from known poses
P1, . . . , PN. We assume that the input images are corrupted
by static screen-space artifacts that do not conform to the
scene’s epipolar geometry. Our approach is to jointly op-
timize, thereby separating, the explicit, clean 3D geometry
of the scene from a view-independent 2D field representing
screen-space artifacts.
3.1. Preliminaries: 3D Gaussian Splatting
We adopt 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [9] as our under-
lying scene representation. The scene is modeled as a set
of 3D Gaussians, where each Gaussian Gk is defined by a
center position µk ∈R3, a covariance matrix Σk, an opacity
αk ∈[0, 1], and view-dependent color coefficients ck rep-
resented via Spherical Harmonics. For a given viewpoint,
these 3D Gaussians are projected onto the 2D image plane.

Our formulation is agnostic to this choice, and we evaluate
with both strategies (Sec. 4.2).
3.2. Modeling screen-space artifacts
Standard 3DGS assumes that ˆC3D(u) should directly match
the ground-truth image Igt(u). However, in the presence of
screen-space artifacts, this assumption forces the optimiza-
tion to generate “floaters”—3D Gaussians placed arbitrarily
close to the camera center to mimic the artifact—resulting
in overfitting to the training images. To resolve this, we in-
troduce a screen-space overlay model that defines the ar-
tifact as a learnable 2D tensor shared across all N views in
the dataset. This model consists of two components:

whether it is better explained by consistent 3D geometry or
the static 2D overlay. The clean render ˆC3D—the 3D scene
rendered without the overlay—is the artifact-free output we
ultimately care about: at test time we discard the overlay
and render ˆC3D from novel views.
3.3. Optimization and Regularization
Minimizing the reconstruction error between ˆC and Igt is
insufficient, because the decomposition in Eq. (2) is under-
constrained: the loss only sees the composited color ˆC, so
many ( ˆC3D, A, W) triples reproduce the same image. Two
degenerate solutions are particularly harmful. First, pixels
that are relatively static across views, or that depict high-
frequency, view-dependent effects that 3DGS struggles to
fit, can be erroneously baked into the 2D overlay.
Sec-
ond, the overlay can collapse into a semi-transparent, spa-
tially uniform exposure layer: the 3D scene is reconstructed
too bright, and a low-opacity dark overlay dims it back to
the correct intensity. Such an overlay contains no genuine
artifact—it merely rescales the exposure of an otherwise
clean render—yet is invisible to the photometric loss. To
suppress these solutions and ensure the overlay captures
only true screen-space artifacts, we introduce a composite
loss function that prioritizes explaining pixel data through
the 3D scene structure over the 2D overlay and that reduces
high-frequency errors in the 2D overlay. The loss is defined

4. Evaluation
We evaluate SSA-3DGS on both diverse synthetic scenar-
ios derived from standard real-world datasets and on a self-
captured real-world dataset. The aim is to answer three key
questions: (1) Robustness: Can we consistently recover
high-fidelity 3D geometry from multiple types of heavily
corrupted inputs? (2) Disentanglement: Does the joint op-
timization successfully separate the clean 3D scene from
the overlay? (3) Regularization: Are the proposed sparsity
and total-variation regularizers necessary?
4.1. Implementation details
We implemented our method on top of gsplat [28], using
the default 3DGS hyperparameters. Our method is agnostic
to the densification strategy: we report results for both the
default Adaptive Density Control (ADC) [9] and MCMC
densification [10].
For MCMC, we use the same maxi-
mum number of splats as the original configurations. ADC
serves as our primary setting for the cross-method compar-
ison for two reasons: DeSplat builds on a variant of ADC,

and MCMC occasionally diverges to meaningless recon-
structions when vanilla 3DGS is trained on corrupted inputs
(Sec. 4.6), which would unfairly penalize the baseline.
Instead of initializing the overlay from constants, we
seed it from per-pixel training-set statistics: for each pixel
u we compute the mean color µ(u) and the max-over-
channels standard deviation σ(u) across all training images,
and set W(u) = µ(u) and A(u) = exp(−σ(u)/s) with
s = 5 × 10−2. Pixels whose intensity is stable across views
(possible artifacts) therefore start nearly opaque, while pix-
els with strong multi-view variation (likely scene content)
start near-transparent. Both maps are then optimized us-
ing Adam with a fixed learning rate of 5 × 10−3. We set
λssim = 0.2 and, when enabled, λtv = 1 × 10−2. The
sparsity weight is λsparse = 5 × 10−2 in all experiments
except the mud experiments (the synthetic mud category
and the real mud scene), where we lower it to 1 × 10−2
as mud typically covers larger fractions of the frame at near
opaque alpha values. All remaining settings are identical
across all experiments on both the synthetic and the real-
world dataset.
4.2. Experimental setup
We evaluate on two complementary datasets: a synthetic
benchmark built on Mip-NeRF 360, where we compos-
ite controlled artifacts onto otherwise clean, multi-view-
consistent captures (Sec. 4.3), and a self-captured real-
world dataset, where the artifacts are physically induced
(Sec. 4.4). The synthetic benchmark provides exact, clean
ground truth for every corrupted view on an established
benchmark dataset. It lets us isolate the effect of each ar-
tifact category, while the real-world data tests whether the
method transfers to genuine, physically induced obstruc-
tions. We report three standard reconstruction metrics: Peak
Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity Index
(SSIM), and FLIP [1].
We prefer FLIP to the learned
LPIPS metric [29] because it is explicitly designed to model
the differences a human observer perceives when alternat-
ing between a rendered image and its reference without de-
pending on features learned from a particular image distri-
bution. In all experiments, metrics are computed between
the held-out clean test views and our clean render ˆC3D
(Eq. (2)), i.e., the 3D scene rendered without the learned
overlay, thereby measuring the true restoration quality of
the underlying geometry.
4.3. Synthetic benchmark
We build our synthetic benchmark on the seven publicly re-
leased scenes of the Mip-NeRF 360 [2] dataset, an estab-
lished benchmark in the NVS research community provid-
ing complex, unbounded indoor and outdoor 3D scenes with
ground-truth camera poses. Following the established Mip-
NeRF protocol, we downscale the images by a factor of 2
(and the outdoor scenes by 4) and construct the validation
set by uniformly sampling every 8th image. Because these
captures are clean and multi-view consistent, we composite
synthetic artifacts onto the training views, leaving the held-
out test views untouched to serve as clean ground truth.
Synthetic defect simulation. To strictly control the evalu-
ation environment, we use the albumentations library
to generate the synthetic artifacts. We simulate a range of
screen-space artifacts:
1. Watermark: a semi-transparent logo overlay (α = 0.4)
typical of stock footage.
2. UI Elements: synthetic buttons/HUD elements drawn
on the frame periphery to mimic video-game or screen-
recording captures.
3. Dashboard: a fixed, alpha-matted PNG asset compos-
ited over the lower portion of every frame to mimic dash-
cam footage.
4. Rain spots / Mud: environmental contamination of the
lens enclosure, generated with the Spatter transform
(intensity = 0.6, Gaussian σ = 4) in its rain and mud
modes, respectively.
To maintain temporal consistency of the artifacts relative to
the sensor frame, we generate the augmentation parameters
once for the first frame and use the A.ReplayCompose
mechanism to apply the same artifact to all other training
frames. We do not include sensor-level defects such as dead
pixels: they occupy isolated pixels of such high frequency
that the multi-view averaging of vanilla 3DGS already sup-
presses them, so the failure mode this paper targets does not
arise.
4.4. Real-world dataset
A central concern with synthetic corruptions is whether
the method transfers to genuine, physically induced arti-
facts. To address this, we additionally captured a real-world
dataset. Unlike the synthetic benchmark, where artifacts
are digitally composited, here they are produced by physi-
cal obstructions. The dataset comprises two scenes that test
two common sources of screen-space artifacts (Fig. 4):
1. Mud scene (pure screen-space artifact).
We smear
mud onto the transparent protective housing of an action
camera and record the scene through the dirtied enclo-
sure. Because the mud is rigidly fixed to the housing, it
is a pure screen-space artifact—the real-world analog of
our synthetic mud/spatter category, and a common, real-
istic failure case for action cameras and outdoor robotics.
2. Occlusion scene (near-static artifact). The lower part
of the camera recording rig is accidentally visible in the
field of view in every frame. Critically, because this oc-
cluder sits outside the camera enclosure, it is illuminated
by, and reflects, the surrounding environment. Its ap-
pearance, therefore, varies slightly from view to view
as the camera moves. This scene deliberately probes a

4.5. Baselines
We compare SSA-3DGS against the following baselines:
• Baseline 3DGS: vanilla 3DGS [9] trained directly on the
corrupted images, representing the behavior of current re-
construction pipelines when fed imperfect data.
• DeSplat [26]: a 3DGS variant that explicitly separates
near-camera “distractor” Gaussians from scene Gaus-

We compare against DeSplat because,
among the
distractor-robust methods discussed in Sec. 2, it is both the

Visual inspection of Fig. 3 confirms the quantitative re-

screen-space artifacts into the scene as semi-transparent,
near-camera clouds that shadow the true geometry.

4.8. Ablation study
To validate our design choices, we ablate the two regular-
ization terms; Tab. 3 reports mean scores over the five syn-
thetic artifact categories and the two real scenes (all runs use
ADC densification). Adding Lsparse improves mean PSNR
by 0.43 dB, and adding LT V a further 0.51 dB, while FLIP
improves from 0.207 to 0.180. The two terms play com-
plementary roles. Sparsity drives the alpha matte toward
zero wherever an artifact is not needed, which both keeps
genuine scene content out of the overlay and collapses the
degenerate exposure layer described in Sec. 3.3, where a
low-opacity dark overlay might dim an over-bright recon-
struction. Total variation suppresses high-frequency residue
that the overlay would otherwise absorb; because it couples
each pixel to its neighbors instead of leaving the overlay to
be fit independently per pixel, it also propagates gradients
across local neighborhoods and stabilizes the optimization.

5. Limitations
Our method relies on multi-view consensus to separate the
static overlay from the scene. A sufficiently dense capture
is thus required to avoid 3DGS from explaining artifacts
through view-dependent effects that are not penalized by
overlapping views. Our overlay model also assumes that
the artifact is fixed in screen space. Artifacts that are only
approximately static—such as the rig occluder in our occlu-
sion scene, whose reflections change with viewpoint—are
only partially captured by the overlay. While SSA-3DGS
still improves substantially over the baseline (Tab. 2), fully
view-dependent occluders remain an open problem.
6. Conclusion
We have presented SSA-3DGS, a robust framework that ex-
tends 3D Gaussian Splatting to handle input imagery cor-
rupted by static screen-space artifacts. By explicitly mod-
eling the image formation process as a composition of a
world-space 3D scene and a view-independent image-space
2D overlay, we cast reconstruction as a dual-objective joint
optimization that simultaneously disentangles sensor-plane
obstructions and reconstructs clean 3D geometry with-
out supervision.
Our approach restores high-fidelity 3D
scenes from heavily corrupted inputs, ranging from syn-
thetic watermarks to naturally occurring environmental ef-
fects and genuine, self-captured real-world artifacts. It also
serves as a potent multi-view image restoration tool that,
unlike generic 2D denoising applied as a per-frame pre-
processing step, preserves multi-view consistency. These
results suggest that explicitly modeling capture imperfec-
tions is a viable path toward reliable 3D reconstruction on
unconstrained, “in-the-wild” captures, significantly reduc-
ing the reliance on laborious manual cleaning or ground-
truth masks.
