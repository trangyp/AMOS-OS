ABSTRACT
Context. Integral Field Spectroscopy (IFS) is a key technique to study galaxies, stellar and planetary systems, and transients, by
spatially splitting the scene and dispersing this light onto the detector. This technique is now central for many modern telescopes,
including the Lazuli Space Observatory which will host a low spectral-resolution image slicer spectrograph.
Aims. To prepare for future IFS-equipped instruments and to better understand the systematics of current ones, realistic simulations
are needed.
Methods. We present slicersim, a Python package designed to simulate IFS observations. slicersim is a modular and flexible
tool that allows the user to build a scene, define a telescope, a spectrograph, and a detector, and to simulate the resulting observation,
including various sources of noise and instrumental effects.
Results. The package is designed to be easily extensible to any IFS, with a current focus on that from the Lazuli Space Observatory.
In this paper, we present the structure and logic of the package, detailing its main components, and providing an example of its usage.
Conclusions. Using slicersim we demonstrate the feasibility of a large cosmology campaign on Lazuli — a sample of 8000 Type Ia
Supernovae uniformly distributed between z = 0 and z = 1.5, observed within the first few years of its operations for a typical average
signal-to-noise of 25 per resolution element rest-frame optical wavelengths.
Key words. Lazuli; IFS; Type Ia Supernovae
1. Introduction
For decades, the study of astrophysical transients, such as su-
pernovae (SNe), has been based on the combination of an initial
discovery emerging from a photometric image survey, enriched
by follow-up data, including more precise photometric data and
spectroscopic information. The latter has been particularly im-
portant in analyzing those transients, either to (sub)classify them
(e.g., Filippenko 1997; Folatelli et al. 2013; Dimitriadis et al.
2025) or to study their properties and those of their progenitor
systems (e.g., Blondin et al. 2012; Silverman et al. 2012; Gal-
Yam et al. 2014; Maguire et al. 2014; Burgaz et al. 2025).
In the last few decades, photometric discovery rates of SNe
have surpassed the spectroscopic follow-up capabilities (see dis-
cussion in Kulkarni 2020). This led the Dark Energy Survey
(DES, DES Collaboration et al. 2024) perform the first state-
of-the-art Type Ia supernova cosmology analysis using a sam-
ple of O(1, 000) Type Ia Supernovae (SNe Ia) that were classi-
fied exclusively using photometry to probe cosmological param-
eters (Möller & de Boissière 2020; Boone 2021). However, it
is now known that the right spectroscopic information can pro-
vide higher precision (lower residual scatter) and higher accu-

The advent of wide-field imaging surveys such as the Zwicky
Transient Facility (ZTF, Bellm et al. 2019; Graham et al. 2019),
the Vera C. Rubin Observatory’s Legacy Survey of Space and

developments in Section 4.

2. Package Structure and Logic
To simulate realistic IFS observations, one has to model the com-
plex interplay between the astrophysical scene of interest, the
telescope, the spectrograph, and the detector. Consequently, the
slicersim package is built around these four components: the
Scene, the Telescope, the Spectrograph, and the Detector.
These components are brought together in the Simulation
class, which orchestrates the simulation of an observation. We
will review each component in the following sub-sections. The
code infrastructure is illustrated in Fig. 1.
For convenience, a suite of Target classes has been imple-
mented to simplify user interaction with Simulations through
high-level methods, such as building a specific scene (i.e., a
Type Ia Supernova or a CALSPEC standard star), accessing
the exposure time calculator (ETC), probing and visualizing
the variance contributions. Functionalities or features specific
to an instrument are developed as dedicated target subclasses.
For instance, the current version of slicersim is released with
LazuliTargets, which inherit from the generic Target, but
has, in addition, tools specific to Lazuli, such as the management
of the two spatial channels of this IFS, or high-level functionali-
ties related to the IFS detector (see Section 2.6.3).
2.1. Scene
Scene is the class that simulates the astrophysical observation as
given by nature. It is itself a container that relies on three lower-
level elements (python classes): the PointSource, a spatial
point source with sky location and a spectrum; the Background,
a spatially uniform spectral source; and the Host, a spatially
structured background with (potentially) spatially varying spec-
trum.
In the way slicersim is built, each of these elements
uses methods to define their spectra. These functions take a
wavelength as required argument and may have additional key-
arguments. For example, a SN Ia PointSource model can be
generated using the Spectral Adaptive Lightcurve Template, aka
SALT, model (Guy et al. 2007, 2010; Betoule et al. 2014; Ken-
worthy et al. 2021), and hence the function accepts in addition
to wavelength, a phase, a redshift, a stretch, a color, and an am-
plitude.
In version 1.0, slicersim has the following built-in ele-
ments:
– PointSource: For any input spectrum (wavelength and
flux), slicersim will build an interpolator to enable a sim-
ulation of the IFS spectrum as observed. It could also be a
SN Ia (either SALT or twin-embedding, Boone 2021; Ganot
et al. 2025); any sncosmo source (Barbary et al.
2023),
which includes a large selection of core collapse supernovae
templates; a black-body spectrum; any CALSPEC source.
– Background: slicersim considers the zodiacal light, using
the Aldering (2001) model as implemented by Euclid (see
section 5.1.1 of Euclid Collaboration et al. 2022).
– Host: Currently, the sole host galaxy implementation under
development in slicersim is a simple Sersic profile with
spatially uniform spectrum. Future implementation will con-
tain more realistic models and shall accept any 3D galaxy
cube (RA, Dec, λ), e.g., as given by GalSim (Rowe et al.
2015).
As documented in the corresponding functions and methods,
fluxes are expected to be given in erg/s/cm2/A and wavelengths
in Angstrom (Å). More functionalities are in development, such
as based on zero-points, see online documentation for up to date
information.
The Scene class has these three elements as attributes and
has methods to generate the spectro-spatial scene as given by
nature, i.e., prior to being affected by the point-spread function
(driven by the telescope optics in the case of a space telescope).
A Scene instance needs an input wavelength array to generate
the scene spectra. This is provided through the Simulation ob-
ject, which grabs it from its spectrograph attribute (see Sec-
tion 2.6).

2.3. Spectrograph
The Spectrograph (the IFS instrument) is a central compo-
nent of the simulation. It defines how the sky is split into sub-
spatial elements and how the wavelengths are dispersed. The
“slicer” spectrograph design is fully supported by slicersim
(SlicerSpectrograph). In addition, a micro-lens array (MLA)
design has also been developed (MLASpectrograph), mostly for
testing and comparison purposes, though not all functionalities
are compatible with this extra design (e.g., the projection onto
the detector, see Section 2.6.3). Both designs inherit from the
same Spectrograph class and vary only by how the light is
spatially split and the need of a cross-dispersion profile for MLA
spectrographs. The Lazuli IFS spectrograph uses a slicer.
A Spectrograph needs three inputs: a (chromatic) spectral
resolution (e.g. the resolving power, R, or equivalent), a (chro-
matic) throughput, and “spaxel” configurations. It also accepts
settings for the thermal properties of optical elements, which are
assumed to be null if not otherwise specified.
As illustrated in Fig. 1, a Telescope instance is (optionally)
passed to the Spectrograph to simplify the code, as it holds
the information about the spatial PSF entering the spectrograph
(including the jitter), which is used to generate noiseless cubes;
see Section 2.6.1.

2.3.2. Spaxels
A “spaxel” is the spatial element on the sky as seen by the spec-
trograph (Cappellari & Copin 2003). The decomposition into
spaxels happens in two steps for a slicer. First, n mirrors split the
sky in one direction, defining n rectangular “1D” sub-images.
Its narrow dimension defines the width of the slicer spaxel. The
long one of the slice is sampled by the detector. To ensure equal
sampling of the slit and the spatial direction at the detector, it
is the norm for a slicer to have an anamorphic magnification of
2:1, such that the PSF is sampled the same by the slicer mirrors
as it is by the detector pixels along the spatial direction. Indeed,
the narrow dimension (the width of the slice) contains 2 detector
pixels to sample the line spread function.

2.4. Detector
The Detector sets how the data is recorded and consequently
how the signal variance is built. The Spectrograph models how
the incident irradiation (scene + telescope thermal radiation) is
dispersed onto the detector and Detector computes how each
pixel reacts and records this incoming flux. The Lazuli spectro-
graph will use a Teledyne H4RG-10 detector, which generates
signal ramps from non-destructive read-outs. In the released ver-
sion of slicersim, only HxRG-like CMOS detectors have been
implemented that feature non-destructive, sample-up-the-ramp
(SUTR) read modes; CCD-like detectors are on the list of future
implementations.
An (HxRG) Detector is defined by: its pixel size
(pixel_size), the array shape (shape), the time between two
frame reads (tframe), a dark current (dark), a per frame read-
out noise (ron), a (wavelength dependent) quantum efficiency
(qe), a gain (gain), a saturation level (saturation) and a read-
out mode (aka MACC mode) nmd specifying “n” groups, “m”
frames per group, and “d” drops between groups. We also in-
clude the term roic_glow to account for glow from the read-
out integrated circuit (ROIC) due to the in-pixel source-follower
(Regan & Bergeron 2020; Ives et al. 2020), which acts like a
per-frame-read dark current (e−/frame; see parameter units in
Table 1).
A “ramp” is defined between two detector resets. In
slicersim one can set a maximum number of groups allowed
per ramp, such that the multi-ramps mode is used for faint tar-
gets that would need more acquisition time than that allowed by
this maximum number of groups. Alternatively, this mode could
be used to acquire time series of integrations.
Given the use of nmd-ramp to decrease the effective read
noise, the variance of each pixel could be computed either us-
ing the Rauscher et al. (2007) model (as updated by Rauscher
(2010), default) or by that from Kubik et al. (2016) (as updated
by Euclid Collaboration et al. 2026). Both agree within a few
percent.
More complex detector effects that affect variance, such as
pixel correlations (e.g. inter-pixel capacitance) are ignored in the
current version. A detailed detector simulator (pyxim, Lacroix
et al. in prep) is under development and eventually will be inter-
faced with slicersim.

For the Lazuli IFS, we have two sources of thermal emission:
i) the telescope and the spectrograph optics before the disperser,
and ii) the IFS optics between the disperser (including the prism
itself) and the detector. The former is dispersed and thus effec-
tively is similar to a “background” scene signal, while the latter
is undispersed, and thus acts more like a detector dark. Conse-
quently, to compute the impact of this undispersed thermal emis-
sion, we include the wavelength-dependent Detector.qe to get
this effective pixel dark in electrons. For the telescope mirror and
instrumental optics prior the IFU, Ωis given by the spaxel sky
area and s is the primary mirror surface area. For optics within
the spectrograph, the solid angle is given by the optics’ f-ratio
(and shape, e.g., circular or rectangle) and s by the physical de-
tector pixel size.

2.6.1. Generating a realistic cube
The core purpose of Simulation is to generate a realistic cube
(get_cube()), which returns both the cube “as observed” and
the variance cube.
This cube generation works in two steps. First, a noise-
less cube is generated (get_projected_scene()). To do
so, the Scene spectra (1D array for PointSource and
Background, a 3D-array for Host) is generated given the
Spectrograph’s wavelength and is then convolved by the ef-
fective “spatial+jitter” PSF from the Telescope; in current the
implementation of slicersim the diffraction induced by the slit
width itself is ignored. Once the cube is generated, the effective
Spectrograph’s LSF is applied along the wavelength direction.
In slicersim v1.0.0 all spectra have the same LSF, which
effectively means that the spectrograph-induced PSF is uniform
across the detector. A more realistic behavior is currently under
development and will be included in a near future release.
Second, we generate the variance cube associated with this
noiseless cube. This step differs for “MLA” and “slicer” IFU
designs within slicersim. In a slicer, the data cube directly
projects onto the detector. This means that each cube element
(flux(i, j, λ)) is projected onto the detector and is thus directly
affected by the detector noise as described in section 2.4.
For an MLA-based IFU, one has to first compute the
wavelength-dependent cross-dispersion profile to estimate how
many detector pixel a flux(i, j, λ) cube element is effectively
projected onto. The current release assumes a Gaussian cross-
dispersion profile, whose width is linearly increasing with wave-
length. The cube “as observed” is drawn from the noiseless cube
given the variance cube assuming Gaussian noise.

that the PSF appear elongated along the slice length (x-axis di-

2.6.2. Extracting the target spectrum
The target spectrum and its variance is usually the final prod-
uct of interest to a the user. These are accessible using the
Simulation.get_spectrum() method.
Once the realistic cube and its associated variance cube have
been generated (see Section 2.6.1), the expected variance spec-
trum is extracted. In slicersim 1.0 this extraction is optimal:
we use the same PSF to generate the cubes and to estimate the
variance spectrum (from the variance cube). We then take the
spectrum from the Scene (so not from the realistic cube), which
is then randomly sampled given the variance spectrum assuming
Gaussian noise. To study biases, one would need to extract the
spectrum for the realistic cube after perturbing it by the biases
of interest. This is a planned development beyond the released
version 1.0.

2.6.3. Generating the detector image
One does not need to project data onto the detector to precisely
estimate the expected signal variance and, thereby, build a real-
istic signal-to-noise estimator. However, having the possibility
to do so is convenient for visualizing how the data may actually
look and for building the inference pipeline that will start from
real data to measure the input flux.
To build the expected detector image, slicersim requires
external input — often called an “optical model” — to build the
mapping function. This function converts (x, y, λ) coordinates at
the slicer (or MLA) plane into (i, j) coordinates at the detector
plane; x, y being the sky-coordinates relative to the center of
the field of view. In practice, slicersim only needs a few sam-
ples per slice (lenslet element) and performs a 2D interpolation.
Once this model is built, the package can also provide the in-
verse function (i, j) to (x, y, λ). This mapping is handled by the
SlicerMapper objects.
This mapping is illustrated in Fig. 4 for Lazuli. This obser-
vatory IFS is designed to have two observing fields: a narrow
and a wide field. To illustrate the point-source scenario, this fig-
ure shows a pure “point-source” cube (no host galaxy, no back-
ground) and the instrumental aberrations have been turned off to
clearly see the incoming PSF. Since Lazuli is a slicer spectro-
graph, slices are located side by side on the detector. The narrow
(wide) field projects slices on the top (bottom) half of the detec-
tor, with slices located at the center of the field of view, near #30
(#90) are located near the left edge of the detector. For each slice,
the dispersion goes downward with increasing wavelengths. This
figure further illustrates the resolving power of the spectrograph,
that is much higher in bluer wavelength (see also Fig. 5. In this
figure this is visible as blue wavelengths span a much large num-
ber of pixels for a fixed wavelength binning than red ones.
Such external data may be derived from a Zemax optical
model (as for Fig. 4). The optical model itself will likely be re-
fined with on-sky calibration.

3.1. Changing internal parameters
Each internal slicersim object, i.e., Simulation and below
following the code structure illustrated Fig. 1, has an .update
method. This method enables the user to self-consistently change
any “mutable parameters” of these objects. Most parameters are
mutable, e.g., the read-out noise, read-out mode, dark current
from the detector, the temperature and the size of the telescope
mirrors, and the dispersion resolution or the spaxel size of the
spectrograph; see the .mutable_parameter object attribute.
Some attributes are derived parameters, such as the through-
put (.throughput) or the wavelength (.lbda) for the spectro-
graph. They could either be changed using their root parame-
ters (such as noptics for the throughput) or directly by dedi-
cated setting methods, such as set_throughput. These meth-
ods enable slicersim to handle more complex configuration
updates than just changing a number — the primary usage of the
.update method.
The slicersim package has been designed such that, if the
user changes a parameter, the code will remain self-consistent.

3.2. Exposure times and signal-to-noise ratios
The .update method (see Section 3.1) enables one to change the
detector.nmd read-out mode, which effectively corresponds
to changing the exposure time and consequently the signal to
noise of the simulated spectrum (see Section 2.6.2). One can
then loop over the read-out mode configuration until one reaches
the desired mean (or max, or median etc.) signal to noise in the
wavelengh range of interest; be it rest- or observer-frame. The
simulation.get_band_snr() method simplifies the compu-
tation of the signal to noise of interest for the current configura-
tion, while the simulation.fetch_snr() fits for the read-out
mode. These methods are illustrated in Fig. 6 for a z = 1 Type Ia
supernova observed with Lazuli. Alternatively, one can directly
set the read-out mode configuration (nmd and nramps) using the
target.change_detector method.
By default the Detector has a maximum number of 64
groups per frame and 8 frames per group; both mutable param-
eters. By default, simulation.fetch_snr() loops over the
number of groups. If, during the fitting loop the maximum num-
ber of groups allowed has been reached and the signal-to-noise
is still lower than requested, the code will freeze the maximum
read-out mode configuration (e.g., nmd=(64, 8, 0)) and then
iterate over the number of ramps. Once this has converged, if the
signal to noise is too high, the number of ramps gets frozen and
the number of groups per ramp is fitted; hence, in the current im-
plementation of slicersim, all ramps share the same number
of groups. Conversely, a minimum number of groups could be
specified, and, upon fitting the best configuration the number of
frames per group, will reduce to 4, then 1 (see option), for bright
targets.

the

The Target object uses this Simulator method inside
its high-level target.setup_to_snr() method, which first
fits for the optimal detector configuration (nmd, nramps) and
then sets it using simulation.update(). Finally, one can
simply use the target.get_exposure_time() method (or
simulation.get_times()) to obtain the total exposure time.
This is how using slicersim as an exposure time calculator
works.
3.3. Probing the sources of variance.
Taking full advantage of the flexibility of slicersim, one can
use the simulation.update() method to: (1) switch off any
variance source, (2) compute the expected spectrum variance
(simulation.get_spectrum()), and (3) compare it with that
obtained with the variance source on. This functionality is used
inside of the simulation.get_variance_contribution()
method that scans all variance sources this way.
There are currently eight variance sources implemented
in slicersim: the detector dark, read-out noise (ron) and
ROIC glow (roic_glow); the thermal emission from ele-
ments within the spectrograph after the disperser (undispersed,
thermal_dark); the thermal emission from the telescope and
the spectrograph prior to the disperser (dispersed, thermal); and
the photon noise from the three scene elements (pointsource,
background and host).
We illustrate one such variance contribution decomposition
in Fig. 7 for a z = 1 host-less SN Ia observed with Lazuli’s spec-
trograph with three nmd = (64, 8, 0) ramps (as for Fig. 6, Sec-
tion 3.2). As expected, the relative contribution of each source of
variance varies with wavelength. In this example, photon noise
from the target (pointsource) dominates the variance contribu-
tion where the flux peaks (between 8000 and 1400 nm observer-
frame). For the Lazuli telescope, the thermal contribution from
the mirrors is only visible in the reddest wavelengths but remains
small in comparison to other detector effects (dark, roic_glow
and ron). Given that the IFS optics are all cold (∼230K) the in-
ternal thermal emission (thermal_dark) is also low. This nicely
illustrates the use of slicersim to help with the conceptual de-
sign of an instrument.

4. Conclusion and Discussion
We have presented slicersim, a new Python package for sim-
ulating IFS slicer observations. This package is a modular and
flexible tool that allows the user to build realistic simulations.
It has been developed to support the development of the Lazuli
Space Observatory slicer (Roy, et al. 2026), but the code struc-
ture has been designed to be flexible and extensions could be
made for any IFS.
The main components of the package are the Scene, the
Telescope, the Spectrograph, and the Detector. These
components are brought together in the Simulation class,
which orchestrates the simulation of an observation. A top level
class Target is made to simplify the user experience in inter-
acting with the Simulation, and a set of LazuliTargets are
provided that automatically loads the default Lazuli properties.
In this paper, we have detailed each of these components and
provided an example of how to use slicersim as an exposure
time calculator or to investigate the origin of the variance of a
given observation. We have also listed nominal values for key
characteristics of the expected Lazuli IFS and illustrated the use
of slicersim in studying how such an instrument could be used
to execute a Type Ia Supernova campaign (see Perlmutter et al.
in prep, Roy, et al. 2026 and Wevers et al. 2026).
