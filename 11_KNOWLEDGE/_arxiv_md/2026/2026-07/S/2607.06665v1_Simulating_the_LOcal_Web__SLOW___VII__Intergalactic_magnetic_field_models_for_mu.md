ABSTRACT
Context. The propagation of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays (UHECRs) and ultra-high-energy gamma-rays remains an open question
in astroparticle physics, with the intergalactic magnetic field (IGMF) playing a crucial role in deflecting charged particles and shaping
electromagnetic cascade spectra. Characterizing the IGMF across cosmic large-scale structure is therefore essential for interpreting
multi-messenger observations and constraining the magnetogenesis scenarios that seeded it.
Aims. We aim to provide accurate IGMF models to the astroparticle physics community and test their properties and robustness.
Methods. We analyze IGMF models derived from the constrained cosmological simulation SLOW alongside a set of rescaled magnetic
field models. We further introduce a novel algorithm to determine an “ideal position” for galaxies lying below the constraining power
of the initial conditions, enabling accurate line-of-sight magnetic field extraction toward relevant sources.
Results. The models span a wide range of filling factors and sample distinct regions of the electron density–magnetic field strength
phase space in filaments, while converging in the cores of galaxy clusters; the simulated field from SLOW best reproduces the IGMF
derived from the electromagnetic 𝛾-ray cascade. Models extracted using the introduced “ideal position” yield improved accuracy and
may benefit multi-messenger studies more broadly. The large-scale structure drift of simulated clusters exploited by the algorithm also
offers a potential route to refining the simulation’s constrained initial conditions.
Key words. Methods: numerical – Astroparticle physics – Magnetic fields – Cosmology: large-scale structure of Universe
1. Introduction
The origin and nature of the intergalactic magnetic field (IGMF)
remain some of the outstanding open questions in modern as-
trophysics. Its strength and topology affect the propagation of
ultra-high-energy cosmic rays (UHECR, 𝐸≥1018 eV, Dolag
et al. 2005; Hackstein et al. 2018) and TeV gamma rays (e.g.
Neronov & Semikoz 2009; Dolag et al. 2009, 2011), making
multi-messenger observations a powerful probe of magnetogene-
sis in the large-scale structure of the Universe (Durrer & Neronov
2013). Accurately modeling the IGMF along the line-of-sight to
relevant astrophysical sources is therefore a critical ingredient in
interpreting these observations.
Cosmological magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) simulations
offer a physically motivated approach to constructing such mod-
els. Pioneering efforts by Sigl et al. (2004) first estimated UHECR
deflections through the simulated IGMF, finding large deflection
angles. Later Dolag et al. (2005) introduced the important im-
provement of constrained initial conditions tied to the observed

local large-scale structure, and consequently found deflections to
be generally small outside of galaxy clusters. The benefit of using
constrained simulations is in using these predictions in UHECR
applications, for example, for backtracing real-world observed
cosmic rays to their sources.
A promising field to probe the nature of magnetogenesis is
the magnetic field in voids. The electromagnetic 𝛾-ray cascade
(Neronov & Semikoz 2009) allows for deriving a lower limit
on the IGMF strength along the line-of-sight to blazars (e.g.
D’Avezac et al. 2007; Neronov & Vovk 2010). A TeV 𝛾-ray emit-
ted by a blazar will interact with the extragalactic background
light (EBL) to produce an electron-positron pair, which travels
in the same direction as the original 𝛾-ray due to the high energy
of the latter. Subsequently, the charged particles will inversely
Compton scatter with the cosmic microwave background (CMB)
and upscatter photons to GeV. Measuring a lower flux of GeV
photons compared to the flux expected from the cascade the-
ory points to the existence of a magnetic field, which deflects
the charged particles off the line-of-sight (D’Avezac et al. 2007;
Neronov & Semikoz 2009). Most recent (conservative) results
based on this effect are 𝐵> 1.3 × 10−17G (Blunier et al. 2025).

Despite this progress, two recurring shortcomings limit the
accuracy of existing magnetic field models for multi-messenger
studies. First, many simulations employ unconstrained initial con-
ditions, meaning that the simulated large-scale structure does
not reproduce the observed local Universe (e.g. Vazza et al.
2021; Tjemsland et al. 2024). Second, even simulations with
constrained initial conditions are often too small to encom-
pass the distances to many relevant sources without resorting
to techniques such as mirroring the magnetic field profile at
the box boundary (Dolag et al. 2011) or the concatenation of
multiple simulation volumes (Tjemsland et al. 2024), which in-
evitably reduces the fidelity of the resulting magnetic field profile
along the line of sight. Additionally, interesting UHECR/𝛾-ray
sources often are galaxy mass halos that are notoriously hard
to replicate/cross-match even in constrained simulations, as the
constraints are linearly reconstructed and the linear threshold lies
around 3-4 Mpc (Hernández-Martínez et al. 2024; Seidel et al.
2025). Another limitation shared by a broad class of UHECR

2. Methods
2.1. Simulation
The magnetic field models presented in this work are based on the
constrained cosmological simulation SLOW (Simulating the LO-
cal Universe), which was already described in detail by previous
work, which we briefly summarize below:
2.1.1. Initial Conditions
The initial conditions of a constrained cosmological simulation
are not only imprinted with random Gaussian fluctuations but also
include information about the real-world large-scale structure of
the Local Universe. They come in three flavors: Constraints from
galaxy number density, galaxy peculiar velocity measurements,
or hybrid forms. A brief historical overview of different con-
strained Local Universe simulations can be found in Dolag et al.
(2023). The initial conditions for SLOW are based on the ap-
proach using galaxy peculiar velocities, which is described in
detail by Sorce (2018). A summary of the most important steps
in building these initial conditions, as well as a detailed descrip-
tion of the cross-identified structures and clusters, is provided by
Dolag et al. (2023), Hernández-Martínez et al. (2024), and Sei-
del et al. (2025). The observational dataset underlying the initial
conditions is the Cosmicflows-2 catalog (CF2, Tully et al. 2013).
The resulting initial conditions are based on the CLONE (Con-
strained LOcal & Nesting Environment, Sorce et al. 2021, 2024)
simulations (in this case, realization number 8) that are also the
basis of the HESTIA suite of simulations (Libeskind et al. 2020)
and the simulations by Hackstein et al. (2018) used a similar
method to produce their constraints, among others.
2.1.2. The SLOW Simulation
The SLOW suite of simulations includes several versions of var-
ious resolutions and the included subgrid physics (see, Dolag
et al. 2023; Hernández-Martínez et al. 2024). In this work, we
use the SLOW-AGN15363 (also called SLOW-FP15363) simu-
lation for positional data of clusters and galaxies that include a
supermassive black hole (SMBH). More than 50 galaxy clus-
ters have been cross-matched to real-world galaxy clusters so far
(Hernández-Martínez et al. 2024; Seidel et al. 2025). Not only the
large-scale structure (Dolag et al. 2023), but also the morphology
and evolution of individual galaxy clusters, such as the bridge be-
tween A2667 and A3651 (Dietl et al. 2024) or the Fornax cluster
(Reiprich et al. 2025), were shown to match observations closely.
Also investigated was the amount of turbulence in Coma, Virgo,
and Perseus (Groth et al. 2026).
We use the SLOW-CR30723 simulation (Böss et al. 2024) to
extract the IGMF. The latter simulation was run with the MHD
solver (Dolag & Stasyszyn 2009; Bonafede et al. 2011) and the
on-the-fly Fokker-Planck solver CRESCENDO (Böss et al. 2023)
to model CR proton and electron evolution, but it is a pure non-
radiative simulation with no star formation or SMBH. This sim-
ulation was already used to analyze the synchrotron emission
from the simulated electron population and the simulated mag-
netic fields, as well as magnetic field models rescaled to the
electron density, gas pressure, or turbulent pressure (Böss et al.
2024). Böss et al. (2025) also derived expectations for diffuse
𝛾-ray emission from galaxy clusters and the cosmic web as mod-
eled directly from the simulated CR energy density and spectra.
Multi-messenger particles from galaxy clusters have also been
modeled using this simulation and Monte Carlo simulations to
propagate CRs (Hussain et al. 2025).
The two simulations are needed because SLOW-AGN15363
does not include a magnetic field but includes AGN that are
needed to select clusters and galaxies that include an SMBH.
SLOW-CR30723 in turn does not include AGN but includes a
magnetic field. The positions of halos and subhalos, however,
match very closely between the two simulations, as they use the
same initial conditions and can easily be cross-matched.
Both simulations have a volume of (500ℎ−1𝑐Mpc)3. SLOW-
AGN15363 consists of 2×15363 particles leading to a resolution
of 𝑀gas ≈6.8 × 108𝑀⊙and 𝑀DM ≈3.7 × 109𝑀⊙. SLOW-
CR30723 consists of 2 × 30723 particles leading to a resolution
of 𝑀gas ≈8.5×107𝑀⊙and 𝑀DM ≈4.6×108𝑀⊙. The maximum
spatial resolution at 𝑧= 0 are ℎsml,min = 5.97 kpc and ℎsml,min =
3.18 kpc, respectively. The gravitational softening of star and gas
particles at 𝑧= 0 are 𝜖stars ≈2kpc, 𝜖gas ≈8kpc and 𝜖stars ≈1kpc,
𝜖gas ≈4kpc, respectively. The simulations use Planck cosmology
(Planck Collaboration et al. 2014): Ωm = 0.307, Ωbaryon = 0.048,
ΩΛ = 0.692, and 𝐻0 = 67.77 km s−1 Mpc−1.
2.1.3. Simulation Code
All simulations of the SLOW simulation suite were run with
the OpenGadget3 Code (Groth et al. 2023), which is an im-
proved implementation of the cosmological Tree-SPH code Gad-
get2 (Springel 2005). The physics subgrid model of SLOW-
AGN15363 is mostly identical to the Magneticum suite of simu-
lations (Dolag et al. 2025). For a detailed description of the sub-
grid model, see the same reference. The MHD implementation
used for SLOW-CR30723 was presented by Dolag & Stasyszyn
(2009), and non-ideal MHD effects, such as magnetic diffusion
and dissipation, were implemented by Bonafede et al. (2011). Di-
vergence cleaning to enforce ∇· B = 0 is implemented following
Tricco et al. (2016) as presented by Steinwandel & Price (2025).
Halos and subhalos are identified using Subfind (Springel et al.
2001), which employs a standard friends-of-friends algorithm
(Davis et al. 1985) and is modified to include the baryonic com-
ponent (Dolag et al. 2009).

2.2. Magnetic field
SLOW-CR30723 was set up with a primordial magnetic field
(PMF, Kandus et al. 2011) as the seed magnetic field, as opposed
to magnetic field seeding by the outflows of galaxies (“astro-
physical seeding”, e.g., Donnert et al. 2009; Beck et al. 2013;
Vazza et al. 2025; Garg et al. 2025). At the starting redshift of
𝑧= 120, the magnetic field assigned to every gas particle is
B = (10−14, 0, 0) G. This uniform magnetic field with constant
strength and direction throughout the whole simulation box corre-
sponds to an inflationary magnetogenesis scenario (Mukohyama
2016) and is regularly employed in cosmological simulations
(Dubois & Teyssier 2008; Marinacci et al. 2015; Mtchedlidze
et al. 2022; Lehle et al. 2026). Typically, inflationary magneto-
genesis models are able to produce larger coherence lengths, as
they are not limited by causality; however, they can also pro-
duce a stochastic distribution (for a review, Kandus et al. 2011;
Durrer & Neronov 2013). Another scenario for producing PMFs
is cosmological phase transitions, which result in a stochastic
distribution (Durrer & Neronov 2013).
The magnetic field predicted by SLOW was analyzed by Böss
et al. (2024) in absolute terms and with regard to reproducing
diffuse synchrotron emission from cosmic web filaments, specif-
ically around the Coma cluster. In the Coma cluster replica, the
simulated magnetic field 𝐵sim profile matches measurements us-
ing the rotation measure (RM) by Bonafede et al. (2010) well up
to a radius of ∼1Mpc (Böss et al. 2024). Further from the center,
in the filaments, the magnetic field strength drops significantly,
likely due to a lack of resolution to drive turbulent dynamo ampli-
fications (Steinwandel et al. 2022). To reconcile this mismatch,
Böss et al. (2024) adopted several models, rescaling the mag-
netic field strength based on the thermal gas pressure, turbulent
pressure, or electron density.

2.4. Estimating Uncertainty
We estimate the uncertainty of these magnetic field profiles
(along the line of sight) using three different methods and com-
pare them to random lines-of-sight through the simulation box.
All methods have in common that we calculate the magnetic
field along multiple lines-of-sight towards positions offset from
the “ideal” position, but still within a realistic spatial uncertainty
scale 𝜆D = |𝒙D,ideal −𝒙D,shift|, where 𝐷is the dimension (either
3D or 2D), and 𝒙𝐷
shifted, is the shifted centroid (see above).
In the first method (“2D”), we choose 50 random positions
inside a circle in the sky plane around the 2D “ideal” position
with a maximum distance of 3 × 𝜆2D. The distances of these
random positions are drawn from a Gaussian with a standard
deviation of 𝜆2D.
In the second method (“3D”), we choose 50 random positions
inside a sphere around the 3D “ideal” position with a maximum
distance of 3 × 𝜆3D. The distances of these random positions are
drawn from a Gaussian with a standard deviation of 𝜆3D.
In the third method (“subhalos”), we select galaxy mass halos
in a sphere around the 3D “ideal” position with a radius of 3×𝜆3D.
We select halos with a stellar mass of 10 ≤log(𝑀★/𝑀⊙) ≤12
and enforce that they host a supermassive black hole (SMBH),
which is the basic requirement for hosting a blazar (an active
galactic nucleus with a jet pointed towards the observer/earth). As
the MHD simulation (SLOW-CR30723) does not contain black
holes, we cross-match the subhalos of the MHD simulation with
SLOW-AGN15363 that does contain black holes (See Sect. 2.1).
Again, we want to note that these are not replicas of a galaxy
constrained in SLOW, but are used to recreate a realistic environ-
ment for a UHECR or TeV gamma-ray source, which usually sits
in the gaseous halo of a galaxy. The resulting number of suitable
galaxies is of the same order as for the other two methods.
2.5. Cascade spectrum
The last part of our methodology consists of employing the mag-
netic field profiles extracted using the method presented above
in a Monte Carlo simulation of the electromagnetic 𝛾-ray cas-
cade using the code ELMAG3.03 (Kachelrieß et al. 2012; Blytt
et al. 2020). The magnetic field profile along the line-of-sight
is gridded and read in as an input by the magnetic field module
of ELMAG3.03. Employing such a magnetic field model within
ELMAG was done by Dolag et al. (2009), Dolag et al. (2011), and
Tjemsland et al. (2024), among others. Our work improves on
Tjemsland et al. (2024) by using a constrained simulation and on
Dolag et al. (2009, 2011) with the method to improve the pointing
towards observed galaxies with the method outlined above.
ELMAG3.03 simulates the process of the electromagnetic 𝛾-
ray cascade (see, e.g., Neronov & Semikoz 2009; Neronov &
Vovk 2010; Durrer & Neronov 2013): TeV 𝛾-rays emitted by
a blazar interact with the extragalactic background light (EBL)
to produce an electron-positron pair in the inverse process of
electron-positron annihilation. Subsequently, the charged parti-
cles inversely Compton scatter with and accelerate CMB photons
to GeV energies. Due to the high energy of the initial 𝛾-ray and
relativistic beaming in the forward direction, both interactions do
not significantly change the propagation direction of the involved
particles. However, if there is a significant IGMF, the charged
part of the cascade can be deflected, and the GeV 𝛾-rays do not
reach the observer. This can be seen as a decline of 𝛾-rays in the
GeV part of the spectrum.
We simulate this effect using our simulated magnetic field
profile along the line of sight to the ideal position of Mrk501 and

3. Results
3.1. Intergalactic magnetic field Profiles
We extract models of the extragalactic magnetic field from the
constrained cosmological simulation SLOW by finding an “ideal”
position within the simulation value for a given source of UHECR
or TeV gamma-ray. We estimate the uncertainty of these magnetic
field models by calculating the magnetic field along the line-of-
sight to positions offset from the “ideal” position. Additionally,
we use the Monte Carlo Code ELMAG3.03 to estimate the prop-
agation of this uncertainty when the magnetic field model is
applied in a simulation of UHECR or TeV gamma-ray propaga-
tion.
Figure 6 presents the (simulated) magnetic field along the
line-of-sight towards the “ideal” position of a given source (in
this case Mrk501) in the SLOW simulation, and the magnetic
field along the line-of-sight towards the offset positions as de-
termined by the different methods to measure the uncertainty of
the magnetic field (see Sect. 2.4), in the first three panels. In
the last panel, each line represents the magnetic field along the
line-of-sight between a pair of random positions within the sim-
ulation box, but at the same distance as the distance to the “ideal”
position from the observer. There are 50 such pairs.
The magnetic field along the line-of-sight to the “ideal” po-
sition (red line) takes on values roughly between 10−9𝜇G to
10−6𝜇G (10−15 G to 10−12 G). We discuss in the next section
how this compares to observations. The magnetic field towards
the offset positions is in the same regime and has almost no dif-
ference in the first 10 Mpc. The structure changes afterward, but
is similar for each method. However, the last ∼10 Mpc vary
significantly. In the case where the positions only vary in 2D, the
magnetic field profiles are calculated up to the same distance,
while in the “3D” and “subhalos” case, the distances vary.
Of particular note is the “subhalos” case, which stands apart
from the other methods precisely because it incorporates a real-
istic environment as it inevitably ends within a cluster, while the
other methods can end within a filament or void. This inclusion
of realistic environmental structure is what drives a clear and dis-
tinguishing feature: an increase in the magnetic field in the last
few Mpc, extending on average up to 10−4𝜇G. This behavior is
a direct consequence of modeling the environment realistically,
rather than an artifact of position selection alone. By contrast, the
purely random positions (bottom panel) yield a magnetic field
strength in the same overall regime, but the scatter is larger in the
first 10 Mpc and also does not include a realistic environment in
the last few Mpc.
To summarize, the “3D” and “subhalos” methods include a
positional uncertainty not only in the angular position but also
in the distance, while the “2D” methods only include angular
uncertainty. These first three methods model the first few Mpc
accurately because the position of the observer is clearly defined
in the simulation. Additionally, the “subhalos” methods include a
realistic environment in the last few Mpc. However, all magnetic
field lines towards the ideal position create less scatter in the first
few Mpc, compared to using random lines of sight.
3.2. Rescaled magnetic field profiles
So far, we have focused on the simulated magnetic field 𝐵sim
along the line of sight to a source. This model, directly obtained
by solving the MHD equations, reproduces well the magnetic
field strength in clusters (Böss et al. 2024). However, in the
density regime of filaments, it was shown to underpredict the
magnetic field strength, resulting in effectively zero synchrotron
emission (Böss et al. 2024). Investigation of the magnetic field
along a line-of-sight through a filament therefore needs to use
different models, which we presented in Sect. 2.2.
Figure 7 presents the magnetic field along the line-of-sight
to the ideal position of a source (here Mrk501) for all presented
magnetic field models. Similar to Fig. 2 and Fig. 3, 𝐵dyn,↑results
in the highest magnetic field followed by 𝐵F, 𝐵ff, 𝐵𝛽, and 𝐵sim.
𝐵dyn,↓is a special case, almost reaching 𝐵dyn,↑in small over-
densities 5, but otherwise resulting in negligible magnetic field
strengths.
We compare the models to measurements of the magnetic
field in the Coma cluster (Bonafede et al. 2010) and the average
field strength in filaments (Carretti & Vazza 2025), both utilizing
the rotation measure (RM), and several measurements of the
IGMF (Neronov & Vovk 2010; Tjemsland et al. 2024; Webar
et al. 2025; Blunier et al. 2025), all utilizing the 𝛾-ray cascade
(see Sect. 2.5). As this line-of-sight does not pass through a
galaxy cluster, the magnetic field strength of the Coma cluster
(solid black line) is never reached. The average field strength in
filaments (solid gray line) is reached by 𝐵dyn,↑and 𝐵ff in small
overdensities, and once even by 𝐵dyn,↓. The line-of-sight mostly
passes through intergalactic space, which is best reproduced by
the simulated magnetic field 𝐵sim, which is, over most of the
distance, just slightly stronger than the measurement of the lower
limit by Neronov & Semikoz (2009, dashed gray line), Tjemsland
et al. (2024, dash dotted gray lines), and Webar et al. (2025,
dotted gray lines, uncertainties are included). Recent estimates by
Blunier et al. (2025) lie further below 𝐵sim, for both the strongest
constraints by an individual blazar (light gray solid lines) and the
combined constraints (dark gray solid line). However, these are
the most conservative constraints as they assume a conservative
blazar activity time scale of just 𝑇∼16yr (the time span of
Fermi/LAT data in their analysis).
In conclusion, the available magnetic field models are able to
reproduce all relevant regimes of the IGMF in the local Universe
if combined correctly.

4.2. Comparison to previous work
Magnetic fields from cosmological simulations have been used
in a multitude of works studying multi-messenger particles (i.e.,
𝛾-rays and UHECR). Here we list a selection.
Pioneering work has been done by Sigl et al. (2004), who
were the first to estimate the deflections of UHECRs through the
IGMF as simulated by a cosmological simulation and using a
source distribution drawn from the gas distribution of the same
simulation within 50 Mpc. A crucial shortcoming was the use of
an unconstrained cosmological simulation. This was improved
upon by Dolag et al. (2005, Coruscant), who used constrained
initial conditions based on the IRAS 1.2-Jy all-sky redshift sur-
vey. They concluded that deflections of UHECRs (𝐸≥4 × 1019
eV), accounting for structures within 110 Mpc, are small (< 1◦)
except in galaxy clusters.
Focusing on the magnetic field along the line-of-sight to sin-
gular objects, similar to what this study presents, Dolag et al.
(2009) studied the effect of 𝛾-ray halos building around point-
like blazars caused by the electromagnetic pair-cascade (Neronov
& Semikoz 2009). The same magnetic field models were used
by Dolag et al. (2011) to study the decline of the GeV 𝛾-ray flux
produced by the same cascade from TeV 𝛾-rays and deflections
caused by the magnetic field. A caveat of both studies was their
limited simulation size, where the studied sources were located
at distances 𝑑𝑖∈[130, 200] Mpc (Dolag et al. 2009) or even
𝑑≈591 Mpc (Dolag et al. 2011), while the size of the simu-
lation was limited to ≈115 Mpc.7 This required the mirroring
of the line-of-sight magnetic field to extend the model, which
potentially decreased the accuracy.
More recent studies of UHECR propagation on the basis of
cosmological simulations include Hackstein et al. (2018), who
investigated the impact of different magnetogenesis scenarios
and source distributions on the anisotropy of UHECR arrival
directions. Models drawn from this simulation potentially reach
large enough distances to include more of the relevant sources

This list shows that many studies using magnetic field mod-
els from cosmological simulations either have the shortcoming of
not using constrained initial conditions or not being large enough
to cover distances to many relevant sources. Using magnetic field
models extracted from SLOWCR-30723 can reconcile these is-
sues. However, SLOW is also limited to a box size of 500 Mpc/h
(and therefore does not reach, for example, 1ES 0229+200) and
does not yet provide the full range of possible magnetogenesis
scenarios. The simulation by Hackstein et al. (2018) also recon-
ciles these shortcomings. We improve on it in resolution and by
using a different numerical scheme, but we do not yet provide all
their magnetogenesis scenarios.
Crucially, we are the first to suggest an algorithm to find
an “ideal position” for UHECR/TeV gamma-ray sources below
the constraining scale of the initial condition and are therefore
able to provide more accurate magnetic field models along the
line-of-sight.
Finally, studies that back-propagate UHECRs to find their
sources often use very elaborate models of the galactic magnetic
field (e.g., Unger & Farrar 2024), but resort to employing a Gaus-
sian random field with a Kolmogorov turbulence spectrum (e.g.,
Bourriche & Capel 2026) or an even simpler approach of Gaus-
sian beam widening based on the same spectrum (e.g., Bister &
Farrar 2024). The accuracy of such an investigation could poten-
tially also be increased by employing magnetic field models as
extracted from a cosmological simulation.
5. Conclusion
In this work, we present properties of the simulated magnetic field
from the constrained cosmological simulation SLOW (Dolag
et al. 2023) and rescaled magnetic field models (Böss et al. 2024).
We present a novel algorithm to determine an “ideal position” for
galaxies below the constraining power of the initial conditions
within the simulation, in order to extract magnetic field models
along the line-of-sight to relevant sources interesting for the study
of UHECRs, ultra-high-energy gamma-rays, or the origin of the
IGMF.
We find that the presented magnetic field models cover a
large range of magnetic field filling factors and cover different
regions in the electron density-magnetic field strength phase in
the density regime of filaments, but agree in the center of galaxy
clusters. However, the IGMF agrees best with the lower limits
derived from the gamma-ray cascade of GeV 𝛾-rays from TeV
𝛾-ray sources when using the simulated magnetic field.
We present an algorithm to find an “ideal position” for galax-
ies in the SLOW simulation, whose positions are unconstrained
by the initial conditions. The large-scale structure drift between
observed and simulated galaxy clusters used in this algorithm
can potentially also be used to improve the initial conditions.
Finally, we show that the magnetic field models derived from
this “ideal position” result in more accurate simulations of the
𝛾-ray cascade spectrum and therefore might also benefit other
multi-messenger studies employing a model for the IGMF.
