ABSTRACT
Context. Originating from massive stars that have lost the majority of their hydrogen envelopes, Type IIb supernovae (SNe IIb) are stellar ex-
plosions of partially stripped stars. Because of this extensive stripping, their spectra exhibit transitional signatures bridging the gap between
hydrogen-rich (Type II) and helium-rich (Type Ib) events, making them crucial astrophysical probes for investigating the final evolutionary stages
of massive stars.
Aims. We aimed to investigate the physical properties and the underlying explosion mechanisms of the Type IIb SN 2025aico. Through a compre-
hensive analysis of early-phase optical light curves and spectroscopic data, we aim to constrain the fundamental explosion parameters and evaluate
the physical state of the event.
Methods. We present early multi-band optical imaging and low-resolution optical spectroscopic follow-up observations of the Type IIb
SN 2025aico, spanning ∼70 days from the explosion. We constrain the properties of SN 2025aico using a hybrid model that combines shock-
cooling emission and radioactively powered diffusion, and by analyzing its spectroscopic evolution. We use various approaches to constrain the
56Ni mixing from early data, and also compare our spectra with models to constrain the properties of the progenitor.
Results. The explosion epoch of SN 2025aico is estimated to be MJD 61032.69, while the rise time in the rM-band is 22.30 ± 0.70 days. The
peak pseudo-bolometric luminosity in the optical bands is Lopt = (4.07 ± 0.10) × 1041 erg s−1. The fitting yields a moderate to relatively low 56Ni
mass of MNi = 0.033+0.006
−0.004 M⊙and an ejecta mass of Mej = 2.79+0.21
−0.18 M⊙. The photospheric velocity near the bolometric peak, measured from
the Fe ii λ5169 line, is 6450+180
−160 km s−1. The derived envelope properties suggest a compact He-star progenitor possessing an H-rich envelope of
Menv ≈0.01 M⊙and a radius of Renv ≈6 –10 R⊙.
Conclusions. The derived physical properties of SN 2025aico indicate an origin from a moderate-mass, stripped He-star in a compact binary
system, characterized by a minimal residual hydrogen envelope. The explosion itself demonstrates weak to moderate 56Ni mixing throughout the
ejecta.
Key words. supernovae: general – supernovae: individual: SN 2025aico – galaxies: individual: LEDA 35384
1. Introduction
Massive stars (MZAMS ≳8 M⊙) end their evolution as core-
collapse (CC) supernovae (SNe), leaving behind a compact rem-
nant (Woosley et al. 2002; Janka 2012). Observationally, CC-
SNe are classified based on the presence or absence of hydro-
gen lines in their spectra. Events exhibiting prominent hydrogen
lines are classified as Type II SNe. Conversely, SNe lacking per-
sistent hydrogen features are categorized as stripped-envelope
(SE) SNe (Clocchiatti et al. 1996). This class encompasses Type
IIb events, which exhibit transient hydrogen features only in
their early spectra; Type Ib SNe, defined by the presence of
helium; and Type Ic SNe, which are distinguished by the ab-
sence of both hydrogen and helium signatures (Filippenko 1997;
Modjaz et al. 2019). These events originate from massive pro-
genitors that have been partially or completely stripped of their
hydrogen or helium envelopes, either by strong stellar winds
(e.g. Wolf-Rayet (WR) stars; Smith 2014; Crockett et al. 2008)
or via mass transfer processes in binary systems through Roche-
lobe overflow (RLOF) (Ritter 1988; Maund et al. 2004).
Type IIb events belong to a subclass of SE-SNe and are con-
sidered transitional objects between SNe II and SNe I. They
exhibit hydrogen lines during their early evolution, whereas at
later times, their spectra become dominated by helium lines,
resembling those of SNe Ib (Filippenko 1988). The photomet-
ric and spectroscopic diversity of SNe IIb is fundamentally
driven by the properties of their residual thin hydrogen en-
velopes. Based on progenitor characteristics, SNe IIb can be
classified into compact (cIIb) and extended (eIIb) subcategories
(Chevalier & Soderberg 2010). SN cIIb progenitors are charac-
terized by relatively small radii (∼1–100 R⊙) and higher pre-
explosion masses, yielding single-peaked light curves or those
with a negligible shock-cooling phase (Barmentloo et al. 2024).
Conversely, SNe eIIb progenitors possess more extended radii
(≳100–200 R⊙) and lower masses, resulting in double-peaked
light curves. In the SN eIIb scenario, the initial peak is governed
by shock-cooling emission (Nagy & Vinkó 2016; Dessart et al.
2018), whereas the broader secondary peak is sustained by ra-
dioactive decay (Arnett 1982; Arnett & Fu 1989) and recom-
bination processes (Nagy et al. 2014; Nagy & Vinkó 2016). In
contrast, for the SN cIIb scenario, the shock-cooling phase is
much shorter and fainter, or even entirely absent.

these transients (Ensman & Woosley 1988; Woosley & Eastman
1997; Bersten et al. 2012). Such mixing can arise from large-
scale asymmetric explosions and hydrodynamic instabilities
(Maund et al. 2009; Dessart et al. 2012). Dessart et al. (2015)
demonstrated that He features in SE SN spectra result from

the combined effects of the mixing of 56Ni and the presence

may be absent. Furthermore, Yoon et al. (2019) noted that the
mixing of 56Ni strongly influences the early-phase colour evo-

rameters governing the early colour evolution, thereby provid-

cooling phase. To accurately constrain the mixing of 56Ni, early-
phase data are crucial; relying solely on peak and late-phase
data makes such constraints highly difficult (Kumar et al. 2022).
Moreover, because SE SN explosions are often inherently asym-
metric (van Baal et al. 2023), any mixing constraints derived
from early-phase observations are strictly limited to the line of
sight.
Constraining the progenitor properties of SE SNe is impor-
tant, as it provides critical insights into late-stage stellar evolu-
tion and mass transfer process in massive binary (Dessart et al.
2015, 2016; Yoon et al. 2017). Furthermore, certain Type cIIb
events bridge the gap between typical SNe IIb and SNe Ib
(Medler et al. 2021), suggesting an evolutionary continuum
characterized by the presence of only a residual amount of hy-
drogen. However, placing tight constraints on these progenitor
properties requires early-phase observations with comprehen-
sive coverage. Therefore, we present early multi-band photomet-
ric and low-resolution spectroscopic observations of the nearby
cIIb SN 2025aico, obtained using various facilities. By analyzing
this event, we aim to constrain the properties of the progenitor
and the degree of early 56Ni mixing. These findings will yield a
clearer picture of the early radiative transfer process of the ex-
plosion and the binary stellar evolutionary pathways leading to
cIIb events.
In this work, we model the light curve by incorporat-
ing both the shock-cooling phase and radioactively pow-
ered diffusion (Piro 2015; Sapir & Waxman 2017; Arnett & Fu
1989; Nagy & Vinkó 2016). Furthermore, we perform a semi-
quantitative spectroscopic analysis of crucial features and inves-
tigate the early evolution to constrain the 56Ni mixing, a crit-
ical parameter for early SE-SNe evolution (Yoon et al. 2019;
Moriya et al. 2020). Drawing upon these modelling and analyti-
cal results, we discuss the nature of the progenitor and the explo-
sion mechanism. In Sect. 2, we report the fundamental proper-
ties of SN 2025aico and its host galaxy. In Sect. 3, we present
the photometric data and analyse the light curve features. In
Sect. 4, we describe the spectral sequence and perform a semi-
quantitative spectroscopic analysis. In Sect. 5, we examine the
shock-cooling emission and non-thermal line evolution, along-
side the proposed progenitor scenario. Finally, we summarize
our findings and results in Sect. 6.
However, this article focused primarily on standard early-
time and photospheric phase spectroscopic analyses. For a de-
tailed investigation into spectropolarimetry, we refer the reader
to Pyykkinen et al. (2026, in prep.).

are shown in Fig. 2. A detailed description of the observations

cadence about ∼1 day throughout the SN evolution. Only the
magnitudes in the UV and the U, B, V filters are reported in the
Vega system, while those in all other bands are in the AB mag-
nitude system. We estimated the explosion epoch by taking the
average time between the last upper limit (mL > 20.30 mag) and
the first detection (mL = 16.86±0.01 mag) in the GOTO-L band,
which yields MJD 61032.69±0.51. We noticed that SN 2025aico
exhibits a brief shock-cooling emission during the very early
phase, similar to those observed in events such as SN 2011hs
(Bufano et al. 2014) and SN 2011dh (Arcavi et al. 2011). The
shock-cooling light-curve peak of SN 2025aico is prominent in
the shorter-wavelengths bands (with the U band declining by
2.3 mags in just 3.7 days). However, as the optical bands are
poorly covered at early phases, we cannot estimate the decline
rate in these bands.
To better constrain the epoch and the magnitude at maxi-
mum in all bands, which exhibit fairly good coverage, we ap-
plied polynomial fits to individual light curves; the uncertain-
ties are estimated through a Monte-Carlo process. The appar-
ent magnitude light curves of SN 2025aico reach the maxi-
mum brightness on MJD 61052.90 ± 0.28 in the gM-band, and
MJD 61054.99 ± 0.48 in the rM-band. These provide rise times
of 20.21 ± 0.58 days and 22.30 ± 0.70 days in the gM and rM
bands, respectively. These rise times are consistent with those
observed in typical SNe IIb such as SN 2011dh (∼21.9 days in
the R-band; Ergon et al. 2014) and SN 2024abfo (∼24.1 days
in the r-band; Reguitti et al. 2025). The apparent magnitude at
maximum are 15.72±0.02 mag and 15.60±0.02 mag in gM band
and rM band, respectively.
We used a linear fitting procedure to the post-peak light
curves to determine the decline rates in time different intervals
(0 ≲t ≲15 days from maximum and t ≳20 days after max-
imum). We note a rapid post-peak decline in the apparent light
curves, which can be attributed to adiabatic expansion combined
with radioactive decay. However, an alternative explanation in-
volving the recession of the recombination front of helium in
the co-moving frame, similar to the rapid decline following the
plateau phase in SNe II-P (Kumar et al. 2013; Nagy et al. 2014),
cannot be entirely ruled out. A further discussion of this fea-
ture is presented in Sect. 3.4. The decline rates between 0 and
15 days is γ0<t<15 = 8.53 ± 0.49 mag (100 d)−1 in the gM band,
and γ0<t<15 = 6.33 ± 0.39 mag (100 d)−1 in the rM band, sug-
gesting a rapid decrease in temperature. After ∼20 days, the
decline rates decrease to γt>20 = 1.88±0.23 mag (100 d)−1 in the
gM band and γt>20 = 2.76 ± 0.21 mag (100 d)−1 in the rM band.
The higher decline rates in the redder bands suggest a receding
of the photosphere in the co-moving frame, indicating that the
inner regions of the ejecta with higher temperature are becom-
ing gradually visible. This behaviour is consistent with most of
SE-SNe like SN 2017iro (Kumar et al. 2022) and SN 2022ngb
(Zhao, J.-W. et al. 2026). The main parameters inferred from the
multi-band apparent light curves are reported in Table B.2.
3.2. Intrinsic colour evolution
The intrinsic colour evolution of SN 2025aico is presented in
Fig. 3. The early-phase colour evolution provides a rough es-
timation of the 56Ni mixing, a crucial parameter for SE-SNe
(Dessart et al. 2015, 2016; Yoon et al. 2019). Therefore, we
compare our observed colour evolution with models taken from
Moriya et al. (2020), which include scenarios ranging from with-
out mixing to full mixing.
During the very early phase (t
≲
5 days), the colour
rapidly reddens, with g −i changing from 0.12 ± 0.10 mag
to 0.53 ± 0.12 mag. This rapid reddening marks efficient cool-
ing driven by adiabatic expansion. Such a feature is common
among SNe IIb that exhibit a prominent shock-cooling phase,
such as SN 1993J (Richmond et al. 1994, 1996) and SN 2011fu
(Kumar et al. 2013; Morales-Garoffolo et al. 2015). From t ∼
7 days to t ∼17 days after the explosion, the colour becomes
bluer, revealing the contribution of radioactive heating. During
this period, the colour reaches 0.26 ± 0.04 mag in B −V and
−0.15 ± 0.06 mag in g −i. Between t ∼20 and t ∼30 days
after the explosion, the colour becomes redder again as the cool-
ing of the fast-expanding ejecta becomes more efficient than
the radioactive energy input. The corresponding colour reaches
1.09 ± 0.05 mag in B −V and 1.02 ± 0.15 mag in g −i. How-
ever, for epochs t ≳30 days after the explosion, the colour index
becomes stable and then gradually turns to blue.
To constrain the mixing of the 56Ni in SN 2025aico, we com-
pare our observational data against models featuring varying de-
grees of 56Ni mixing from Moriya et al. (2020). The models as-
sume an initial stripped He-star progenitor of 3.85 M⊙that ex-
plodes with an ejecta mass of Mej = 2.5 M⊙, a kinetic energy
of Ek = 1 × 1051 erg, and a nickel mass of MNi = 0.05 M⊙.
We note that as the mixing becomes stronger, the early colour
evolution becomes more monotonic. For SN 2025aico, we ob-
serve a prominent “U”-shaped colour evolution during the early
phase (t ≲25 days after the explosion), which is also visible in
SN 2024aecx (Zou et al. 2026). This shape serves as evidence
for weak or negligible 56Ni mixing. From the early-phase com-
parison, we suggest that the mixing of 56Ni in SN 2025aico
closely resembles models with no or minimal mixing, and cer-
tainly does not exceed half-mixing. However, the early shock-
cooling in the early phase could hamper us from accurately
constraining the degree of 56Ni mixing. At later phases, we
find that our observational data deviates significantly from the
model predictions. This discrepancy likely arises from the lower
synthesized 56Ni mass in SN 2025aico, which injects less en-
ergy into the ejecta and fails to maintain the temperature high.
Furthermore, recombination during the post-peak evolution of
SN 2025aico plays a crucial role, a topic we discuss in detail in
Sect. 3.4.
3.3. Pseudo-bolometric light curve
The pseudo-bolometric light curve of SN 2025aico, along with
those of comparison events, is shown in Fig. 4. We chose the
comparisons that are typically well-studied or have similar lu-
minosities. Each pseudo-bolometric light curve was constructed
using photometry in the optical bands, ranging from the B- to
the z bands, utilizing the superbol code (Nicholl 2018). Specif-
ically, due to the lack of early optical coverage, the first hol-
low data point was derived by scaling the L-band luminosity
based on a blackbody temperature inferred from the Swift/UVOT
UV and NUV data. The pseudo-bolometric peak luminosity of
SN 2025aico is Lopt = (4.07 ± 0.10) × 1041 erg s−1 and the
corresponding rise time is 21.89 ± 0.67 days. The rising time
of SN 2025aico is consistent with SN 1993J (Richmond et al.
1994, 1996) and SN 2008ax (Pastorello et al. 2008), but slightly
shorter than SN 2022ngb (Zhao, J.-W. et al. 2026). We note that
SN 2025aico exhibits a lower luminosity compared to events
like SN 1993J and SN 2011fu (Kumar et al. 2013), but shared
a similar peak luminosity with low-luminosity events such as
SN 2011ei (Milisavljevic et al. 2013), SN 2011hs (Bufano et al.
2014), and SN 2022ngb.

SN 2025aico presents prominent shock-cooling emission
during its very early phases (especially in bluer bands, see
Sect. 3.1), a feature also visible in SN 1993J, SN 2011fu, and
SN 2024aecx, indicating the remaining H-rich envelope. How-
ever, the luminosity of the shock-cooling emission in the optical
bands is relatively faint when compared to other events and to
the radioactively powered peak of SN 2025aico itself, reaching
only Lopt ≈1.5×1041 erg s−1 initially. This feature suggested that
most of the emission is concentrated in the UV and bluest opti-
cal bands, suggesting a compact envelope that produces a low-
luminosity spectral energy distribution (SED) tail in the optical
bands (Rabinak & Waxman 2011; Nakar & Sari 2010). Further-
more, the duration of the shock-cooling phase for SN 2025aico
is shorter than that of comparison events, lasting only ≲4 days,
which suggests a relatively compact and/or low-mass envelope
(Pastorello et al. 2008; Roming et al. 2009). Moreover, for the
faint shock-cooling emission to remain visible, the mixing of
56Ni should be limited. This is because otherwise radioactive
heating would obscure the very early shock-cooling features.
This physical constraint is consistent with the modelling derived
from the earlier colour evolution.
We also observe a rapid decline within ∼20 days after
the rM-band maximum, a feature also visible in SN 1993J and
SN 2011fu, which we also noticed in the apparent light curve in
Sect. 3.1. This feature suggested that the outer ejecta gradually
become optically thin, resulting in a rapid recession of the pho-
tosphere in the comoving frame. This rapid decline spans ∼10–
15 days after the rM-band maximum, a duration slightly shorter
than that observed in SN 1993J. Finally, the late-time decline rate
of SN 2025aico is comparable to that of most SNe IIb. However,
it is slightly faster than the rates of SN 1993J and SN 2011fu,
yet slower than that of fast-evolving events such as SN 2024aecx.
This intermediate behaviour suggests a moderate degree of γ-ray
leakage in SN 2025aico.
3.4. Light curve modelling
To constrain the explosion parameters of SN 2025aico, we con-
struct the bolometric light curve by fitting a blackbody func-
tion to the SED. We only keep the detection photometric data
and ignore the upper-limit photometric data. Then, we imple-
ment the model from Arnett & Fu (1989), which is a hybrid two-
component light curve model, highly suitable for CC SNe be-
cause it includes the contribution of recombination. This model
accounts for the early shock-cooling emission and the radioac-
tively powered light curve. We first fit the portion of the light
curve that is mainly powered by radioactive decay (for t >
5 days). The heating rate follows the radioactive decay chain
of 56Ni →56 Co →56 Fe (Arnett 1982; Valenti et al. 2008).

4. Spectroscopy
4.1. Spectral sequence
The spectral evolution is presented in Fig. 6. A detailed descrip-
tion of the observations can be found in Appendix A. The spec-
troscopic data span a period from −20.4 days to +38.9 days rela-
tive to the rM-band maximum, with a fairly good coverage in the
pre-peak phases. Narrow emission lines such as Hα, Hβ, [N ii],
and [S ii] originate from H ii regions within the host galaxy, indi-
cating active star formation in the local environment. All spectra
are flux calibrated to match the photometric data, and then cor-
rected for reddening and reported to the rest frame.
At very early epochs (−20.4 and −19.8 days relative to the
maximum in the rM band), the spectra exhibit a blue, relatively
featureless continuum. During this period, the blackbody tem-
perature decreases from 11.79±0.28 kK to 8.20±0.27 kK. Such
temperatures are comparable to those observed in events with
early shock-cooling emission, including SN 2024aecx (∼15 kK;
Zou et al. 2026). Furthermore, broad P Cygni profiles of the
Balmer and He i λ5876 transitions display highly blueshifted ab-
sorption components, indicating a rapid expansion of the ejecta
around ∼18000 km s−1. Driven by adiabatic expansion, the tem-
perature rapidly decreases to 7.66 ± 0.16 kK by −17.1 days;
at this temperature, the continuum gradually reddens and low-
ionisation metal lines such as Fe ii begin to emerge. This spec-
tral evolution reflects the cooling of the ejecta, coinciding with
the end of the shock-cooling phase, in agreement with our pho-
tometric observations.
As the photosphere retreats from the outer envelope to the
He core, the He i λ5876 line with a P Cygni profile emerges,
showing a blueshifted emission peak. This blueshifted peak is
primarily driven by the steep density profile and the dominance
of electron scattering within the fast-expanding and optically
thick ejecta, a combination that enables line formation inside
the continuum photosphere (Anderson et al. 2014). Transitions
of Ca ii and Fe ii become prominent in the blue region of the
spectra. The broad P Cygni profiles become gradually narrower
in response to the decreasing photospheric velocity. Addition-
ally, a new feature at the blue wing of the Hα absorption ap-
pears at around −10 days. This feature could be explained by
either a high-velocity hydrogen component (Medler et al. 2021)
or by a possible absorption of Si ii (Gangopadhyay et al. 2018;
Zhao, J.-W. et al. 2026). However, since this feature appeared in
the early phase, as most silicon is located in the inner region of
the ejecta, we favour the explanation of a high-velocity hydro-
gen component, such as in SN 2020cpg (Medler et al. 2021). In
addition, we noticed that in the early phase, the absorption of Hβ
line exhibits an asymmetric broad bottom. Around the epoch of
−13.1 days, a possible high-velocity component emerged around
the Hβ line, resembling the Hα line. Nevertheless, the low S/N
of this spectral series and the line blending in the blue side of
these spectra preclude a conclusive identification.
From −14.9 days, the blackbody temperature starts to
rise again, increasing from 7.35+0.16
−0.20 kK to 9.13+0.24
−0.21 kK at
−12.1 days, before finally dropping to 4.87 ± 0.11 kK at
+30.2 days. This suggests that the energy input from the 56Ni
radioactive decay acts as a primary heating source during the
early phase. Following the adiabatic expansion of the ejecta, the
photosphere recedes into the deeper layers, which are charac-
terized by a lower velocity and a flatter density profile. In this
phase, the emission peak of the He i P Cygni profile gradually
shifts toward the rest wavelength (Anderson et al. 2014). The
opacity in the blue region of the spectra is predominantly pro-
vided by metal lines such as Fe ii, Sc ii, and Ti ii. Because tran-
sitions of Hα and He i cannot accurately trace the velocity of
the photosphere (Dessart & Hillier 2005), we estimate the pho-
tospheric velocity by fitting the P Cygni absorption component
of Fe ii λ5169 in the spectrum obtained at −0.9 days with a Gaus-
sian profile. This epoch is near the time of peak bolometric lu-
minosity (MJD 61053.27 ± 0.38). The fitting procedure yields
vph = 6450±170 km s−1, a value that will be used as an indicative
estimate of the ejecta velocity (vej) in the light-curve modelling.
Following the peak luminosity, O i λ7774 and O i λ6158
emerge, features that originate from the deeper regions of the
ejecta. The Ca ii near-infrared (NIR) emission features, along
with a possible [C i] line, gradually become prominent. In the
spectra obtained at +14.0 days and +30.2 days, we observe the
appearance of C i and O i λ9263 transitions in the near-infrared
region. The lines of the Balmer series gradually become weaker
and narrower. At the same time, the spectra are dominated by
the prominent P Cygni profiles of the He i transitions, consistent
with the typical spectroscopic evolution of a SN IIb.
4.2. Comparison with synapps models
To identify the main spectral features, we utilize the synapps
(Thomas 2013) code to fit the spectra at three selected epochs
(−19.8 days, −0.9 days, and +30.2 days relative to the rM-band
maximum). The synapps code utilizes syn++ as the parametrized
generator of model spectra. It assumes spherical symmetry and
homologously expanding ejecta. The photons in this code are
emitted from a sharp photosphere. The results are presented
in Fig. 7. We note that in the early-phase spectrum, the broad
Balmer lines appear to be significantly blue-shifted, while the
continuum is characterized by a BB temperature of approx-
imately 15.1 kK, which is briefly consistent with our result
measured from the BB fit. The blue wing of Hα may ei-
ther result from a blend with Si ii or represent a high-velocity
absorption component of Hα itself (Zhao, J.-W. et al. 2026;
Gangopadhyay et al. 2018; Medler et al. 2021). Furthermore, a
broad He i λ5876 is also visible in the early spectrum, likely ex-
cited by the shock. This feature suggests that some helium is
mixed into the envelope. However, because mixing in the outer
region remains limited (Jerkstrand et al. 2015), these He i lines
rapidly become narrow and no longer visible at later phases.
In the spectrum at peak (−0.9 days), the best-fit BB temper-
ature drops to 6580 K. Metal lines on the blue side of the spec-
tra begin to emerge, including Ti ii, Sc ii, and Fe ii. The Balmer
lines become narrower and less prominent, suggesting that the
line-forming region gradually becomes H-poor. Furthermore, the
high velocity component of Hα gradually becomes invisible. We
note that we cannot achieve a satisfactory fit for the He i P Cygni
profiles because the emission peaks are highly intense. In the red
and NIR regions of the spectra, O i lines are identified, revealing
the inner part of the ejecta. Furthermore, the absorption com-
ponent of the Ca ii NIR triplet flattens and subsequently evolves
into a prominent emission feature. Finally, the red wing of the
Ca ii NIR profile may also be blended with C i (Jerkstrand et al.
2015).

such as Fe ii, Ca ii, and Ti ii are present in the spectra. In general,
the spectrum of SN 2025aico is similar to that of SN 2008ax,
with the expected properties of the material ejected in the CC
of a He core with a residual outer hydrogen envelope. The
He i λ5876 line in SN 2025aico spectra does not exhibit a promi-
nent P Cygni profile. In particular, the absorption component
is weak and only a relatively narrow emission peak is visible.
This morphology is the direct result of weak 56Ni mixing (see
Sects. 3.2 and 4.3). Furthermore, SNe with weak to moderate
mixing scenarios (e.g., SN 2007Y) present similar features. In
contrast, the strong-mixing event SN 2020acat exhibits a promi-
nent He i λ5876 P Cygni profile. These observational results
are consistent with our earlier findings and the conclusions of
Dessart et al. (2015), suggesting that a weak mixing may fail to
excite prominent He-rich features, even when the ejecta environ-
ment is He-rich.
The peak spectrum (∼−0.9 days) of SN 2025aico is sim-
ilar to that of typical SNe IIb. The blue side of the spectrum
is dominated by metal lines. As the ejecta expand, certain fea-
tures, such as the Ca ii NIR triplet, evolve from absorption fea-
tures into emission lines. Since the γ-ray photons were able to
diffuse to the outer part of the ejecta from the weakly to mod-
erately mixed core, a non-thermally excited He i λ5876 line ex-
hibiting a P Cygni profile is visible in SN 2025aico. Compared to
SN 1993J, we note that the Hα absorption trough of SN 2025aico
is sharper and features a double-absorption component in the
blue wing. This morphology can be explained either by blend-
ing with lines such as C i and Si ii, or by the presence of a high-
velocity component of Hα. Additionally, a broad wing appears
on the red side of the He i λ5876 emission peak, which could
originate from higher-velocity helium in the outer ejecta. Nev-
ertheless, since the photosphere moves deeper into the ejecta,
the potential for blending caused by the gradually strengthening
Na i D lines cannot be entirely ruled out.
In the post-peak declining phase (∼30.1 days), the spec-
tra of the various events are quite similar. The P Cygni pro-
file of He i λ5876 becomes broad, resembling those usually
exhibited by other He-rich events. Additionally, the P Cygni
profiles of other He i lines become more prominent. The pri-
mary difference during this epoch lies in the presence of the
Hα line. In SN 1993J, SN 2020acat, and SN 2025aico, the
Hα feature remains visible, although it is much narrower than
at earlier phases. Conversely, for SN 2008ax and the H-poor
event SN 2007Y, the Hα feature is absent at later phases. This
suggests a lower amount of residual hydrogen in the progen-
itors of SN 2008ax and SN 2007Y than in the progenitor of
SN 2025aico. This provides further support to the conclusion
that SN 2025aico represents a transitional event between com-
pact and extended SNe IIb.
5. Discussion
5.1. Host galaxy of SN 2025aico
To obtain a better constraint on the progenitor scenario, it is cru-

profile of the He i λ5876 line becomes no longer detectable.

the rest wavelength. To confirm that this feature does not simply

The weak mixing scenario can explain at the same time the
absence of the He i P Cygni absorption and the peculiar emission
profile following the shock-cooling phase. Because He i lines are
primarily excited non-thermally, weak 56Ni mixing results in the
rapidly expanding outer He-rich layer receiving inadequate illu-
mination from γ-ray photons. Furthermore, as the temperature of
the outer region drops below the He recombination temperature
(8000–10000 K; Hatano et al. 1999), the outer ejecta becomes
more optically thin to the He i λ5876 transition. This lack of
non-thermal excitation and low opacity in the outer layers nat-
urally explains the absence of the P Cygni absorption trough
(Dessart et al. 2015). Meanwhile, deep within the ejecta near the
56Ni core, the material is adequately excited by γ-rays, allowing
the He i emission to form. As noted by Anderson et al. (2014),
because electron scattering dominates the continuum opacity,
line photons have a low destruction probability, enabling this
emission to emerge from layers even deeper than the contin-
uum photosphere. Also, the emission line from the inside of the
ejecta will present a blueshifted peak, which is consistent with
our observation. During the later phase, as γ-rays excited the
outer He i ejecta of SN 2025aico, the P Cygni profiles of both the
He i lines form via non-thermal excitation. Furthermore, the free-
electron density at −0.9 days is higher, as the material is ionized
by radioactively powered γ-ray photons. Because the opacity is
dominated by electron scattering, this enhanced electron density
makes the absorption components of the He i lines visible, pre-
senting the typical features of He-rich ejecta. In addition, the ris-
ing opacity along the line of sight causes the photosphere to shift
outward in the comoving frame. This outward movement results
in the increased velocity and width of the He i λ5876 line, and
hence an increased photospheric velocity (Moriya et al. 2020).
The late phase decrease of the He i λ5876 line velocity can be
explained as an effect of the receding photosphere.
5.4. Progenitor and explosion scenario
SE SNe originate from either single stars that lose their enve-
lope via strong stellar winds or from binary systems through
mass transfer processes. Given that the estimated ejecta mass
of SN 2025aico is between 1.5 and 2.8 M⊙, this yields an upper
limit for the pre-SN progenitor mass of MpreSN ≲4.5 M⊙. As
a consequence of the relatively low mass, it is unlikely that the
progenitor was stripped solely by stellar winds (Folatelli et al.
2015; Yoon et al. 2017). Therefore, binary mass transfer is the
strongly favoured stripping mechanism for the progenitor of
SN 2025aico. According to the models of Yoon et al. (2017),
forming such a compact progenitor requires either a short-period
binary with LMC-like metallicity, or a longer-period binary in-
volving a more massive progenitor and solar metallicity, imply-
ing stronger stellar winds. However, based on our light curve fit-
ting results, the massive progenitor scenario can be confidently
ruled out, as it overestimates the pre-supernova mass (MpreSN ≳
6 M⊙). It is important to emphasize that, within this specific evo-
lutionary framework, we cannot provide robust constraints for a
high-metallicity progenitor that also satisfies our low-mass cri-
teria; thoroughly evaluating the role of metal-rich environments
would require alternative theoretical models. Consequently, we
can only place constraints based on the models utilizing LMC
metallicity. Under this assumption and considering our previous
fitting results, we propose that the progenitor of SN 2025aico
was a stripped He star reasonably consistent with models hav-
ing MpreSN ranging from 3.53 to 4.56 M⊙and a radius ranging
from 10 R⊙to 30 R⊙, which correspond to the models ranging
from Lm11p10 to Lm13p400 in Yoon et al. (2017). These mod-
els reside in binary systems with orbital periods between 10 and
400 days.
To perform a robust consistency check of our results and
to provide a limit on the orbital period of the progenitor, we
compared our high-S/N spectra with the synthetic models from
Dessart et al. (2024), which have a similar companion initial
mass compared to models of Yoon et al. (2017). To minimize
the influence of 56Ni mixing, we analyze the spectra obtained at
the time of the maximum light. Furthermore, we scaled the syn-
thetic spectra to match the luminosity of our spectra. We utilized
models featuring relatively short periods ranging from 560 to
708 days. Because the models of Dessart et al. (2024) assume so-
lar metallicity, and because metallicity is highly degenerate with
the orbital period, it is reasonable that the periods within these
models are slightly longer. Therefore, we adopt the log2p75,
log2p80, and log2p85 models. Because the estimated Mej falls
within the reasonable range of 1.5 to 2.8 M⊙, these models re-
main entirely valid under our assumptions. The comparisons are
presented in Fig. 12.

spectra. Furthermore, the corresponding theoretical light curve

6. Conclusions
In this work, we presented multi-band photometric and spectro-
scopic observations of SN 2025aico. The photometric observa-
tions span a period from ∼−25 days to ∼+45 days relative to the
rM-band maximum, while the spectroscopic observations range
from ∼−20 days to ∼+39 days. The photometric data show
a rise time in the rM-band of ∼22.3 days, similar to those ob-
served in typical SNe IIb, featuring a relatively low-luminosity
peak of Lopt = (4.07 ± 0.10) × 1041 erg s−1. The spectroscopic
observations generally follow the evolution of typical SNe IIb,
with initial broad P Cygni profiles in the Balmer and He i λ5876
lines. However, starting from about two weeks before maximum
light, the profiles of these lines change. In particular, He i λ5876
no longer shows a P Cygni profile and Hα exhibits a double-
absorption trough. Furthermore, we note that the velocity de-
rived from the He i λ5876 P Cygni profile increases in the later
spectra. Combined with the intrinsic colour evolution, these fea-
tures were consistent with a weak to moderate 56Ni mixing.
By fitting theoretical models to the light curve, we find that
the progenitor of SN 2025aico is consistent with a compact He
star that has a residual H-rich envelope of Menv ≈0.02 M⊙and
Renv ≈6–10 R⊙. The ejecta mass is Mej = 2.79+0.21
−0.18 M⊙, accom-
panied by a relatively low nickel mass of MNi = 0.033+0.006
−0.004 M⊙.
Taking into account the dependence on the adopted optical opac-
ity, it is reasonable that the estimated Mej is in the range between
1.5 M⊙and 2.9 M⊙. These values indicate a pre-SN progenitor
mass of MpreSN ≈3–4.5 M⊙that retains only a trace of hydro-
gen, likely having been stripped via binary interaction. From the
model comparison, we assume that for an LMC-like metallicity,
the initial orbit period should reasonably be in the range 10–
400 days by early Case B mass transfer, which we favor in this
article. An upper limit can be given at ∼600 days on the as-
sumption of solar metallicity.
Early-phase data (e.g., the shock breakout, the shock-cooling
phase, and the early rise time) of SNe IIb significantly enhance
our understanding of the explosion mechanism and provide cru-
cial constraints on the progenitor. The Mephisto system, com-
prising a 1.6-m telescope and two 50-cm telescopes with a large
field of view, will enable simultaneous observations in both the
ugi and vrz bands. This capability is crucial for obtaining real-
time color information of SNe during their earliest epochs, en-
abling improved modelling of the shock-cooling phase and of-
fering tighter constraints on the 56Ni mixing, a parameter of re-
markable importance in SE SNe. Furthermore, combining opti-
cal observations with X-ray data from the Einstein Probe (EP;
Yuan et al. 2025) will allow us to capture the first light from the
shock breakout. Ultimately, the synergistic use of these facilities
will yield a much deeper understanding of such transient events.

Appendix A: Data observation and reduction
We carried out multi-band optical follow-up observations of
SN 2025aico in the Johnson-Cousins BV, Sloan ugriz, and
Mephisto uvgriz bands after its discovery. Optical data are col-
lected from: The 1.6m Mephisto telescope with mosaic cam-
era at Lijiang Observatory of Yunnan Astronomical Observa-
tories, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China; The 2.0m Hi-
malayan Chandra Telescope (HCT) with Himalayan Faint Ob-
ject Spectrograph Camera (HFOSC) of the Indian Astronomi-
cal Observatory, Hanle, India; The 0.7m GROWTH-India tele-
scope (GIT) of Indian Astronomical Observatory, Hanle, India;
The 0.67 m/0.92 m Schmidt telescope with a Moravian camera
at Padova Astronomical Observatory, Asiago, Italy; The 1.82 m
Copernico Telescope with the Asiago Faint Object Spectrograph
and Camera (AFOSC), Padova Astronomical Observatory, Asi-
ago, Italy; The 2.56 m Nordic Optical Telescope (NOT), at Ob-
servatorio Roque de Los Muchachos, La Palma, Spain, with the
Alhambra Faint Object Spectrograph and Camera (ALFOSC).
The initial reduction of all raw photometric data was per-
formed using standard procedures within the iraf (Tody 1986)
environment. This process included bias subtraction, overscan
correction, image trimming, and flat-fielding. When multiple
images in a given band were obtained on the same night,
we median-combined them into a single frame to increase the
S/N. We updated the world coordinate system solutions by
applying astrometry.net (Lang et al. 2010) and scamp (Bertin
2010). Subsequently, we performed photometric measurements
on these science images using the dedicated pipeline autophot2
(Brennan & Fraser 2022), which integrates several photometric
packages, such as photutils (Bradley et al. 2025) for source ex-
traction and flux measurements, and hotpants (Becker 2015) for
image subtraction. Because SN 2025aico is located in a region
contaminated by host-galaxy emission, we employed template
subtraction to remove this background flux. Specifically, the in-
strumental magnitudes of the SN were measured using point
spread function (PSF) fitting. The PSF model was constructed
by fitting the profiles of isolated and unsaturated stars within the
SN field. The resulting PSF model was subtracted from the sci-
ence frames, and the residuals at the position of the SN were
evaluated to ensure fitting quality. In cases where the SN was
not detected, an upper magnitude limit was derived.
The instrumental magnitudes of the SN were calibrated by
determining the instrumental zero points (ZPs) and color terms
(CTs). Specifically, the calibration for most bands was per-
formed via synthetic photometry derived from Gaia XP spec-
tra utilizing the gaiaxpy3 (Ruz-Mieres et al. 2025) package. For
the Mephisto ugriz bands, we adopted a calibration methodol-
ogy similar to those described in previous literature (Chen et al.
2024; Zou et al. 2026; Yang et al. 2024). Furthermore, to correct
the ZPs on non-photometric nights and enhance the overall pho-
tometric accuracy, we applied corrections derived from a local
sequence of standard stars in the vicinity of the SN.
Additionally, we obtained archival data from several pub-
lic surveys, including ATLAS, ZTF, and Swift/UVOT. The AT-
LAS c- and o-band light curves were retrieved from the AT-
LAS forced photometry service (Shingles et al. 2021). ZTF
g- and r-band data were obtained through the ALeRCE
2 autophot is a package for transient photometry using PSF fitting
and template subtraction, see https://github.com/Astro-Sean/
autophot
3 gaiaxpy is a package to facilitate handling Gaia BP/RP spectra as
distributed from the Gaia archive, see https://gaia-dpci.github.
io/GaiaXPy-website/
(Förster et al. 2021) and Lasair (Smith et al. 2019) brokers. Data
from Swift/UVOT were reduced using heasoft version 6.35.2, by
subtracting the baseline counts from historical data (Brown et al.
2009, 2014). We used an extraction radius of 5” and a back-
ground radius of 20” to estimate the fluxes. For the UBV bands,
the fluxes were converted to the standard Johnson UBV sys-
tem by applying the colour corrections by Poole et al. (2008).
Note that only UVW2-, UVW1- and U- bands are template-
subtracted; the other bands lack a template image.
The spectroscopic data were obtained using the following
telescopes and instruments: The 2.0m Himalayan Chandra Tele-
scope (HCT) with Himalayan Faint Object Spectrograph Cam-
era (HFOSC) of the Indian Astronomical Observatory, Hanle,
India; the 1.82 m Copernico Telescope with AFOSC; the 2.56 m
NOT with ALFOSC; the 3.6 m Telescopio Nazionale Galileo
(TNG) at Observatorio Roque de Los Muchachos, La Palma,
Spain, with its Low Resolution Spectrograph (LRS). Also, we
retrieved several public spectroscopic data in TNS, which were
reported by Gemini North Telescope in Maunakea Observatory,
Hawaii, USA and Multiple Mirror Telescope (MMT) in Fred
Lawrence Whipple Observatory, Arizona, USA (Andrews et al.
2025; Rehemtulla 2026).
The raw spectroscopic data of SN 2025aico were pro-
cessed using standard routines within the iraf package, follow-
ing the procedures described in Cai et al. (2022). Initial calibra-
tion steps, including bias subtraction, overscan correction, im-
age trimming, and flat-fielding, were performed following an ap-
proach similar to that used for the photometric data reduction.
One-dimensional spectra were then optimally extracted from the
processed two-dimensional frames. Wavelength calibration was
achieved using arc-lamp spectra obtained on the same night,
while flux calibration was performed using spectrophotometric
standard stars. To verify the accuracy of the flux calibration, the
calibrated spectra were compared and scaled against contempo-
raneous photometry of SN 2025aico. Finally, observations of
standard stars were utilized to remove telluric absorption fea-
tures from the SN spectra. A detailed log of the spectroscopic
observations is presented in Table B.1.
