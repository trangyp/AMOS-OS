Abstract. We develop a monadic approach to actions of internal categories. Given an
internal category, we construct a monad on a slice whose algebras are the actions of that
internal category. Then, we give a complete characterization of the monads that arise in
this way; we call them generalized action monads. Finally, we prove that these monads
yield a well-behaved notion of generalized descent.

1. Introduction
Actions of internal groups are one of the most fruitful and widely used categorical
concepts in algebra and geometry. They successfully abstract the concept of symmetry
of a set to general categories, unifying continuous group actions, smooth groups actions
and algebraic group actions, among others. In [6], an important generalization of ac-
tions of internal groups is proposed: the notion of action of an internal category. This
considerably widens the examples, as internal categories are a powerful notion capturing
topological categories, crossed modules, Lie groupoids, double categories and many more
mathematical structures [3, 7, 14]. Internal groups are particular internal categories, with
object of objects given by the terminal object. So the theory of actions of internal cat-
egories really generalizes that of actions of internal groups. Interestingly, internally to the
category of sets, actions of internal categories capture another of the most fruitful and
widely used categorical concept: that of a (co)presheaf (see [6]). So general actions of
internal categories may be thought as internal presheaves.
A key theorem in categorical algebra is that actions of internal groups can be captured
as algebras for a monad. More precisely, the actions of an internal group G in a category C
are the algebras for the monad G×−on C. We generalize this fundamental theorem to the
setting of actions of internal categories. This raises a natural problem: the usual action
monad of a group object acts on the ambient category itself, but an internal category has

Finally, we apply our results to descent theory. Classical descent along a morphism asks
when an object can be reconstructed from local data satisfying compatibility conditions
on overlaps, which are captured by the kernel pair of the starting morphism. Benabou and
Roubaud [1] then managed to capture this theory of descent from a monadic perspective.
The category of descent data is precisely the category of Eilenberg–Moore algebras of
a monad associated to the starting morphism p, given by the composite p∗p! where p∗
is pulling back along p and p! is pushing forward along p (in the case of the codomain
bifibration, p! is simply postcomposition by p). And the comparison functor of classical
descent theory is captured by the comparison functor of monad theory ([2]). Monadic
aspects of descent have then been extensively explored, see for example [14, 15, 5, 6, 10,
9, 11].
Our applications to descent naturally embed into this general monadic framework.
Rather than considering any monad to yield a notion of descent, we restrict ourselves
to generalized action monads. This has the advantage to yield a notion of generalized
descent that satisfies better properties than those shared by all monadic descents. In
particular, we construct a realization functor that is left adjoint to the comparison functor
using coequalizers, in a similar way to the realization functor of classical descent along
a morphism. The idea is that every internal category, equivalently corresponding to a
generalized action monad, encodes the instructions for the compatibility that the local
data need to satisfy. Classical descent along a morphism is then captured by the ˇCech
internal groupoid, built using the kernel pair of the starting morphism. The usual descent
monad is precisely the generalized action monad associated to this internal groupoid. The
idea to use internal categories to encode the compatibility instructions for descent then
allows us to naturally capture also Galois descent.
Galois descent was brought forward by [8] following the standard point of view in
categorical approaches to descent to consider the compatibilities on local data to be given
by symmetries [5, 6, 15]. It is a notion of descent associated to actions of group objects.
The compatibilites are encoded into an action groupoid associated to the starting action
of a group object. This motivated us to develop notions of descent associated to actions
of internal categories, with the aim to simultaneously capture both classical descent along

2.2. Internal categories and actions. Let C be a category with pullbacks. Internal
categories and internal functors in C are standard notions; see, for example, [3, 7, 14]. We
write Cat(C) for the category of internal categories in C and internal functors between
them. An object of Cat(C) will often be denoted D = (D1 ⇒D0), with source and target
maps s, t : D1 →D0, identity map e : D0 →D1, and composition map m : D1×s,D0,tD1 →
D1. The pullback D1 ×s,D0,t D1 abstractly captures composable pairs of morphisms (g, f).
[6] introduced a very interesting notion: that of action of an internal category. This
generalizes the well-known actions of group objects and opens the way to a broader theory
of actions. Actions of internal categories will be one of the central notions of this paper,
and a key element for our applications to descent theory.

D1 ×s,D0,γ C simplifies to the product G × C.

2.3. Classical descent along a morphism. Descent theory studies the following ques-
tion: when can an object be reconstructed from a local form together with compatibility
data? In the classical situation, the local form is obtained by pulling back along a morph-
ism. The compatibility data live on the self-overlap of that morphism. This is the ˇCech
point of view on descent; see [1, 2, 5, 6, 15]. Thanks to theory developed by Benabou–
Roubaud [1], the classical theory of descent along a morphism has been captured from a
monadic point of view. We recall below the main ideas of this approach, as this is the
point of view that is the most useful in this paper.
Let C be a category with pullbacks, and let p: E →B be a morphism in C. Pullback
along p gives a functor
p∗: C/B −→C/E.
An object over B may therefore be inspected locally over E. The question is whether an
object over E comes by pullback from an object over B. The self-overlap of E over B,
that expresses the compatibility of the local form, is the kernel-pair E ×B E of p. Call π1
and π2 the pullback projections.

on the triple overlap E ×B E ×B E. In the usual notation, this is the cocycle condition:
going directly from the first copy to the third copy agrees with going through the second.

pπ1 = pπ2. This defines the comparison functor

(1) a descent morphism if Kp is fully faithful;
(2) an effective descent morphism if Kp is an equivalence.

6. The general fibrational setting
We now explore a generalization of the results of the previous sections to the gen-
eral fibrational setting. We consider an arbitrary bifibration p: E →C satisfying the
Beck–Chevalley condition at every pullback square, rather than necessarily the codomain
bifibration cod: C 2 →C. A reference for bifibrations is [4]. In recent years, similar en-
deavours have been proved to be very fruitful. This is especially the case in the setting of
descent theory, which is where we aim to apply our results. We prove that every internal
category in C still yields an associated monad, with respect to the bifibration p: E →C,
generalizing the action monad we constructed in Section 3. However, trying to rebuild
an internal category from a monad proves harder in the general fibrational setting. We
conjecture that a fibrational generalization of the notion of internal category would be
more suitable for this purpose.
For simplicity, we assume the bifibration p: E →C to be normal (meaning that both
the fibration structure and the opfibration structure are normal).

7. Monadic descent data and realization via right modules
In this section, we apply our results to descent theory. We prove that every generalized
action monad yields a well-behaved notion of generalized descent.
This captures and
gives new insights to both the classical theory of descent along a morphism and Galois
descent (recalled in section 2). At the same time, it opens the way to new applications.
We will show some further examples in the next section. We compare our results with
the existing literature, in particular with the theory of monadic lax descent originated
from B´enabou–Roubaud [1], and that of lax descent objects brought forward by Lucatelli
Nunes [9]. We show that, interestingly, generalized action monads stand in a sweet spot
for descent theory.
In the framework of monadic descent, a notion of descent can be associated to any
monad. So it is clear that our theory will be embedded inside this general framework. But
restricting ourselves to generalized action monads gives an advantage: the theory then
satisfies better properties and becomes closer to what happens in the classical descent
theory. One important example of this is that we then have a realization functor, which
is left adjoint to the comparison functor. We will see that a key ingredient in our theory
will be that of a left adjoint right module over a monad, which slightly generalizes the
concept of generalized action monad. Throughout this section, we will work with this
slightly more general concept.

adjunction between the realization functor and the comparison.

Remark 7.24. The preceding theorem is deliberately stated for effective descent rather
than for descent alone. The reason is that the fully faithful case does not impose the
same rigidity on the comorphism of monads θ : T →RL. Indeed, in the situation of
Theorem 7.23, the comparison functor factors as Kα = Alg(IdE, θ)KεL, where Alg(IdE, θ) :
Alg(RL) →Alg(T) is restriction of algebra structures along θ. Thus descent for Kα is not
only the canonical descent problem associated with the adjunction L ⊣R. It also depends
on the way in which the generalized descent data encoded by T compare with the canonical
descent data encoded by the monad RL. In other words, one must also control whether
restriction along θ creates extra morphisms between algebra structures. More precisely,
Kα is fully faithful if and only if KεL is fully faithful and Alg(IdE, θ) is full on the full
subcategory of Alg(RL) spanned by the canonical objects KεL(C), C ∈C. In particular,
a sufficient global condition is that θ be dense, in the sense that Alg(IdE, θ) : Alg(RL) →
Alg(T) is fully faithful. For instance, this holds if each component θX : TX →RLX is an
epimorphism in E. This is weaker and less rigid than the effective descent statement. The
isomorphism of θ is detected by the unit of the adjunction Φα ⊣Kα on free T-algebras,
as in Lemma 7.22. By contrast, full faithfulness of Kα only forces the counit of Φα ⊣Kα
to be an isomorphism. Thus ordinary descent controls only the embedding of objects
of C into the category of T-descent data, while effective descent additionally forces all
T-descent data to come from objects of C. This is why ordinary descent has no direct
analogue of Theorem 7.23 with the condition “θ is an isomorphism” simply replaced by a
formal injectivity condition.
