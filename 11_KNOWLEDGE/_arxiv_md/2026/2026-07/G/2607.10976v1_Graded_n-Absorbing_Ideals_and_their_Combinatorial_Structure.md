Abstract. Graded n-absorbing ideals generalize graded prime ideals by extending ab-
sorption properties to products of (n + 1) homogeneous elements.
We study several
generalizations of graded prime ideals, including graded n-absorbing, graded weakly n-
absorbing, graded strongly n-absorbing, and graded n-absorbing primary ideals, as well
as related graded n-absorbing subgroups. Our primary result establishes a combinatorial
model for graded n-absorbing principal monomial ideals in polynomial rings with the stan-
dard grading. By identifying principal monomial ideals with exponential vectors in Nm,
we show that the graded n-absorbing principal monomial ideals correspond precisely to
lattice points in the simplex {α ∈Nm : |α| ≤n}. Consequently, the Hasse diagram of
principal monomial ideals is realized as the 1-skeleton of the Cayley graph of Nm, yielding
a geometric and combinatorial interpretation of graded n-absorption.
1. Introduction: Generalizations of Prime Ideals and Primary Ideals
The theory of prime ideals is central to commutative algebra, encoding multiplicative
structure in a form well-suited to geometric, arithmetic, and analytic applications. However,
many ideals of algebraic and geometric interest resist description in terms of prime ideals
alone: a proper ideal I of commutative ring R is prime if whenever ab ∈I, then a ∈I
or b ∈I. Anderson and Badawi [3] provide a more flexible framework to understand how
products of elements behave relative to a fixed ideal, generalizing the notion of a prime
ideal to an n-absorbing ideal: An n-absorbing ideal is a proper ideal I such that whenever
a product x1x2 · · · xn+1 ∈I, the product of some n of the factors also belongs to I. The
case n = 1 recovers prime ideals exactly, and the parameter n measures how far the ideal
is from being prime.
The n-absorbing framework has been extended in many directions, including primary
ideals [6, 7], commutative semigroups [24], and graded rings [2, 18]. This work expands the
study of n-absorbing ideals to the setting of graded commutative rings and graded ideals.
Graded rings arise naturally throughout mathematics, in algebraic geometry through the
construction of projective schemes, and in representation theory through weight space
decompositions. For a group G with identity e, a G-graded ring is a ring R decomposing
as R = L
g∈G Rg with RgRh ⊆Rg+h. A graded ideal is one that decomposes compatibly
with this grading, and a graded ideal I is graded n-absorbing if the n-absorbing condition
is imposed only on products of homogeneous elements. Naghani and Moghimi introduced
2-absorbing graded ideals in their 2016 work [18] by adopting the framework of Badawi
[5]. More recently, Akray et al published work in 2024 on graded n-absorbing I-ideals [1].

Theorem 3.1. Let F be a field and R = F[x1, . . . , xm]. For every proper, principal
monomial ideal I = (xα) of R,
ωR[X](I[X]) = ωR(I).
Proof. Let I = (xα), and define X such that X /∈{x1, . . . , xm}. Then the extension I[X]

4. Suggestions for Future Work
There are two immediate directions we can consider taking this work. Following Choi’s
work [8, 9], we can consider first expanding this framework from principal monomial ideals
to finitely generated monomial ideals. While this change may appear to be minor upon
first glance, this change is a significant step up in complexity. This is due to the fact that
in the principal monomial case, we were able to encode the information about the graded
n-absorbing ideals into the single exponential vector.
By now considering an ideal I of the form I = (xa1, xa2, . . . , xar), several compli-
cations arise. Most significantly, we can no longer represent ideals by a single point in
a lattice. For example, if we consider the ideal (xy, xz, yz), we see that the generators
are each of degree 2, but its behavior is defined by its overlap pattern rather than simply
by its degree. There is no immediately obvious analog of the |α| ≤n condition. This is

notions of adjacency that can be used in this framework include adding one minimal
generator, increasing one exponent in one generator , and performing a cover relation in the
monomial ideal lattice. However, with the choice of adjacency, we will produce different
graphs. Hence the Cayley graph interpretation is no longer immediate. Instead, we might
obtain unions of simplices, order complexes, Newton polyhedra, polyhedral cell complexes,
or convex hulls. Given that many algebraic properties of monomial ideals are encoded in
Newton polyhedra or polyhedral cell complexes, these seem the more likely candidate.
A promising direction to consider before jumping to general finitely generated monomial
ideals is to consider square free monomial ideals. From the Stanley-Reisner correspon-
dence, we can associate square free monomial ideals to simplicial complexes [12, 13]. In
this particular setting, we can conjecture that the graded n-absorbing ideals will correspond
to faces or vertex covers of simplicial complexes, or wiring diagrams [17, 19].
The second major area of expansion to consider is the extension from principal monomial
ideals to principal binomial ideals, and then to general binomial ideals. This jump is larger
in nature since binomial ideals encode both combinatorial information as well as algebraic
information arising from cancellation between terms. As a simple example, (xn −yn) and
(xn−1y −yn) have the same degree but very different factorization behavior. Since graded
n-absorbing ideals are defined through products of their elements, cancellation becomes
central.
The simple degree counting arguments we were able to use in the principal
monomial ideal setting will generally fail in this setting.
A richer model of geometry will be needed to encode the information in this setting.

and is governed by subgroups of Zm instead of Nm. As a result, our geometry will live in
Zm/L rather than the simplex we previously had. This is fundamentally different from the
Cayley graph description we have in our work, and the Hasse diagram description breaks
down. Any classification in this setting will probably involve lattice relations, factorization
structure, and association of primes. For example, over an algebraically closed field,
