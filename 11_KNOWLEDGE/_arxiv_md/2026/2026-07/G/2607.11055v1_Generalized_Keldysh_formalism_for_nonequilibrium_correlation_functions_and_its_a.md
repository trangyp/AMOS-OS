Generalized Keldysh formalism for nonequilibrium correlation functions and
its application to fluctuation dynamics
Ken Inayoshi,1, ∗Hiroshi Shinaoka,1 and Yuta Murakami2
1Department of Physics, Saitama University, Saitama 338-8570, Japan
2Institute for Materials Research, Tohoku University, Sendai 980-8577, Japan
Recent advances in time-resolved spectroscopies provide increasing access to collective dynamics in correlated
quantum materials. However, computing the corresponding nonequilibrium two-particle correlation functions
remains a major challenge. Here, by introducing a contour-dependent virtual probe field within the generalized
Keldysh formalism, we propose an approach that computes such correlation functions with the vertex corrections
essential for describing collective dynamics. In particular, we introduce a linear integral equation that computes
the correlation functions without explicitly constructing the four-time vertex kernel, and develop its matrix-free
Krylov solver based on quantics tensor trains. Combining our method with nonequilibrium dynamical mean-field
theory, we show that the fluctuation dynamics of the order parameter in a nonequilibrium symmetry-broken state
depends significantly on whether vertex corrections are included, and that the fluctuation and its decay time grow
near the nonthermal critical point. Our approach thus provides a practical route for evaluating nonequilibrium
correlation functions, which are emerging as key observables for characterizing states far from equilibrium.
Introduction— Driving quantum materials out of equilib-
rium can generate a variety of intriguing transient states and
dynamical regimes [1–5].
Their characterization has been
greatly advanced by time-resolved spectroscopies. In partic-
ular, time-resolved Raman scattering [6–14] and resonant in-
elastic X-ray scattering (RIXS) [15–24] now enable measure-
ments of the real-time evolution of low-energy excitations in
materials, including charge, spin, and phonon excitations. The
observed spectra correspond to nonequilibrium two-particle
correlation functions, such as the time-dependent dynamical
structure factor. Furthermore, such correlation functions en-
code higher-order correlation quantities, such as the quantum
Fisher information witnessing quantum entanglement [25–30],
which have recently attracted attention as new indicators for
characterizing nonequilibrium states [31, 32].
These experimental developments have stimulated extensive
theoretical efforts to compute the corresponding nonequilib-
rium spectra and correlation functions in correlated electron
systems, including time-resolved Raman responses [33–36]
and RIXS spectra [37–42].
A direct route is provided by
wave-function-based methods, such as exact diagonalization
and tensor-network approaches [38, 39, 43]. However, these
approaches are generally limited to small or low-dimensional
systems at zero temperature and are difficult to extend to real-
istic systems. As a complementary approach, the nonequi-
librium Green’s function (NEGF) method [1, 44–48] can
treat large, high-dimensional systems and naturally incorpo-
rate finite-temperature effects. However, it is essentially based
on the one-particle Green’s function, which describes one-
body excitations, as captured by time-resolved angle-resolved
photoemission spectroscopy (tr-ARPES) [49–61]. Accurately
incorporating many-body correlations to compute nonequilib-
rium correlation functions requires solving the nonequilibrium
Bethe-Salpeter equation (BSE) [48, 62], requiring substantial
memory and computational resources [1, 36, 63].
For this
reason, the correlation function is often approximated by the
element-wise product of one-particle Green’s functions (bub-
ble approximation), neglecting vertex corrections, i.e., without
collective effects [35].
A promising route beyond this limitation is to introduce a
virtual external probe field on the Keldysh contour and com-
pute the desired correlation function as the functional deriva-
tive of a one-particle observable with respect to this field [36].
In this work, we develop this idea into a general framework
for vertex-corrected nonequilibrium correlation functions us-
ing the generalized Keldysh formalism [64] for a generalized
contour-dependent Hamiltonian (GCH), whose branch depen-
dence encodes the virtual probe field [36] (Fig. 1(a)). In par-
ticular, we introduce a linear integral equation that, given the
converged one-particle Green’s function, directly computes its
variation (Fig. 1(b)). To solve it, we develop a matrix-free
Krylov solver that avoids both separate self-consistent simula-
tions for each probe field and the explicit construction of the
four-time vertex kernel, representing the two-time objects as
quantics tensor trains (QTTs) [65–72].
As a demonstration, we combine our method with nonequi-
librium dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT) [1, 4, 73–78]
and study the dynamics of order-parameter fluctuations in a
nonequilibrium symmetry-broken state (Fig. 1(c)). We find
that the vertex correction drastically alters the fluctuation dy-
namics, and that the fluctuation is strongly enhanced as the
system approaches the nonthermal critical point.
Generalized Keldysh formalism— The experimental spec-
tra, such as the time-resolved dynamical structure factor, are
calculated from the lesser and greater components of correla-
tion functions. In equilibrium, these components follow from
the retarded component via the fluctuation-dissipation theo-
rem. However, out of equilibrium this relation breaks down
and these components must be computed directly.
To this
end, we add to the Hamiltonian a virtual external field that de-
pends on the Keldysh contour C (Fig. 1(a)), and generalize the
Kubo formula [79] to access all components of the correlation
function (Matsubara, left-mixing, retarded, and lesser).

Numerical implementation— The numerical implementa-
tion of the perturbative GCH method does not depend on
how the two-time functions 𝐺and 𝐺′ are represented. They
can be stored either as dense matrices on a time grid (as in
the conventional method [1, 48, 95, 96]) or in a compressed
form [67, 97–100]. In this work, with a view toward future
calculations on large lattice systems, we adopt the QTT repre-
sentation [65–67] (see the tensor network in Fig. 1(b)) for two
reasons. First, it gives access to very fine time resolution at
low memory cost. Second, the diagrammatic operations such
as the element-wise product and the convolution integral can
be carried out directly in this compressed representation [67].
The detailed QTT implementation of Green’s functions is ex-
plained in Refs. [69–72] (see the End Matter for the bond
dimension and truncation cutoff used in the simulation).

Demonstration— Physical quantities encoded in correla-
tion functions, including entanglement measures [25, 29],
are emerging as promising diagnostics of nonequilibrium
states [31, 32]. We therefore use our approach to study order-
parameter fluctuations near a nonequilibrium phase transition,
taking as an example an interaction quench from a symmetry-
broken state in the Hubbard model. Such quenches are known
to exhibit a nonthermal critical point, distinct from the equi-
librium critical point, where the transient order-parameter dy-
namics changes qualitatively [98, 99, 105–108]. Since previ-
ous studies have focused mainly on the order parameter itself,
a one-particle observable, it remains unclear whether this non-
thermal criticality is also reflected in fluctuations.

Conclusion— We have developed a new approach for com-
puting vertex-corrected nonequilibrium correlation functions
using a contour-dependent virtual probe field and a matrix-free
QTT solver. Applied to nonequilibrium DMFT, the method
shows that vertex corrections dramatically reshape antiferro-
magnetic fluctuation dynamics and that both the fluctuation
amplitude and decay time are enhanced near the nonthermal
critical point.
The present formulation opens several promising directions.
For example, instead of scanning the probe time 𝑧𝑝, one can
treat 𝐺′(𝑧1, 𝑧2; 𝑧𝑝) as a single QTT with an extra leg for 𝑧𝑝
and obtain 𝐺′ for many 𝑧𝑝at once. The memory efficiency
of the QTT representation should become especially valuable
in multi-orbital and large-lattice systems, where the full nine-
component storage required by the generalized Keldysh for-
malism grows rapidly [69, 71, 72]. This makes our approach
promising for computing nonequilibrium spectra of realistic
correlated systems, including the experimental observables
probed in recent spectroscopic experiments [35, 36, 40].

As stated in the main text, the perturbative GCH method re-
