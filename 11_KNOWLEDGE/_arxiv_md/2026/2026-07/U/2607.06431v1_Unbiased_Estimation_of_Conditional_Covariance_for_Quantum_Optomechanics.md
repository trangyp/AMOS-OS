Unbiased Estimation of Conditional Covariance for Quantum Optomechanics
Katsuta Sakai1, 2 and Nobuyuki Matsumoto1, ∗
1Department of Physics, Faculty of Science, Gakushuin University, 1-5-1 Mejiro, Toshima, Tokyo 171-8588, Japan
2TRIP Headquarters, RIKEN, Wako 351-0198, Japan
Continuous measurements can prepare macroscopic mechanical oscillators in conditional quantum
states, but their covariance is difficult to verify. The conventional retrodictive estimator assumes
a forward–backward covariance symmetry and can be biased, because physical dynamics such as
feedback damping reduces the observability of the state from future records. Here, we derive an exact
linear-Gaussian estimator from causal, retrodictive, and smoothed trajectories. For a milligram-
scale mirror, it agrees with a Riccati prediction based on parameters fixed independently, while the
conventional estimate exhibits a large bias in the covariance-space metric, dM ∼5. Our method
paves the way toward unbiased testing of macroscopic entanglement within a calibrated linear-
Gaussian model, which will be applicable to tabletop mirrors as well as gravitational-wave kg-scale
test masses.
Introduction.—
Continuous
monitoring
turns
the
quantum back-action by measurement into a resource
for preparing and controlling mechanical motion. Offline
processing conditions the mechanical state on a detec-
tor record, whereas real-time processing uses the same
information to apply feedback and modify the uncondi-
tional dynamics [1–4]. By retaining each measurement-
conditioned trajectory instead of averaging over trajec-
tories with randomly evolving centers, conditioning re-
moves the apparent dephasing due to ensemble averag-
ing and exposes residual decoherence from unmonitored
channels [4]. Although model dependent, this reconstruc-
tion can reveal a localized, nearly pure state even when
the unconditional ensemble is strongly mixed [1, 4]. In
the linear Gaussian regime, the conditional mean obeys
a quantum Kalman–Bucy equation and the conditional
covariance a deterministic Riccati equation [1, 5].
For
Gaussian mechanical states, the covariance matrix certi-
fies squeezing, purity, and, for bipartite systems, entan-
glement.
Recently, conditional trajectories, mechanical squeez-
ing, sub-phonon conditioning, and near-ground-state
feedback cooling have been demonstrated across macro-
scopic optomechanical platforms [3, 4, 6, 7].
For sus-
pended interferometers, measurement-based entangle-
ment has been proposed for both kilogram-scale test
masses and milligram-scale pendulums [8, 9]. These ad-
vances make record-based preparation attractive for mas-
sive oscillators.
A central unresolved issue is how to verify the condi-
tional covariance directly from an experimental record.

Suspended-mirror experiment and data analysis.— The
data were obtained with the suspended-mirror cavity of
Ref. [20]. A triangular 1064-nm ring cavity contains a
7.71-mg mirror suspended from a silica fiber. A 30-mW
detuned field shifts its center-of-mass mode from 4.55 Hz
to about 280 Hz, and feedback stabilizes the optical an-
tidamping at an observed quality factor of about 250.
Most optical, mechanical, sensing, and feedback pa-
rameters entering the state-space model were determined
from auxiliary calibrations, including cavity scans, the
optical-spring curve, ring-down, and weighing measure-
ments. The detector output was converted to displace-
ment using the calibrated cavity-reflection slope, giving
a 12% one-sigma relative amplitude-scale uncertainty.
The segment-specific feff and Qeff were fitted only to
the ordinary displacement spectrum and were fixed be-
fore any trajectory-difference covariance was evaluated.
Given the fitted feff, the independently measured optical-
spring curve restricts the normalized detuning to two
branch candidates. A zero-crossing analysis of the band-
passed time trace selected the small-|δ| branch; the re-
sulting detuning was then held fixed as a model input.
Thus, all parameters entering the filters and the Ric-
cati comparison were fixed independently of the covari-
ance reconstruction; the detuning was not varied as an
independent spectral-fit parameter. No parameter was
adjusted to match the trajectory-difference covariances,
the reconstructed conditional covariance, or the Riccati
comparison.
Uncertainties include finite-record statis-
tics, calibration-scale uncertainty, and Monte Carlo prop-
agation of nuisance parameters fixed by independent cali-
brations and pre-reconstruction spectral assignments; for
Fig. 4 the nuisance contribution is evaluated from paired
experiment–model residuals. The details are summarized
in the Supplemental Material.

Discussion.— A central difficulty in covariance veri-
fication is that a quantum-scale conditional covariance
must be resolved from much larger record-derived or
model-derived fluctuations. A separate verification stage,
such as time-dependent homodyne tomography over re-
peated preparation–verification cycles [24], would be con-
ceptually least circular, but requires an independent ver-
ification measurement with sub-Heisenberg accuracy. In
model-conditioned spectral witnesses, such as the spin–
mechanical experiment [25], the inference can be viewed
in the present notation as a subtraction such as V =
Vu −Var(−→
x ), where Vu is the unconditional covariance.
This is valid, but it becomes ill conditioned when the
certified residual is of order ℏwhile the contributing co-
variances are set by a much larger thermal or classical oc-
cupation; for example, ¯nth ≃2 × 1010 for a 300-Hz mode
at 300 K. Feedback cooling relaxes this dynamic-range
requirement by reducing the unconditional motion, but
it also suppresses and broadens the mechanical spectral
feature from which fitted models are constrained. Purely
spectral subtraction is therefore sensitive to finite-record
fluctuations, calibration uncertainty, readout noise, and
model mismatch.
The two-trajectory protocol avoids assigning the con-
ditional covariance solely through such a fitted spec-
tral residual by using the empirical trajectory difference,
Var(−→
x −←−
x )/2 [4]. Its identification with the forward
conditional covariance, however, requires the symmetry
assumption V (E) ≃V . This assumption becomes frag-
ile precisely when feedback cooling is useful: closed-loop
damping, cavity detuning, sensing–back-action correla-
tions, and colored force noise modify how the state is
observable from future records.
In that case the con-
ventional two-trajectory estimator returns (V +V (E))/2,
rather than V , and the error is systematic; it cannot be
removed by increasing the record length. The covariance-
weighted statistics above reflect this distinction:
the
two-trajectory reconstruction is compatible with its own
finite-band biased prediction but not with the forward-
covariance target.
The
present
three-trajectory
estimator
keeps
the
record-based character of the two-trajectory protocol
while eliminating the unknown V (E) contribution alge-
braically with the smoothed trajectory. It is still condi-
tional on the calibrated linear-Gaussian model used to
construct the causal, future-likelihood, and smoothed fil-
ters, and is therefore not model-free tomography. It also
does not remove the generic statistical challenge of resolv-
ing an order-ℏcovariance from finite records. Its key ad-
vantage is that it does not require the forward–backward
symmetry V (E) ≃V . Thus feedback cooling can be used
to reduce the unconditional macroscopic motion without
invalidating the covariance estimator through a forward–
backward symmetry assumption.
The covariance re-
ported here is not an adjustable spectral parameter: it
is reconstructed from record-derived pairwise trajectory-
difference covariances using filters fixed by independent
calibrations, while the finite-band Riccati covariance re-
mains a calibrated prediction that need not agree with
the reconstruction.

Future en-
tanglement tests should combine covariance closure with
frequency-resolved agreement, held-out records or con-
trolled parameter changes, propagation of uncertainty
for the signed estimator, and checks of physicality, resid-

Under these
conditions and observability of the fixed model, Eq. (3)
can reconstruct a bipartite Gaussian covariance for en-
tanglement testing using the positive-partial-transpose
criterion [18, 19], providing a practical route to pro-
posed mirror and gravity-induced-entanglement experi-
ments [8, 9, 26].
Summary.— We have validated an unbiased three-
trajectory reconstruction of the conditional covariance
from a single continuous measurement record.
The
causal, future-likelihood, and smoothed trajectories re-
move the symmetry assumption V (E) ≃V , recover all
covariance elements, and accommodate feedback and col-
ored noise through state augmentation. For a milligram-
scale suspended mirror, the reconstruction agrees with
the independently constrained Riccati prediction, while
the conventional two-trajectory estimator shows the ex-
pected systematic mismatch from the forward-covariance
target. Since the covariance is obtained from pairwise
trajectory-difference statistics rather than assigned by
the Riccati equation, the agreement provides a falsifiable
covariance-closure test, supported by frequency-resolved
residuals with no resolved systematic deviation. Within
a calibrated linear-Gaussian model, a single noisy record
can therefore both condition a macroscopic oscillator
and verify its full forward covariance without assuming
forward–backward symmetry.

ORGANIZATION OF THE SUPPLEMENTAL MATERIAL
This Supplemental Material is organized into three main sections. Section S1 gives the theory of the three-trajectory
covariance-reconstruction method, deriving the identity that combines the causal forward trajectory, the future-
likelihood trajectory, and the smoothed trajectory. Section S2 summarizes the auxiliary calibrations and parameter
assignments used before covariance reconstruction, including the cavity scan, optical-spring calibration, operating-
detuning branch selection, displacement calibration, and noise-budget analysis. Section S3 defines the augmented
state-space model used for the covariance-verification analysis, including the continuous-time model, its sampled-
noise covariance, the forward and future filters, and the spectral reconstruction procedure.
The logical role of each part is as follows. Section S1 contains the estimator identity and is independent of the
details of the suspended-mirror implementation. Section S2 provides the fixed experimental inputs used by the filters
and by the Riccati comparison, and defines the propagated covariance used for the integrated covariance-component
consistency statistic. The calibration subsections determine numerical parameters, while the noise-budget subsection
provides independent consistency checks. Section S3 gives the covariance-closure pipeline. Sections S3.1–S3.3 define
the augmented state-space realization and sampled-noise covariances, whereas Sections S3.3.1 and S3.4 construct the
three trajectories and form the spectral covariance-closure statistic.

CALIBRATION AND NOISE-BUDGET
This section collects the calibration and parameter-assignment procedures that precede the covariance-
reconstruction analysis. Most optical, mechanical, sensing, and feedback parameters entering the state-space model
were calibrated by auxiliary measurements. The only segment-dependent quantities obtained from the selected main
displacement record before covariance reconstruction were the effective optical-spring resonance frequency feff, the
feedback-stabilized quality factor Qeff, and the cavity-detuning assignment. The segment-specific feff and Qeff were
fitted only to the ordinary displacement spectrum and were fixed before any trajectory-difference covariance was eval-
uated. Given feff, the independently calibrated optical-spring curve restricts the normalized detuning to two branch
candidates, whose numerical values for the operating point are given below; a zero-crossing analysis of the band-passed
time trace then selected the small-|δ| branch. All auxiliary calibrations and pre-reconstruction segment assignments
were fixed before any trajectory-difference covariance was evaluated. Thus all parameters entering the filters and the
Riccati comparison were fixed independently of the covariance reconstruction.
For clarity, Section S2 is not part of the covariance estimator itself. Its role is to make the inputs to the estimator
auditable and to define how their propagated uncertainties enter the covariance-component consistency checks. The
quantities that enter the filters are the fixed numerical parameters summarized in Table S1, the assigned detuning
branch, and the noise-source spectra used in the state-space model.
The calibration and pre-reconstruction parameter-assignment chain consisted of the following steps. First, cavity
scans were used to determine the finesse F, cavity linewidth κ, input-coupling ratio κin/κ, round-trip length Lrt, and
mode-matching ratio ηmm. Second, the optical-spring response was used together with the reflected-power calibration
to determine the optical-spring amplitude scale, the input power, the incidence angle β, and the optomechanical
coupling G. Third, the segment-specific feff and Qeff were fitted only to the ordinary displacement spectrum and
were fixed before any trajectory-difference covariance was evaluated.
Fourth, the fitted resonance frequency and
the independently calibrated optical-spring curve restricted the segment detuning to two branch candidates, whose
numerical values are reported in the operating-detuning and zero-crossing sections, and the zero-crossing analysis
selected the small-|δ| branch. Fifth, the mechanical quality factors of the mg-scale mirror and the heavier control
mirror were measured by pendulum ring-down. Finally, the mirror masses were measured with an electronic balance.

There are two distinct situations in which such a singular or ill-conditioned future-information matrix can appear.
The first is an augmented-state artifact. Auxiliary dynamical state variables, introduced for example to Markovianize
colored noise, may have negligible observable imprint on the measured record. This often manifests itself as a singular
or ill-conditioned Jnn in the chosen augmented-state coordinates. It can make the future likelihood flat in the auxiliary
directions even when the target variables themselves remain observable. This case should be handled by removing
unnecessary auxiliary variables before constructing the target-space future likelihood.
The second situation is a genuine target-space observability problem. It can occur, or be approached, when cor-
related force and readout noises destructively cancel in the measured in-loop record, so that the record carries little
information about a noise-driven component of the target motion. In that case the loss of information remains after
the auxiliary coordinates have been removed or marginalized. This possibility corresponds to a singular Jtar. In the
present experiment, the laser-frequency-noise channel is the relevant example of this possibility. A laser-frequency
fluctuation produces both a force-induced mirror displacement through the optical spring and an apparent displace-
ment in the cavity readout. These two terms are coherent components of the same noise source. If the residual
stabilized frequency noise were sufficiently large, cancellation between these components could reduce the information
carried by the in-loop displacement record about the frequency-noise-driven component of the target motion. This
channel is therefore the main residual observability caveat of the fixed-parameter model. In the present analysis,
however, the nominal boost-on residual frequency-noise contribution is subdominant in the verification band and is
not introduced as an additional fitted covariance degree of freedom. Because no independent out-of-loop measurement
of the stabilized residual frequency noise was available, this caveat is treated as a systematic observability limitation
rather than as an independently calibrated state-space noise contribution.
