ABSTRACT
O- and B-type stars are young and hot, thereby serving as vital tracers of the star formation and spiral arm structure of the Milky
Way. At the dusk of the Gaia DR3 era, a high-confidence and accurate catalogue appears timely. Here we have characterized a
population of 105,971 OB-type stars (Teff > 10,000 K; hereafter OB stars) within 2 kpc from the Sun, using an astro-photometric
Bayesian inference tool. Our resulting map unveils a complex view of the young stellar populations across the thin disk, with
prominent large-scale features such as the Cepheus Spur, the Giant Oval Cavity, and a segment of the Sagittarius-Carina spiral
arm all visible. Their inhomogeneous spatial distribution implies that massive star formation has taken place clustered across a
few highly concentrated regions. We find a correlation between the overdensities of OB stars and young open clusters (<20 Myr),
although OB stars can be better detected in high-extinction regions. We identify over 4200 OB stars as core-collapse supernova
(ccSN) or direct-collapse black hole (BH) progenitor candidates, and therefore targets of interest for spectroscopic follow-up.
Furthermore, we find no OB-type star ccSN progenitor to explode within the next 1 Myr within 100 pc, at which such an event
could be harmful to Earth’s biosphere. Finally, we identify more BH progenitors to collapse within the next 1 Myr than ccSN to
explode, despite the former’s much scarcer number - which could be indicative of a recent massive star formation burst in the
local Milky Way.
Key words: catalogues - stars: early-type - stars: massive - stars: distances - Galaxy: structure - Galaxy: solar neighbourhood
1 INTRODUCTION
Our Milky Way is a barred-spiral galaxy, whose structure and prop-
erties can be analysed in more detail than its distant/extragalactic
counterparts (e.g. Bland-Hawthorn & Gerhard 2016; Brown 2021),
and whose study is critical to better understand how disk galaxies
form and evolve (e.g. van der Kruit & Freeman 2011; Gaia Collab-
oration et al. 2023b). Within its main components, the spiral arms
are the drivers of the chemical, kinematic and energetic evolution of
galaxies (e.g. Romanelli et al. 2025). Nevertheless, from our internal
perspective, we are only able to map them out indirectly, through the
exploitation of tracers. Given that star formation in the Milky Way

Gaia’s ESA satellite (Gaia Collaboration et al. 2016) has revo-
lutionized our view of the Milky Way over the past decade. This is
particularly true in the solar neighbourhood (Zucker et al. 2023), with
the identification of important structures such as the Radcliffe Wave
(Alves et al. 2020), the Split (Lallement et al. 2019), the Cepheus
(Pantaleoni González et al. 2021) and Sagittarius (Kuhn et al. 2021)
spurs, superseding historical features such as the Gould Belt (Pan-
taleoni González et al. 2026). Amongst other breakthroughs, Gaia
data has enabled the compilation of all-sky catalogues of OB stars
larger by several orders of magnitudes compared with their historical
counterparts (e.g. Poggio et al. 2021; Zari et al. 2021; Pantaleoni
González et al. 2025), including from the Gaia consortium them-
selves as the golden sample of OBA stars from Gaia Collaboration
et al. (2023c).
In Quintana et al. (2025a; hereafter, Q25) we compiled a census
of ∼25,000 O- and B-type stars within 1 kpc to map out their spatial
distribution and various characteristics derived from photometry.
The objective was to produce a catalogue as accurate and complete
as possible, valuable for the scientific community. Most notably, this
catalogue provides targets for upcoming ground-based spectroscopic
follow-up such as the SCIP survey of the William Herschel Telescope
Enhanced Area Velocity Explorer (WEAVE, Jin et al. 2024) and the
4-metre Multi-Object Spectroscopic Telescope (4MOST, de Jong
et al. 2019).

In this work, we expand it up to a distance of 2 kpc. This corre-
sponds to an increase of the surface area covered of a factor of four,
which will allow us to start intercepting the Perseus and Sagittarius-
Carina arms (e.g. Reid et al. 2019; Zari et al. 2021) and therefore to
extract more accurate properties of the entire Milky Way. Mapping
out stellar populations down to late B-type stars enables us to dis-
entangle both the scale of OB associations as well as larger features
(spurs, superbubbles and spiral arms). The resulting catalogue can
then be compared with other Milky Way components identified and
catalogued with unprecedented accuracy with Gaia data, such as star
clusters (e.g. Hunt & Reffert 2023) and interstellar dust & gas (e.g.
Edenhofer et al. 2024; Kormann et al. 2026). Before the upcoming
release of Gaia DR4, which will notably benefit from astrometry
∼2.25 times more precise than Gaia DR3 (Brown 2019), this study
aims at improving our understanding of the structure, star formation
and ccSN explosion landscape within the local Milky Way, by com-
piling an accurate and complete census of OB stars within 2 kpc and
combining it with other catalogues.
This paper is divided as follows. In Section 2, we outline the
astro-photometric fitting method used to identify a population of
OB stars, and present the general results. In Section 3, we test the
validity of our catalogue by comparing the estimated physical pa-
rameters (effective temperatures, initial stellar masses) with external
spectroscopic and Gaia-based catalogues, and by crossmatching it
with other lists of OB(A) stars. In Section 4, we visualize the spatial
distribution of OB stars from our sample and study the structures
they trace, particularly focused on the spiral arms. In Section 5, we
compare OB-star density distribution with the star cluster catalogue
from Hunt & Reffert (2023, 2024) in order to analyse the model of
clustered star formation across the Milky Way’s thin disk. In Section
6, we identify and characterize OB-type star ccSN progenitors. Our
findings are summarized in Section 7.
2 IDENTIFYING OB STARS
2.1 Selection of candidate OB stars
Our selection process of candidate OB stars within 2 kpc is based
on astrometry and photometry from Gaia DR3 (Gaia Collaboration
et al. 2023a), which contains astrometric information for ∼1.5 billion
sources. Given the increasing uncertainties of the Gaia DR3 paral-
laxes with the distance (e.g. Quintana et al. 2023), as well as the
incompleteness arising from growing interstellar extinction towards
the Galactic Centre (e.g. Nogueras-Lara et al. 2019), we decided to
limit the sample to stars within 2 kpc. Furthermore, we stress that,
just like in Q25, we will not follow the sometimes-used definition of
an OB star, of stars of spectral type B2 and earlier for main-sequence
(dwarf) stars, B5 and earlier for giants and any O- and B-type stars
for supergiants (Morgan 1951; Pantaleoni González et al. 2025). In-
stead, we focus on stars with an effective temperature greater than
10,000 K, effectively including every main-sequence O- and B-type
star alongside blue supergiants (i.e., every star classified as "O" or
"B" according to the Harvard spectral classification, Pecaut & Ma-
majek 2013). Including late B-type stars notably allows us to better
estimate general stellar properties and fit the initial mass function.
To that end, we followed a similar approach as in Q25 where
we performed a cylindrical cut in order to reduce edge effects. We
have relied on the astroquery Python package (Ginsburg et al.
2019) to select Gaia DR3 sources that fell within a distance of

2.2 SED fitting
To estimate physical parameters of our candidate OB stars, we ap-
ply a spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting process, wherein we
compute a forward-modelled SED, based on the combination of stel-
lar atmosphere and evolutionary models with a 3D extinction map,
to an observed SED. We perform the fitting with the emcee Python
package (Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013) using a maximum-likelihood,
and free parameters of the initial mass log(𝑀/𝑀⊙), fractional age
Fr(Age) and distance 𝑑, alongside the additional uncertainty ln(f)
that effectively serves as an indicator of the quality of the fit. A
full description of the SED fitter is described in Q25, with the main
changes detailed below.
The observed SED combines Gaia DR3 parallax with photometry
from Gaia DR3, 2MASS, IGAPS DR1 and VPHAS+ DR2 (see Sec-
tion 2.1). Aside from the 𝑔, 𝑟and 𝑖bands from IGAPS and VPHAS+
that we already incorporated in Q25, we also include their U-band.
While the U-band from VPHAS+ does not require any specific cal-
ibration, the U-band from IGAPS does. The correction used in this
work is based on the method from de Burgos et al. (2025), which
consists of a slit cut around the point of largest density in the 𝑈−𝑔
vs 𝑔−𝑟colour-colour diagram, and was tailored to select target OB
stars for the WEAVE-SCIP-LR survey (de Burgos et al. 2025). Here
we apply this correction to every IGAPS field (consists of 5x5 deg2
areas from 𝑙= [30◦,215◦] and 𝑏= [−4◦,4◦]), and therefore leverages
all the IGAPS data within a given field, obtaining a list of corrections
for the U-band. This correction improves slightly our SED fitter as
the peak emission from OB stars is located in the near-UV domain,
though the U-band is generally satured for O-type stars within 2 kpc.
The model SED was constructed with the same stellar atmosphere
models as in Q25 at solar metallicity and log 𝑔= 4, with the BT-
NextGen models from Allard et al. (2012) for 𝑇eff = 3000 −5000
K, from the Kurucz models (Coelho 2014) for 𝑇eff = 6000 −20, 000
K and from the Tubingen Non Local Thermodynamical Equilib-
rium (NLTE) Model Atmosphere Package (Werner & Dreizler 1999;
Rauch & Deetjen 2003; Werner et al. 2003) for 𝑇eff = 21, 000 −
50, 000 K. Likewise, we still interpolate log(𝑇eff) and log(𝐿/𝐿⊙)
from the single stellar evolutionary models from Ekström et al.
(2012).
Finally, we constrain the visual extinction 𝐴𝑉as a function of
distance with the 3D extinction map from Edenhofer et al. (2024),
interpolating from the main map for sources closer than 1.25 kpc,
and from the reconstructed map for sources located between 1.25
and 2 kpc. To validate the usage of this reconstructed map, we have
tested in the highly-reddened Cyg OB2 (see Appendix A).

3 CATALOGUE VALIDATION
3.1 Completeness
3.1.1 Sources without G-band photometry
In Section 2.1, we excluded de facto all Gaia DR3 sources with
missing G-band photometry. They correspond to ∼0.3 % of the entire
Gaia DR3 catalogue, shared among stellar and extragalactic objects
(Gaia Collaboration et al. 2023a).
A notable cause of missing G-band photometry is saturation, as
it has been estimated that ∼20 % of stars with 𝐺< 3 mag are
missing from Gaia DR3 (Fabricius et al. 2021), an issue we have
compensated for by the inclusion of the missing OB stars from the
BSC, as mentioned in Section 2.3. Therefore, we argue that the
impact of missing G-band photometry is negligible, as this catalogue
includes all stars with 𝑉< 6 mag.
3.1.2 Interstellar extinction
Interstellar extinction can significantly dim the visual magnitude of
OB stars, becoming too faint to be detected by Gaia. Our SED-fitted
OB stars have a median and 1𝜎𝐺-band photometry of 11.5 mag
and 2.0 mag, respectively, far brighter than the magnitude limit of
𝐺≈21 mag (Riello et al. 2021). Most of the reddening across our
volume is relatively low (the 𝐴𝑉derived from Edenhofer et al. 2024
have median and 1𝜎values of 1.17 mag and 1.20 mag, respectively),
allowing us to map out the vast majority of the OB stars.
Our catalogue actually contains only ∼100 OB stars with 𝐴𝑉> 8
mag, and they are all are located within the Cyg OB2 region, which
is famous for its high extinction (𝐴𝑉= [5–10] mag for its population
of massive stars, see, e.g. Wright et al. 2015). Consequently, aside

3.2 Comparison with literature
We compare two critical aspects of our sample with the literature,
namely comparing the SED-fitted effective temperatures with spec-
troscopic catalogues in Section 3.2.1 and the SED-fitted initial stellar
masses with the open clusters catalogue from Hunt & Reffert (2024;
hereafter, HR24) in Section 3.2.2. However, see also Appendix C a
comparison of our sample with external catalogues of OB(A) stars.
3.2.1 Spectroscopically inferred temperatures
Our SED-fitted effective temperatures were estimated based on astro-
photometric data. To assess their quality, we compare them with
effective temperatures derived from spectroscopic measurements,
for all our 773,077 candidate OB stars within 2 kpc.
To that end, we have selected the following samples:
• The Gaia consortium estimated stellar physical parameters as

on the combination of the BP/RP spectra with the Radial Velocity

3.2.2 Photometry-inferred initial stellar masses
Another parameter estimated through our SED fitter is the initial
stellar mass. This value has also been derived for star cluster members
in Hunt & Reffert (2023, 2024), which we know has a strong overlap
with our OB stars (Q25) and OB associations within 1 kpc (Quintana
et al. 2026). Both our SED fitter and the method from Hunt & Reffert
(2024) assume that stars are single, making this comparison suitable.
To aid this comparison, we have selected the star clusters from
Hunt & Reffert (2023, 2024) with low-reddening (median A𝑉< 1
mag) and high-quality (cluster significance test, CST > 10), such that
the results from their isochrone fitting are more reliable. In doing so
we restricted their list to a subset of 919 clusters shared between
225,372 members, 10,550 of which successfully crossmatch with
our catalogue of candidate OB stars.
Fig. 4 shows a comparison between the initial stellar masses of
both our catalogues. Immediately apparent in this plot is a vertical
offset, as we underestimate log(𝑀/𝑀⊙) by a median value of 0.06
dex. This trend cannot be attributed to interstellar extinction (as
we have selected only low-reddening clusters from HR24). Instead,
given its uniformity across the HR diagram, we attribute this offset
to genuine methodological differences. Hunt & Reffert (2023) based
their calculations on the SPISEA code from Hosek et al. (2020), that
uses the PARSEC isochrones (Marigo et al. 2017), as well as the
ATLAS9 (Castelli & Kurucz 2003) and PHOENIX (Husser et al.
2013) stellar atmosphere models, that are different from the ones we
have used for our SED fitter (Section 2.2).

4.2 The Perseus arm
The Perseus arm is one of the main Milky Way spiral arms (see,
e.g., Churchwell et al. 2009). As seen in Fig. 7, within 2 kpc we
only start to intercept it (one of its tracers, W5, on the upper left
that corresponds to the Cassiopeia region, is mostly located beyond
our limit). This arm has been noticeably hard to map out in the
region known as the "Perseus Gap" that extends between the Auriga
and Cassiopeia regions (i.e., 𝑙= [140◦-170◦]), where few stellar and
molecular tracers have been identified (e.g., Marco & Negueruela
2016). This gap, also referred to as the "Giant Oval Cavity" and also
traced by dust and molecular clouds (Vergely et al. 2022; Wang et al.
2025), has been identified as the largest known superbubble of the
Milky Way, supported by the energy and momentum of the supernova
explosions it underwent (Chen et al. 2025).
Tracers of the Perseus arm at lower Galactic longitudes include the
Cassiopeia OB associations such as Cas OB5 aged 10–20 Myr (e.g.,
Quintana et al. 2025b), and Aur OB2 aged < 10 Myr (e.g., Quintana
et al. 2023), both mostly located beyond the edges of our map.
One of the most prominent overdensities is Gem OB2, located
on the third quadrant of Fig. 7. It was rediscovered by K24 and sits
right in the middle of the Perseus spiral arm fit from Reid et al.
(2019). Given that the position of Gem OB2 also matches with the
water masers at around 𝑙= 190◦from Choi et al. (2014), it is highly
probable that Gem OB2 is associated with a young massive star-
forming region, and thereby constitutes a tracer of this spiral arm.
Nevertheless, the contours shown on the right panel from Fig. 7
would suggest that Gem OB2 rather belongs to the Orion-Cygnus
arm. Further into the third Galactic quadrant, tracers of the Perseus
arm appear again (e.g., Vázquez et al. 2008), but are situated beyond
the map limits.
However, we should stress that the Perseus spiral arm fit from
Reid et al. (2019) has been questioned, particularly when it comes to
the Perseus Arm. Figure 14 from Gaia Collaboration et al. (2023b)
also displays the alternative model of this arm from Levine et al.
(2006) characterized by another pitch angle, wherein the Perseus
Gap becomes an inter-arm region (and so do all the tracers located
in the third quadrant, such as Gem OB2 and Aur OB2). We refer to
Appendix E where we compare the spatial distribution of OB stars
with alternative spiral arm models.

4.4 The Sagittarius-Carina arm
The Sagittarius-Carina arm is by far the most prominent feature of
our map, that we have highlighted by an ellipse. In particular, at
𝑙= [−30◦−30◦] sit several concentrations that likely correspond to
the Sagittarius, Scutum and Serpens OB associations and harbor the
famous Lagoon and Eagle Nebulae that are related to those associa-
tions (Wright et al. 2019; Wright 2020; Hunt & Reffert 2021; Stoop
et al. 2023). Another famous tracer of the Sagittarius-Carina arm
is the Carina Nebula complex that includes Car OB1 at 𝑙= 286.5◦
(Maíz Apellániz et al. 2020, 2022; Berlanas et al. 2023, 2025), located
slightly beyond the edge of our map alongside other overdensities of
OB stars at the fourth quadrant of Fig. 7, that likely correspond to the
Crux and Centaurus OB associations catalogued in Wright (2020).
The fit of this arm by Reid et al. (2019) reveals a slight offset
compared with our overdensities of OB stars. This was previously
reported by Zari et al. (2021), who concluded that the distribution
of young stars was more complex than simply following the spiral
arms. However, the luminous OBA stars from their catalogue could
live up to a few hundreds of Myrs, and this is also the case for the late
B-type stars in our catalogue, which could have arisen from this arm
before moving away from it. Another cause of the offset between the
fit from Reid et al. (2019) and our overdensities of OB stars could
stem from the uncertainties in the fit from Reid et al. (2019): in Fig.
7, we note a clear lack of masers in the fourth quadrant, where the
offset is the most pronounced.

6.1 The final fates of massive OB-type stars
6.1.1 Choice of SN model
We use the statistical SN model introduced in Maltsev et al. (2025;
hereafter, M25) to map out the final fates of massive OB stars in our
sample. M25 predict the outcome of iron-core collapse (successful
versus failed neutrino-driven SN explosion) in a massive star, given
its carbon-oxygen core mass, 𝑀CO, and metallicity, while distinguish-
ing between hydrogen-rich envelope retaining and envelope-stripped
ccSN progenitors. The resulting final fate landscape is characterized
by a bimodal structure. Stars are predicted to successfully explode
as a SN, unless their 𝑀CO is within a narrow band correlating with
failed SNe, and unless their 𝑀CO is above a high-mass threshold
representing a plateau of failed SN outcomes extending up to the
pair-instability mass gap (Woosley & Heger 2021). Recent gravi-
tational wave observations of binary BH mergers favour M25 over
several other competing SN models (Willcox et al. 2025).
To apply this model, we convert the 𝑀CO-thresholds for SN-BH
transitions for massive solar-metallicity single-stars using the (al-
most linear) 𝑀CO-to-𝑀ZAMS relation inherent in the stellar evolution
models from Schneider et al. (2021) and Temaj et al. (2024), that
underlay the construction of M25. The core-collapse SN model we
use is summarized in Table 2.

6.3.1 Age constraints on Cyg-OB2
Immediately apparent from Fig. 11 is the contrast between the 𝜏cc-

6.3.3 Neglected binarity - the case of *P Cyg
In addition, our method is afflicted with systematic uncertainties due
to the neglected binary interaction effects and to the specifics of
the adopted physics of the single-star evolutionary models we use
for parameter inference (e.g. the choice of a fixed rotation rate),
both of which influence the life expectancy and explodability of
massive stars. We consider an example to elucidate on how these act
in practice. Table 3 includes * P Cyg, an hypergiant luminous blue
variable, whose median ZAMS mass implies a direct collapse into
a BH, but whose error bars are much larger than the interval of 1.2
M!⊙failed ccSN in Table 2. Its upper error bar is so extended that it
is consistent its ZAMS mass surpassing the threshold to the plateau
of direct-collapse BH formation outcomes. Luminous supergiants
have large error bars on parameter estimates with the SED fitting
method, especially when they are highly extinguished and variable. In
previous work, * P Cyg has been predicted to be a candidate Type IIb
supernova progenitor (e.g. Groh et al. 2013), though with an inferred
ZAMS mass of 37 M⊙according to Rivet et al. (2020) (who used
a different set of single-star evolution and SN models), according to
the single-star SN-BH thresholds of M25. It would directly collapse
to a black hole. However, since * P Cyg is likely a (Case B) donor
transferring envelope mass to a binary companion (Mahy et al. 2022),
its M25-explodability is appropriately classified using the stripped-
star – not the single-star – prescription, for which the threshold
to a plateau of failed ccSN outcomes is 𝑀ZAMS ≃70 M⊙, for solar
metallicity progenitors (see their Fig. A.5). According to this scheme,
* P Cyg therefore will more likely explode at the end of its life.
We therefore trust our statistical estimates of 𝜏cc-distributions and
observationally inferred mean rates more than our predictions of
individual ccSN explosion or direct-collapse BH formation events.
We nonetheless encourage the community to analyze our list of the
next ccSN explosion and direct-collapse BH formation progenitor
candidate star observations with detailed binary evolution models to
draw more faithful conclusions on their fates and associated waiting
times.
7 CONCLUSIONS
In this study, we have identified a population of 105,971 O- and
B-type stars within 2 kpc of the Sun, applying an astro-photometric
(SED fitting) Bayesian inference technique to estimate their stellar
physical parameters. As the Gaia DR3 era comes to an end, this cat-
alogue therefore provides high-confidence targets for spectroscopic
follow-up.
Comparing our census with various spectroscopic surveys, we
have a tendency of slightly underestimating effective temperatures
(except for APOGEE DR17 where we observe the reverse trend).
Furthermore, for the sample of OB stars that also is in HR24, we
have lower log(𝑀/𝑀⊙) by a median value of 0.06 dex, an offset we
attribute to differences in adopted stellar evolution and atmosphere
models.
This catalogue benefits from a high-level of completeness, both at
the bright end thanks to the inclusion of the Bright Stars Catalogue
to compensate for the saturation in Gaia DR3, and at the faint end
except for the latest B-type stars in regions of extreme reddening such
as Cyg OB2, validating the reconstructed (i.e., between 1.25 and 2
kpc) version of the Edenhofer2024 extinction map.
Our map of OB stars unveils a complex view of the young stellar
populations across the Galactic thin disk, with a sharp contrast be-

star-forming regions such as Ori OB1, Mon OB1, Gem OB2 and
Cepheus. Prominent large-scale overdensities include a significant

the spiral arm location and width, but further work needs to be carried
out in that direction, particularly in line of the different spiral arm
models using different tracers, with currently no clear consensus.
Our catalogue serves as a tracer of star formation in the local Milky
Way complementary to that of the young open clusters from Hunt &
Reffert (2023, 2024), with OB associations hosted in OCs at a relative
probability of ∼10 %, and our bright OB stars allowing us to penetrate
deeper into the high-extinction regions. But while the derived surface
density star formation rates in open clusters (ΣSFR,OC) are larger in
the Galactic Centre directions compared with the Galactic Anticentre
directions, the large error bars prevents us from confidently conclud-
ing that about star formation decreases towards the Outer Galaxy
due to exponential drop in gas density, as predicted by star formation
models of disk galaxies. More accurate censuses of the population
of OB stars and open clusters will allows us to solve these questions.
Aside from the improved astrometry that will be delivered by the
two last data releases from the Gaia mission, its proposed successor,
GaiaNIR, could help us to pierce through the high-extinction regions
towards the Galactic Centre (e.g. Nogueras-Lara et al. 2019). Indeed,
GaiaNIR is set to observe ∼50 billion stars in the near-infareed, and
will therefore resolve currently-hidden populations of OB stars and
open clusters in that direction (Hobbs & Høg 2018; Hobbs 2024),
helping us to better understand star formation in the Milky Way.
Beyond tracing the structure of the local Milky Way, our OB star
catalogue can serve as a valuable tracer of (massive) star formation.
Leveraging a recently introduced supernova model, we have iden-
tified 3998 ccSN explosion and 233 direct-collapse BH progenitor
candidate OB stars within 2 kpc, providing a first picture of a potential
landscape of upcoming ccSN explosions and BH formation events in
the local Milky Way. Based on the rotating single-star evolutionary
models from Ekström et al. (2012), we identify more BH progenitors
to collapse within the next 1 Myr than ccSN progenitors to explode.
As our observationally-inferred ccSN rates are much higher than the
local ccSN rates derived from the 1 kpc census of OB stars on a
timescale of several hundreds of Myrs from Q25, this favours a re-
cent episode of nearby massive star formation having taken place, in
line with the results from Zari et al. (2023). This also corroborates
the time variability of the number of ccSNe occurring per unit time,
which is tied to physical conditions rather than more or less strictly
following a constant rate throughout long timescales.
We also do not find any of the OB stars to explode within 1 Myr
at a distance of 100 pc (the upper distance limit at which long-term
cosmic ray emission triggered by a nearby ccSN explosion could
be harmful to the Earth’s atmosphere). We will further explore this
question in Paper II (Quintana, Maltsev et al., in prep.), where we
will provide updated estimates of the star formation, core-collapse
supernova and black hole formation rates compared with Q25.

APPENDIX A: INTERSTELLAR EXTINCTION IN CYG
OB2
Compared with Q25, our SED fitting process summarized in Section
2.2 utilises the reconstructed version of the 3D dust map from Eden-
hofer et al. (2024) between 1.25 and 2 kpc, whose precision is lower
than for the main map below 1.25 kpc.
To validate the exploitation of this version, we turn to Cyg OB2, as
it is one of the most extinguished regions of the volume covered by our
census (e.g. Wright et al. 2015), and was the subject of a dedicated
target study for a previous version of the SED fitter (Quintana &
Wright 2021). We have thus crossmatched our catalogue of SED-
fitted OB stars as well as the Cygnus OB association members from
Quintana & Wright (2021) with the catalogue of massive OB stars
in Cyg OB2 from Wright et al. (2015) and the subset of O-type stars
from Berlanas et al. (2020), both of which include spectroscopic
measurements of A𝑉for individual Cyg OB2 members.
In Fig. A1 is displayed the comparison between these measure-
ments. Immediately apparent is the net improvement between the red-
dening values estimated in Quintana & Wright (2021) and the present
study. The A𝑉values from Quintana & Wright (2021) are nearly con-
sistently under-estimated compared with spectroscopy. They were
interpolated from the Bayestar2019 3D dust map from Green et al.
(2019), which were found to be under-estimated by a factor of ∼22
% in Delchambre et al. (2023), a correction applied in the improved
version of the SED fitter in Quintana et al. (2023).
By contrast, our A𝑉values from this study -(interpolated from
Edenhofer2024) align much better with spectroscopy: although
they are slightly over-estimated, they agree within the error bars.
The comparison from Fig. A1 thereby validates the usage of the re-
constructed version of the Edenhofer2024 dust map for the latest
version of the SED fitter.

within 2 kpc.
Our catalogue includes 37,756 stars from Z21. This is a similar

log(𝑇eff) of 4.11 for our 37,756 stars in common), suggesting that

included in Z21. Likewise, 50,529 out of the 66,369 remaining stars

SED-fitted OB stars within 2 kpc because they were colder (A-type)

Perseus Gap and the Sagittarius-Carina arm.

only in our catalogue are in the general golden sample of OBA stars
from G23.

catalogues in a Gaia CMD (Fig. B1 from Q25). We argue that a
similar selection bias could be present here, as the distribution from

APPENDIX E: COMPARISON WITH OTHER SPIRAL
ARM MODELS
In Section 4, we compared the spatial distribution of our OB stars
with the spiral arm model from Reid et al. (2019) and the contours
shaped by the Gaia OB stars from Gaia Collaboration et al. (2023b).
We stress there that there is currently no clear consensus on the
large-scale spiral structure of the Milky Way, and several models are
available in the literature.
Here we will therefore compare our spatial distribution of OB stars
with other models of the spiral arms. To that end, we take advantage
of the SpiralMap module from Python (Prusty & Khanna 2025),
from which we choose the following models:
(i) Drimmel_Ceph_2024 is the model from Drimmel et al.
(2025), based on a sample of 2857 dynamically young Cepheids.
(ii) Hou_Han_II_2014 is the model from Hou & Han (2014), that
leveraged 900 masers alongside 1300 GMCs and 2500 Hii regions.
(iii) Taylor_Cordes_1992 is the model from Taylor & Cordes
(1993) that also exploited known Hii regions to map out the spiral
arm structure of the Milky Way.
(iv) Vallee_1995 is the model from Vallee (1995) that combines
magnetic field data with interstellar dust & gas as well as stars.
All these models have been plotted on top of our nomalised sur-
face density map of OB stars in Fig. E1. Effectively, they present a
complementary view of the structure of the local Milky Way, with
individual spiral arms characterized by different pitch angles. While
Taylor_Cordes_1992, Vallee_1995 and Drimmel_Ceph_2024
all have four arms, Hou_Han_II_2014 is characterized by a number
of six arms.
Out of these four models, only Drimmel_Ceph_2024 includes the
local (Orion-Cygnus) arm, but is also the model deviating the most
from our overdensities of OB stars, with the Perseus arm beyond the
coverage of our sample and the Orion-Cygnus not crossing Cyg OB2,
