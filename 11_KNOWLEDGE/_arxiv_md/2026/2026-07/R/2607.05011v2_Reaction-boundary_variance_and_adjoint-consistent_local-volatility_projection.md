Reaction-boundary variance and adjoint-consistent local-volatility projection
Chris Angstmanna, Tim Gebbieb
aSchool of Mathematics and Statistics, University of New South Wales, Sydney, NSW 2052, Australia

Abstract
We derive an operational-time variance kernel for a latent-order-book reaction boundary and use it to separate three
objects usually collapsed in calendar-time volatility models: a structural boundary cumulant, a clock projection, and
a pricing-measure choice. The reaction boundary is the zero of a bid–ask imbalance field. For a locally linear book,
signed order-flow perturbations displace this zero through a damped Abel response kernel, so the variance of bound-
ary increments is obtained as a finite-scale Green-function cumulant rather than introduced as a primitive diffusion
coefficient. For long-memory forcing with exponent 0 < γ < 1, the operational variance has a closed asymptotic form
involving effective signed-forcing intensity, liquidity slope, resilience, memory, and operational coarse-graining scale. A
deterministic activity clock gives the benchmark local-volatility projection. More general, non-unique clocks generate
candidate calendar-time pricing systems. We argue that such projections are admissible only when the induced forward
density operator and backward valuation operator remain adjoint on the same state space. Adjoint consistency is there-
fore a reality constraint on operational-to-calendar time projection: it disciplines non-unique time and identifies where
incompleteness enters.
Keywords:
operational time, reaction boundary, local volatility, non-unique time, adjoint operators, market
incompleteness, latent order book

1. Introduction
Volatility is often introduced at the level of a calendar-
time diffusion, either postulated directly as in Black–
Scholes–Merton or inferred from option prices in local-
volatility models [5, 29, 18, 22]. The inference viewpoint
is also linked to state-price recovery from option prices,
where cross-strike curvature carries information about
risk-neutral densities [9]. Here we reverse this order. We
first compute a mesoscopic transport coefficient for a re-
action boundary in a latent order book. Only after this
operational-time coefficient is obtained do we discuss its
projection into calendar time.
The reaction boundary is the log-price location at
which a local bid–ask imbalance field changes sign. For a
locally linear latent book, small imbalance perturbations
are converted into boundary displacements by the inverse
local book slope.
Signed order-flow perturbations are
propagated to the boundary by the same damped Abel
response kernel that appears in latent-liquidity accounts
of concave market impact [7, 32, 28, 16].
However, the
object computed here is not the mean impact path. It is
the second cumulant of the boundary displacement over a
finite operational scale.

The construction combines latent-order-book response,
long-memory signed forcing, and forward–backward ad-
joint consistency. The result is a structural decomposition
of local volatility into boundary variance, activity, and
pricing-measure choice.

8. Relation to local volatility
The deterministic-clock PDE in Eq. (26) has the same
algebraic form as a local-volatility pricing equation. The
5


## Page 6


interpretation is different.
In a standard local-volatility
model the coefficient is specified directly in calendar time
or inferred from option prices. Here it is the projection of
an operational reaction-boundary cumulant, and the op-
erational kernel can be discussed before any calendar-time
clock is selected.
The asymptotic form of Ξ is what makes the adjoint
condition concrete.
Non-unique time does not change
the Green-function cumulant itself: it changes how the
scale-dependent kernel, with its explicit ∆−γFγ(ν∆) de-
pendence, is sampled and conditioned in calendar time.
Thus two clocks can agree on a scalar projected variance at
a point while implying different forward laws once hidden
activity, filtration, or clock-risk information is taken into
account. The local-volatility equation should therefore be
read as an admissible projection of the asymptotic kernel,
not as a primitive replacement for the operational model.
The construction is also distinct from a standard time-
changed process. A standard time-change model specifies
a deterministic or stochastic clock and then studies the
induced calendar-time process [6, 12, 11]. Here the clock
is part of the modelling ambiguity.
The family U con-
tains candidate projections, whereas Ad(U) contains only
those projections that preserve the forward–backward ad-
joint relation needed for pricing.
This places the clock
choice closer to a model-uncertainty problem than to a
purely parametric calibration problem [15, 13].
Finally, the adjoint-real condition is not the same
as a Markovian projection or marginal mimicking re-
sult.
Markovian projection asks whether a possibly
non-Markovian calendar-time process can be represented
by a Markov process with matching marginal distribu-
tions [25, 10].
The present question is earlier:
given
an operational kernel, which projections into calendar
time produce mutually consistent forward and backward
operators?
The answer is not supplied by the variance
projection alone.
It is supplied by adjoint consistency.
Thus the asymptotic form of the boundary kernel makes
the candidate local variance explicit, while the adjoint
condition decides whether this candidate can live on the
proposed state space as a pricing model.
9. Discussion and limitations
Here we provide a simple analytic bridge from mi-
crostructure response to local volatility. It uses the same
Green-function response that explains concave impact
[32, 28, 16]; but computes a second cumulant rather
than a deterministic impact path.
The microstructural
input also sits in the broader order-flow-memory and
latent-liquidity literature, where persistent signed flow,
hidden-order splitting, slow liquidity digestion, and im-
pact invariance are treated as structural market features
rather than exogenous volatility factors [27, 8, 7, 3]. The
deterministic projection gives a local-volatility coefficient
in the usual pricing PDE. The non-unique-time extension
leaves the operational kernel unchanged and moves model
uncertainty into the projection layer.
The construction
should therefore be read as a structural closure rather
than an empirical volatility model.
This distinction is
important.
What is derived is the form of a mesoscopic opera-
tional variance kernel once the local book slope, resilience,
signed-forcing intensity, memory exponent, and opera-
tional coarse-graining scale are specified. The calculation
is local:
these quantities are frozen over the response
window, and the latent book is linearized near the reac-
tion boundary. The finite-scale identity should therefore
be distinguished from its zero-cutoff asymptotic branch.
The asymptotic formula is a clean low-frequency sum-
mary of the mechanism, not a claim that the full latent
book remains linear during large dislocations or that the
microscopic cutoff τ0 can be neglected at every tenor or
sampling scale.
Applications near the microstructural
scale, or to very short-dated instruments, should return
to the finite-cutoff spectral representation rather than
treating the asymptotic prefactor as universal.
The relation to the broader non-unique-time discussion
is therefore specific. The present paper does not require a
full operational-time lattice construction, a full meta-order
impact model, or a multivariate correlation model. It uses
only the local linear response of the boundary, the forcing
covariance, and the clock projection. The broader motiva-
tion for treating clock choice as a source of incompleteness
is discussed in [1]; the derivation here is self-contained.
We do not assert that the physical signed-order-flow
covariance is itself the risk-neutral covariance, nor that
the power-law spectrum motivated by order splitting is
an untempered memory law at all horizons [27, 8, 7]. A
finite-lifetime or tempered memory scale is a natural next
extension, but it is not part of the closed-form result es-
tablished here. Likewise, adjoint consistency should not be
read as guaranteeing that every activity clock gives a one-
state local-volatility model. If the clock, liquidity state, or
projected variance retains hidden information, the correct
adjoint-real representation may require state enlargement;
otherwise the scalar coefficient is only a formal projection
[15, 13, 1].
The numerical surfaces should therefore be
read as dimensionless structural diagnostics of the kernel,
not as calibrated volatility-surface fits (See Appendix C).
Instead, the paper identifies the structural objects that
must be transformed, calibrated, or projected before the
kernel can be used for pricing. In this sense the formula is
most useful as a disciplined decomposition of local volatil-
ity into liquidity response, signed-flow memory, activity,
and measure choice, and makes concrete the role of time
non-uniqueness in discrete event-driven systems.
10. Conclusion
In summary, what we have derived is a coarse-grained
local variance coefficient from the linear response of a re-
action boundary in a latent order book. The key result is
Eq. (20). It expresses the variance of boundary increments
6


## Page 7


in terms of directly interpretable structural quantities: the
effective signed-forcing intensity Aeff, the local liquidity
slope L, the resilience ν, the long-memory exponent γ,
and the operational coarse-graining scale ∆.
In simple physical terms, stronger signed forcing in-
creases boundary variance, while a steeper latent book
suppresses boundary motion through the inverse-square
factor 1/L2. Long-memory order flow controls the scale
dependence through ∆−γ, and resilience enters through
the response function Fγ(ν∆), filtering the contribution
of persistent forcing according to the ratio between the
book relaxation scale and the measurement scale.
Equation (25) then gives the deterministic-clock bench-
mark:
calendar-time local variance is the operational
boundary variance multiplied by the activity rate α(t).
Thus activity does not alter the Green-function response
kernel; it determines how rapidly operational fluctuations
are sampled in calendar time.
Finally, Eq. (29) gives the homogeneous closed form,
where the structural quantities are frozen. This limit is
not meant to be a complete empirical model, but it is
the clearest summary of the mechanism: activity ampli-
fies variance, signed forcing supplies fluctuations, liquid-
ity absorbs them quadratically, resilience damps persistent
memory, and the memory exponent determines how the
observed variance changes with coarse graining. The non-
unique-time extension preserves this operational cumulant
and moves incompleteness, or clock uncertainty, into the
projection from operational to calendar time.
The mathematical message is simple.
The Green-
function calculation determines an operational variance
kernel under explicit local and asymptotic assumptions.
The local-volatility pricing interpretation then requires a
clock and a pricing measure. If the clock is unique and
deterministic, the result reduces to an activity-rescaled
local-volatility coefficient. If the clock is non-unique, the
admissible projections are those that preserve the adjoint
relation between valuation and density evolution. This is
where the microstructure calculation meets mathematical
finance: the order book supplies the operational cumu-
lant, but pricing requires an adjoint-real calendar-time
representation.

Appendix C.5. Interpretation
The plotted object is best interpreted as a projected
structural volatility surface. It is local-volatility-like be-
cause it assigns a volatility level to a state coordinate and
maturity, but it is not a calibrated Dupire local volatility
surface. A calibrated local-volatility surface is usually in-
ferred from option prices under a risk-neutral pricing mea-
sure and must be consistent with the absence of static ar-
bitrage in the associated option surface. The present con-
struction instead starts from order-book structural quan-
tities and projects them into a surface morphology.
This distinction matters for interpretation. The sim-
ulation is designed to ask whether plausible order-book
channels can generate volatility-surface shapes that resem-
ble known market phenomenology, especially equity-like
downside skew. It does not claim that the resulting sur-
face is directly tradeable, arbitrage-free, or equivalent to a
market-calibrated local volatility input.
For comparison with option-calibrated surfaces, the
state coordinate m should be read as a log-moneyness co-
ordinate. In a later empirical comparison, one would nor-
mally align this with log-strike or log-forward moneyness,
for example m = ln(K/S0) or m = ln(K/F0(T)), depend-
ing on the calibration convention. The current notation
m = ln(x/x0) keeps the connection to the traded-price
level visible, while the plotted surface should be compared
only qualitatively with calibrated local volatility.
Within that qualitative interpretation, the combined
structural high-z case is the most market-like of the
first simulations. It combines thinner downside liquidity,
weaker downside resilience through z(m) = ν(m)∆, and
stronger downside forcing. The low-z resilience-response-
only case is mainly diagnostic: at fixed γ, the leading
low-z approximation does not depend on z, so varying
z(m) alone cannot generate leading state dependence in
that branch.

Appendix C.8. Limitations
The simulations are illustrative. They do not estimate
L(m), ν(m), Aeff(m), γ(m), or α(T) from market data.
The state dependence of the plotted surfaces is selected
through declared phenomenological profiles:
inverse-
square liquidity effects, response dependence through
z(m) = ν(m)∆, and a stylised downside forcing scale.
The role of the figures is therefore to make the theory
visually testable and to clarify which structural channels
can plausibly produce surface morphology before any
empirical calibration is attempted.
