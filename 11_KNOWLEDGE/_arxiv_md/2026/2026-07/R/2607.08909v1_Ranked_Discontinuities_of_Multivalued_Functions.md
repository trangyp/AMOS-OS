Ranking Locally Discontinuous Points of Multivalued Functions
Daniel S. Mourad
July 13, 2026
Abstract
We study discontinuity of multivalued functions (also known as problems) P on Baire space
by assigning an ordinal rank to points in the domain of P that have no local realizers. For each
countable ordinal α, let ACCα
N be the problem of solving ACCN in at most α many attempts, with
a new instance provided for each attempt. Our main theorem shows that, for any problem P,
the following are equivalent: (i) P is discontinuous on some set all of whose points have Cantor–
Bendixson rank at most α, and (ii) ACCα
N ≤∗
W P. This extends to points: P ≥∗
W ACCα
N via a
forward function that sends #N to p ∈dom(P) if and only if P is discontinuous on a set A with
rankA(p) ≤α. We also characterize these properties via a Wadge-style discontinuity game for P.
We apply this framework to the thin set and achromatic Ramsey theorems. Extending RTn
k,j
to ordinal parameters, we define RTn
α,β and compute their ranks of discontinuity. The problems
RTn
α,β provide examples of problems with discontinuities of each countable rank which are not
reducible to ACCN. The separation from ACCN is obtained via the notion of guessability with
identified errors.

1.1
Summary of Main Results
It is well known that transfinite ordinals can be used to represent the number of attempts in which an
agent promises to complete a task (see, e.g., [1, 11, 18, 23]) in the following sense. If an agent promises
to complete a task in at most α many attempts, then if they fail their next attempt they will provide a
new ordinal β < α and subsequently promise to complete the task in at most β many attempts. Pauly
and Sold´a [29] show that solving the problem ACCN in one attempt is the least difficult (in the sense of
continuous Weihrauch reducibility ≤∗
W) discontinuous task whose instances all have Cantor-Bendixson
rank ≤1. In this paper, we show that there is a ≤∗
W-least discontinuous task whose instances all have
Cantor-Bendixson rank ≤α; namely, the task of solving ACCN in at most α many attempts, in the
sense that a new instance of ACCN is given for each attempt. We refer to this task as ACCα
N.
Theorem 1. Let P :⊂NN ⇒NN be a problem, and let α be a countable ordinal. The following are
equivalent.
1. There is a set A ⊂NN such that P|A is discontinuous and each point p ∈A has Cantor-Bendixson
rank rankA(p) ≤α.
2. ACCα

Weihrauch degrees. Indeed, it immediately follows from Theorem 1 and Pauly and Sold`a’s result that
for any first order problem P we have P >∗

our ACCα

We also obtain a local account of how points of discontinuity (i.e., points with no local realiz-

The rank-α discontinuity game for P is similar to the Wadge game for P in that players adversarially

ranking.

1.2
Problems with Trivial First Order Part
Dzhafarov, Solomon and Yokoyama [15] introduce the notion of the first order part of a problem. We
provide the definition below.
Definition 1.2.1. Let P :⊂NN ⇒NN be a problem. We define the first order part of P, 1P, to be the
problem with instances dom(1P) = {(p, Ψ) : p ∈dom(P) and Ψ :⊂NN →N is a continuous function}
and solutions 1P(p, Ψ) = {Ψ(q) : q ∈P(p)}.
Essentially, the first order part of a problem P is the problem of, given an instance p of P, to
output a sufficiently long initial segment of a P-solution to p, with the length determined by Ψ.
Dzhafarov, Solomon and Yokoyama show that the first order part of P is Weihrauch reducible to
any problem below P whose codomain is N. As previously mentioned, Pauly and Sold´a [29] show that
ACCN is Weihrauch reducible to every problem with non-trivial first order part. Hence, the entirety of
our ACCα
N hierarchy is below every problem with non-trivial first order part.

Sold`a and Valenti show that the set of problems with trivial first order part forms a sublattice of
the Weihrauch degrees [31, cf. Proposition 4.1]. We can view Theorems 1 and 2 as providing some

1.2.1
Problems Which Have no Rank-α Discontinuities for Any Countable α
In light of Theorem 2, the class of problems whose discontinuities all have rank ∞is a natural object
of consideration. Equivalently, this is the class of problems that are continuous on any scattered subset
of their domain. It turns out that this is a relatively weak condition, being implied by several separate
notions which are already present in the literature.
In the process of performing Weihrauch analysis on classical computability theory, Brattka, Hendt-
lass, and Kreuzer [6] study densely realized problems, those problems whose solution sets are always
dense in NN. Since Player 1 can only win the rank-α discontinuity game for a problem P if P has an
instance with no solution above a certain ρ ∈N<N, densely realized problems are continuous on any
scattered subset of their domain by Theorem 2.
Hirschfeldt and Jockusch [22] introduce the notion of undiagonalizable problems, those problems for
which there is a uniform algorithm for determining whether a particular σ ∈N<N is an initial segment
of a solution to a given instance.
Dzhafarov, Solomon and Yokoyama show that undiagonalizable
problems have trivial first order part. In Theorem 4.2.11, we strengthen this result to show that they
are continuous on any scattered subset of their domain.
Hirschfeldt and Jockusch also introduce the contrasting notion of a problem having diagonaliza-
tion opportunities. A problem P being undiagonalizable implies that P does not have diagonalization
opportunities, but the converse does not hold. While we have not obtained a direct connection be-
tween having diagonalization opportunities and having a rank-α discontinuity for some countable α
(and indeed, having diagonalization opportunities is not Weihrauch invariant so such a connection
would not be straightforward), it turns out that having diagonalization opportunities characterizes a
slight strengthening of Player 1 having a winning strategy in the rank-α discontinuity game for some
countable α. By allowing Player 2 to play any q ∈NN instead of only playing solutions to the most
recent instance of P given by Player 1, we obtain a game in which Player 1 having a winning strategy
is equivalent to P having diagonalization opportunities. We discuss this in more detail in Section 6.1.
The class of problems with trivial first order part which are also continuous on all scattered subsets
of their domain also contains the very bottom of the discontinuous Weihrauch degrees, assuming
enough determinacy.
Brattka [3] shows that, assuming the determinacy of the Wadge game, the
discontinuity problem DIS is the least discontinuous problem in the Weihrauch degrees. Since DIS is
densely realized, for all countable ordinals α we have that DIS does not have a rank-α discontinuity and
that DIS <W ACCα
N. Furthermore, in a construction relying on the axiom of choice, Brattka provides
examples of problems strictly below DIS. By Corollary 4.2.14, any such problems also do not have any
rank-α discontinuities for any countable α and hence are also part of this class.

1.2.2.2
Achromatic Ramsey Theorems for Ordinal-Valued Numbers of Colors
The
achromatic Ramsey theorem was introduced by Erd˝os, Hajnal and Rado [16]. Its reverse mathematical
analysis was proposed by Joe Miller (see [24, Section 2.2.3]) and studied by Wang [33] in the context
of reverse mathematics.
We extend the definition of RTn

colors in the solution to be ordinal-valued. For each ordinal β < ω1 and each α ∈{N} ∪ω1 we define
the problem RTn

If α ∈ω1, then instances are colorings c : [N]n →N coupled with a decreasing sequence of ordinals
strictly less than α whose decreases are in one-to-one correspondence with each new color appearing
in c([N]n). The set of solutions to such an instance is an infinite set A ⊂N coupled with a decreasing
sequence of ordinals strictly less than β whose decreases are in one-to-one correspondence with each
new color appearing in c([A]n). For both the instances and solutions, once the sequence reaches 0, no
new colors can appear. Then, TSn

obtain specific information on which cones cannot be avoided when those conditions are not met.

with discontinuities of each countable rank which are not reducible to ACCN, and in particular are not
equivalent to ACCα

Let X and Y be spaces and let A ⊂X and B ⊂Y . We use letters P and Q to denote partial

We use Greek letters α, β, and γ to denote ordinals. We use ω for the least infinite ordinal and

For an ordinal α, we identify ordinals with the set of their

account.

4.1

In computable analysis, continuity of multivalued functions between represented spaces is defined in
terms of the existence of continuous realizers (see Section 2). This is a global notion of continuity. We

Ranking Discontinuities and the Discontinuity Game
The phenomenon observed in Lemma 4.1.6 is a special case of Theorem 4.1.7 in the following sense.
Suppose that P|[Jτ0K × Jρ0K] is one of the witnesses that a point a0 is a discontinuity of P as in
Condition 2 of Theorem 4.1.7. By Theorem 4.1.5, we have that P|[Jτ0K × Jρ0K] also has a point of
discontinuity a1. It is possible that P(a1) ∩Jρ0K is empty, as in Lemma 4.1.6. If P(a1) ∩Jρ0K is
nonempty, then we may iteratively apply Theorem 4.1.7 to obtain sequences a2, a3, . . . and ρ2, ρ3, . . .
as long as each ai has a solution which is contained in Jρi−1K. If there is no such solution and the pair
of sequences cannot be extended, then we are in the case of Lemma 4.1.6. We assign a rank to a point
of discontinuity based on how long such a sequence can be extended.
Definition 4.2.1. Let P :⊂NN ⇒NN be a problem and let p ∈dom(P) be a discontinuity of P. We
assign a countable ordinal rank of discontinuity to p as follows.
• If P(p) is empty then we say that p is a rank-0 discontinuity of P.
• For countable α > 0, p is a rank-α discontinuity of P if α is the least ordinal such that for all
q ∈P(p) there is ρ ⊏q such that for all σ ⊏p there exists p′ ∈dom(P) ∩JσK with p′ ̸= p such
that p′ is a rank-β discontinuity of P|[JσK × JρK] for some β < α.
• If there is no such ordinal α then we say that p is a rank-∞discontinuity of P.
For single valued functions f ⊂NN →NN, Definition 4.2.1 collapses to only three levels (see
Section 4.3), but for multivalued functions, there can be discontinuities of arbitrarily high countable
rank.
For each countable ordinal α, the property of being a rank-α discontinuity can be characterized by
a two player game, which we call the rank-α discontinuity game.
Definition 4.2.2 (Rank-α Discontinuity Game). Let P :⊂NN ⇒NN be a problem and let α be a
countable ordinal. We recursively define the rank-α discontinuity game for P (or the “α-P game” for
short) as the two player game with the following steps.
Step 1. Player 1 first chooses some p ∈dom(P).
Step 2. If P(p) is empty, then the game is concluded and Player 1 wins. Otherwise, Player 2 plays some
q ∈P(p).
Step 3. At this time, if α = 0 and Player 1 has not already won then the game is concluded and Player 2
wins. Otherwise, Player 1 next plays some initial segment ρ ⊏q.
Step 4. In response, Player 2 plays some initial segment σ ⊏p.
Step 5. Player 1 then chooses a γ < α (which is possible since the prior Step 3 ensures that α > 0) and
play proceeds by starting the rank-γ discontinuity game for P|[JσK×JρK]. The rank-α game ends
when the rank-γ game ends, with the same winner.
We refer to Steps 1–5 as a round of the game. When the ordinal parameter of the round (tested at
Step 3) is β, we call it the β’th round. Thus the initial round is the α’th round; after Player 1 chooses
γ < α at Step 5, the game continues to the γ’th round.
At this point, a reader who wants examples may skip to Section 4.3 to see how the rank-α discon-
tinuity game simplifies in the case of single valued functions.

Remark. We pause to note some equivalent alternative formulations of the discontinuity game.
Firstly, we could have defined the discontinuity game without reference to an ordinal α. In this al-
ternative version of the game, play proceeds as in the ranked discontinuity game except that Player 1
does not choose an ordinal γ at Step 5. Instead, play continues forever until Player 1 wins by playing
an instance of P with no solutions above the current value of ρ. Player 2 wins a run of this game if
Player 1 does not win the run.
We could then apply the theory of transfinite game values developed by Evans and Hamkins [17]
to infinite chess (which has recently been shown to be universal for Gale-Stewart games by Bolan and
Tsevas [2]) to assign ranks to winning positions for Player 1. However, since we only want to assign
ranks at Step 1 of the game (rather than also assigning ranks at Step 3), we opt for the more ad-hoc
approach of making the game-rank part of Player 1’s moves.
Secondly, we could restrict the set of possible Player 2 moves to a countable set by changing the
rules to have Player 1 preemptively play a set of initial segments ρ0, ρ1, . . . that cover P(p) in addition

to the version given in Definition 4.2.2. Since the set of possible Player 2 moves is countable, we
can apply the so-called fundamental observation of transfinite game values [17] to conclude that the
statement “Player 2 has a winning strategy in the rank α discontinuity game for some α < ω1” is
equivalent to the statement “Player 2 has a winning strategy in the infinite-run discontinuity game”.

6.3
Other Rankings of Discontinuity
We compare our results to those of some other notions of ranking discontinuity introduced by Peter
Hertling.
6.3.1
Levels of Discontinuity and Mind Changes
In [21], Hertling defines the levels of discontinuity of a single valued function by iteratively removing
points outside of the closure of the set of discontinuities.
The level of discontinuity of a function
is the least ordinal α such that the α-th iteration of this process yields the empty set, or ∞if this
never happens. De Brecht shows that these levels characterize when a single-valued function between
countable T0 spaces is continuous with α many mind changes [11].
In contrast, a consequence of
Theorem 2 is that if a problem P :⊂NN ⇒NN is not continuously solvable with α many mind changes
then ACCα
N is continuously Weihrauch reducible to the problem of solving P in α many mind changes.
This follows from the fact that if P has domain of pointwise Cantor-Bendixson rank α, then P is
continuously solvable with α many mind changes, which we shall prove below. This echoes the result
of Luo and Schulte [23] that if the pointwise Cantor-Bendixson rank of a collection L of languages is
≤α then L is learnable with α many mind changes. Let wα be an enumeration of α as in Section 5.
In the following definition, the symbol # is used to represent a mind change.
Definition 6.3.1. Let P :⊂NN ⇒NN be a problem and let α be a countable ordinal. We define the
problem MCα(P) :⊂NN ⇒(N ∪{#})N by dom(MCα(P)) = dom(P) and
MCα(P)(p) = {ϕ0#s1ϕ1 . . . #skq : ϕi ∈N<N, α > wα(s1) > · · · > wα(sk), and q ∈P(p)}.
Theorem 6.3.2. Let P :⊂NN ⇒NN be a problem and let α be a countable ordinal.
If P(p) is
nonempty for all p ∈dom(P) and rankp(dom(P)) ≤α then MCα(P) is continuous.

committing to only a single additional mind change; an alternative proof to Theorem 6.3.2 uses the
idea that MCα has access to α + 1 many attempts, while Player 1 can only force Player 2 to change
their mind about α many of these attempts in the rank-α discontinuity game.
6.3.2
Ranks of Forests Classes Representing a Weihrauch Degree
Hertling [20] defines a notion of reducibility between labelled forests which corresponds to Weihrauch
reducibility. He notes that a natural notion of rank of discontinuity emerges from this framework by
taking the rank of the forest class representing a Weihrauch degree. While there are similarities in the
construction, this notion of rank of discontinuity is different from the one we have defined here. Indeed,
in Section 4.3, we observe that the rank of a point of discontinuity for single valued functions collapses
to be either 0, 1, or 2. On the other hand, Hertling exhibits examples of single valued functions whose
forest class has rank ω [20, cf. Example 8.6]. A rough heuristic for the source of the difference is
that the recursion in Definition 4.2.1 takes place while considering instances and solutions one at a
time while the recursive step in Hertling’s notion simultaneously takes into account all solutions to
all instances in an open subset of the domain. It would be interesting to understand the relationship
between these two notions of rank of discontinuity more precisely.
