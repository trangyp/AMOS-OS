Reducing stable forking dependence to finitely
many pregeometries
Scott Mutchnik∗
July 2026
Abstract
We show that one of the main cases of the stable forking conjecture,
stability of the forking relation over a base in a finite-rank supersimple
theory, is determined by finitely many pregeometries in each rank. This
case of the stable forking conjecture has long had an implicitly well-known
pregeometric interpretation: there is a set of matroids Gn such that the
forking instability in rank n is equivalent to the pregeometry on some
rank-one partial type (over a finite set) embedding a matroid in Gn. Our
contribution is to show that this set of matroids Gn, determining based
forking stability in rank n, can be chosen to be finite.
The main part of our proof was already accomplished in rank 3 by
Peretz, but does not extend as stated to higher ranks (and may or may
not directly extend in a weaker sense to higher ranks, by shrinking terms).
However, we obtain a sufficient substitute for Peretz’s work in ranks
n > 3: we turn Peretz’s original universal result into an existence the-
orem. The rest of our proof refines an argument from multi-experiment
parameter identifiability, originating from work in applied model theory
by Li, Meshkat, Ovchinnikov, Pillay, Pogudin and Scanlon.

Introduction
One of the most important problems in model theory is the stable forking conjec-
ture, which hypothesizes an especially strong connection between simplicity and
the even more powerful phenomenon of stability. Stability and simplicity are
already deeply connected even in the absence of the stable forking conjecture:
they are tied together by independence phenomena within the classification the-
ory of first-order logical theories. And while the origins of classification theory
lie squarely within the context of stable theories, the rich web of interactions
between stability theory and classes of unstable theories, such as simple theories,
has become one of the field’s fundamental pillars.
The original problem prompting the invention of classification theory mainly
had to do with stable theories: given a first-order theory T, and an infinite

initial breakthrough on this question was due to Morley ([35]), who showed that

Yet it was Shelah ([45]) who established classification theory’s
central role within model theory by attacking this model-counting question more
comprehensively. His central insight is to isolate, from among the theories whose
model-counting status is nontrivial, the class of stable theories: theories for
which no formula has the order property, meaning that there is no formula
φ(x, y) with a sequence {ai, bi}i<ω such that |= φ(ai, bj) exactly when i > j.
When a first-order theory T is unstable, the problem of counting the number
of models I(T, κ) of T of cardinality κ becomes trivial, for uncountable κ: T
will just have the maximum number of models I(T, κ) = 2κ.1 By contrast, the
model-counting problem in stable theories is extremely complex, but nonetheless
tractable due to the structure theory that Shelah develops for stable theories.
Just as the rank of a definable set, introduced by Morley for uncountably
categorical theories, generalizes the dimension of an algebraic variety, Shelah’s
structure theory defines an independence relation in stable theories that also
has its counterpart in algebraic geometry. Specifically, Shelah’s forking inde-
pendence A |⌣C B, between sets within a model of a theory, generalizes the al-
gebraic freeness of fields A and B over a common subfield C. Shelah shows that
forking independence is symmetric in any stable theory, meaning that A |⌣C B
implies B |⌣C A. Moreover, Shelah shows that stability is characterized by sta-
tionarity of forking independence, or uniqueness of nonforking extensions over
models. Shelah’s structure theory for stable theories serves as the main ingredi-
ent for his proof of the main gap theorem, which gives a sweeping classification
of first-order theories in terms of their numbers of size-κ models I(T, κ), a clas-
sification that is ultimately completed for uncountable κ in [21]. The modern
incarnation of Shelah’s characterization of stability in terms of forking indepen-
dence A |⌣C B is established by Harnik and Harrington in [20].
Because Shelah’s model-counting program breaks down outside the class of
stable theories, the main informal research program of interest today arising
out of Shelah’s work is to classify the more complex, unstable theories. Shelah
initiates this program in [45] by introducing part of what is now considered
to be the classical hierarchy of unstable first-order theories. This classical hi-
erarchy was expanded in [46], completed by Dzamonja and Shelah in [17] (as
referenced earlier in [47], and further investigated in [48]), and given its standard
visual representation by Conant in [15]. At the historic core of this hierarchy
of unstable theories is the class of simple theories: theories where no formula
has the tree property, analogous to no formula in a stable theory having the
order property. While every stable theory is simple, not every simple theory
is stable. Nonetheless, it is within the class of simple theories that one of the

In this paper, we will be interested in stability of the forking relation over a
base in theories of finite SU-rank.
The goal of this paper will be to connect this core case of the stable forking
conjecture to another research program inspired by Shelah’s original develop-
ment of stability theory: geometric stability theory. The paradigmatic object in
geometric stability theory is a strongly minimal set: a model of a theory where
every set definable in one variable, or the complement of that set, is finite. As
shown by Baldwin and Lachlan in [2], strongly minimal sets come equipped
with a pregeometry, or the matroid given by the algebraic closure operation on
that set: for example, vector spaces with the linear span operation, or alge-
braically closed fields with the field-theoretic algebraic closure. And according
to the motivating conjecture of geometric stability theory, Zilber’s proposed tri-
chotomy for pregeometries ([54]), these two examples are essentially the only
nontrivial instances of the pregeometry arising from a strongly minimal set.
Specifically, Zilber posits that the pregeometry on a strongly minimal set is al-
ways (1) “set-like” or trivial, (2) “vector space-like” or locally modular, or (3)
“field-like,” meaning that the theory of the strongly minimal set interprets an
algebraically closed field. The first main breakthrough on this conjecture is due
to Hrushovski ([23]), who shows that the Zilber trichotomy is false. Yet more
recent developments in geometric stability theory have shown that the Zilber
trichotomy is true for some very rich classes of pregeometries. Resolving a clas-
sical open problem, Castle ([12]) shows that the Zilber trichotomy is true for
pregeometries arising from reducts of a characteristic-zero algebraically closed
field, and Castle, Hasson and Ye ([13]) extend Castle’s results to the case of
positive characteristic.
It is implicitly well-known, as a standard application of equidominance tools
from geometric stability theory (see, e.g., [44]), that stability of the forking
relation over a base can be interpreted in terms of pregeometries. Here, we con-
sider embeddings of abstract pregeometries (i.e., matroids) into the pregeometry
given by the algebraic closure on an SU-rank-1 partial type.
Fact 1. Let n < ω. Then there is a set Gn of (infinite) pregeometries, depending
only on n, such that the following are equivalent for any finite-rank supersimple
theory T:
• In T, the forking relation is stable over a base between types of rank n.
• No pregeometry in Gn embeds into the pregeometry on an SU-rank-1 partial
type over a finite set in T.
However, this interpretation alone is not very deep: Gn is just some infinite
set. So for the correspondence with pregeometries to give us actual nontrivial
information, we will need to get additional control over this set of pregeometries
Gn that describes all of the ways in which stability of the forking relation can
fail.

Preliminaries
We assume a working knowledge of simplicity theory (see, e.g., [27] for a ref-
erence) and use standard model-theoretic notation; particularly, all sets/tuples
A, B, C, a, b, c, etc. are assumed to be subsets of a sufficiently saturated ambient
model M |= T of a theory T. We begin by describing the core basic case of the
stable forking conjecture to which our results will apply; we will then review
pregeometries in a minimal type, and the standard fact of geometric stability
theory reducing finite-rank types to minimal types; and finally, we will state the
standard, implicitly well-known pregeometric interpretation of our specializa-
tion of the stable forking conjecture, which follows from this fact of geometric
stability theory.
The stable forking conjecture: The stable forking conjecture is stated in
the introduction, in Conjecture 1. Given the apparent difficulty of resolving this
conjecture, we are justified in distilling the original and most general incarna-
tion of the stable forking conjecture to a central case, which itself remains very
much open. One readily apparent specialization is to relativize the stable fork-
ing conjecture to a base, or equivalently, to restrict the original stable forking
conjecture to where the base is the empty set. This case is implicitly consid-
ered in Peretz ([43]), while the main result of Brower ([7]) explicitly restricts to
where the base is the empty set. Moreover, relativizing to a base is obligatory
for the stable Kim-forking conjecture and simple Kim-forking conjecture in [1],
[5]: these conjectures apply to all NSOP1 theories, while failing to relativize to
a base results in a statement that implies simplicity, so could not apply to all
NSOP1 theories.
Conjecture 2. (Stable forking conjecture over a base)
Let T be a simple L-theory. If a ̸ |⌣C b, there is a formula φ(x, b) ∈tp(a/Cb)
which forks over C, such that φ(x, y) is a stable L(C)-formula.
So φ(x, y) is only required to be stable in L(C), rather than in L, which
weakens the original conjecture. This is equivalent to the original stable forking
conjecture for C = ∅: the based stable forking conjecture is true with the base
C, when the original stable forking conjecture is true over the empty set in the
expansion by constants for C.
The other main specialization is to conjecture the stability of the forking
relation, rather than the existence of a stable formula exhibiting every instance
of forking dependence.
This is done independently of relativizing to a base
in Section 4 of [40], though [43] concentrates on the case of stability of the
forking relation over a base. The underlying premise of this case is that stability
applies to relations that are not necessarily definable. For a general relation to
be stable, it must not exhibit the order property, just as stability of a formula

Our main case of the stable forking conjecture will be stability of the forking
relation x ̸ |⌣C y over a base C.
Conjecture 3. (Stability of the forking relation over a base)
If T is simple, then the dependence relation x ̸ |⌣C y over every base C is
stable: it does not have the order property, so there is no C-indiscernible se-
quence {ai, bi}i<ω such that ai ̸ |⌣C bj exactly when i > j. Equivalently, there is
no sequence {ai, bi}i<κ such that ai ̸ |⌣C bj exactly when i > j, where κ is large
enough to apply the Erd˝os–Rado theorem over C.
As before, stability of the forking relation over every base in every simple
theory is equivalent to stability of ̸ |⌣∅in every simple theory. Stability of the
forking relation over a base is also no stronger than the stable forking conjecture
over a base (this is standard, but observed in Remark 7.5 of [1]): a formula
exhibiting forking in the order property for x ̸ |⌣C y would not be stable.
Because all invariant relations in finitely many variables are definable in
countably categorical theories, stability of the forking relation over a base is
related to the stable forking conjecture over a base in countably categorical
simple theories. As observed in [43] and the proof of Corollary 4.4 of [40], in a
countably categorical simple theory, stability of x ̸ |⌣C y over every finite base
C is equivalent to the stable forking conjecture over every finite base C: the
relation x ̸ |⌣C y is definable by an L(C)-formula φ(x, y) with parameters in C,
for which the formula φ(x, b) defining x ̸ |⌣C b will fork over C. Moreover, it
follows from the proof of Corollary 4.4 of [40] that, in a countably categorical
supersimple theories, stability of the forking relation over a base is equivalent
to the stable forking conjecture over a base; that is, supersimplicity allows us
to remove the finiteness assumption on the base. To sketch the argument, in
a countably categorical simple theory satisfying stability of the forking relation
over a base, if a ̸ |⌣C b, we may use supersimplicity to find finite C0 ⊂C such
that ab |⌣C0 C. Then a ̸ |⌣C0 b, so a satisfies x ̸ |⌣C0 b; by countable categoricity,
x ̸ |⌣C0 b will be definable by a formula φ(x, b) that forks over C0, which can be
chosen to be an instance of the L(C0)-formula φ(x, y) defining x ̸ |⌣C0 y. The
formula φ(x, b) will even fork over C because b |⌣C0 C, so φ(x, b) will exhibit
the dependence a ̸ |⌣C b. And φ(x, y) will be a stable formula in L(C0), so in
L(C), by stability of the forking relation over a base.

We have seen that, if the forking relation is unstable in a finite-rank supersim-

instability satisfying Peretz’s nonforking condition over a bounded initial seg-
ment. This is notwithstanding the fact that, unlike in Peretz’s result in rank

condition. Earlier, we also reviewed the well-known fact that instability of the
