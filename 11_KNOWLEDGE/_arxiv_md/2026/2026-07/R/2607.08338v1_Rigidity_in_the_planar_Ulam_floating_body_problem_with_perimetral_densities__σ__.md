Rigidity in the planar Ulam floating body problem with
perimetral densities σ = 1
8, 3
8 under central symmetry
Oleg Asipchuk, Maksim Kosmakov, and Pavel Zatitskii
Abstract. We prove that the only planar, centrally symmetric, strictly convex body K ⊂R2
with C1 boundary that floats in equilibrium in every orientation for the perimetral densities
σ = 1
8 or σ = 3
8 is a disk.
Introduction
This paper continues our study of Ulam’s planar floating-body problem: “Is a solid of
uniform density which will float in water in every position a sphere?”, see Problem 19 in
The Scottish Book [8]. We briefly recall the geometric setting. In the planar case, a planar
domain K of uniform density floats in equilibrium in every orientation if each waterline
cuts the boundary ∂K into two arcs whose lengths are in the fixed ratio σ : (1 −σ) (see
Auerbach [3]); the number σ is called the perimetral density. For σ = 1
2, noncircular examples
were first constructed by Zindler [16] and later placed by Auerbach in the floating-body setting.
Wegner [13, 14] also constructed noncircular floating bodies for certain implicitly determined
values of the physical density, which is a different parameter from the perimetral density used
here.
Bracho, Montejano, and Oliveros [4, 5] proved that the disk is the only solution for the low-
denominator perimetral densities σ = 1
3, 1
4, 1
5, 2
5 using the so-called Zindler carousel formalism.
The cases σ = 1
3 and σ = 1
4 were mentioned in [3] and attributed to E. Salkowski [11].
The case σ = 1
6 was treated recently in [2]. Here we prove the corresponding result for
centrally symmetric bodies when σ = 1
8 or σ = 3
8. For further background on the floating-
body problem, Zindler curves, carousels, and the known examples and rigidity results, see
[1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 13, 14, 15] and the references therein.
The restriction to centrally symmetric bodies is a natural setting in which rigidity phenom-
ena are expected. At relative density 1
2, Falconer proved the corresponding rigidity statement
for origin-symmetric bodies in dimension three [6], while the general higher-dimensional state-
ment follows from an earlier theorem of Schneider [12]. A short modern proof was subsequently
given by Florentin, Schütt, Werner, and Zhang [7]. This rigidity contrasts with Ryabogin’s
negative answer to the higher-dimensional version of Ulam’s problem: without the symmetry
assumption, there exist strictly convex nonspherical bodies of relative density 1
2 which float in
equilibrium in every orientation [9, 10].

Theorem 1. Let K ⊂R2 be a centrally symmetric strictly convex planar body with C1
boundary. If K floats in equilibrium in every orientation with perimetral density σ = 1
8 or
σ = 3
8, then K is a disk.
The starting point of the proof is a system of differential equations derived by Bracho,
Montejano, and Oliveros [4, 5] for the angles associated with the floating configuration. Central
symmetry reduces this system to two variables. After a change of variables, the equations for
σ = 1
8 and σ = 3
8 have the same Hamiltonian form, with the two cases distinguished only by
the sign of a parameter. We study the periodic solutions of this system and estimate their
periods in terms of the Hamiltonian level. For a noncircular body, these period estimates must
also satisfy a discrete rotational constraint and geometric bounds involving the perimeter. The
resulting conditions are incompatible, ruling out all nonconstant solutions. The remaining
constant solution corresponds to a disk.

4. Proof of the main theorem
Assume, for contradiction, that K satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem 1 but is not a disk.
Let Rot(K) = {θ ∈[0, 2π) : Rθ(K) = K} be its rotational symmetry group. Since K is not
a disk, Rot(K) is a finite cyclic group. Since K is centrally symmetric, its order is even, so
Rot(K) = ⟨Rπ/k⟩for some k ∈N.
Choose γ to be positively oriented. The rotation Rπ/k preserves arc length and orientation
on ∂K. Since Rπ/k is the smallest positive rotation in Rot(K),

Consider first σ = 1/8. By Proposition 8, T > 2π. Combining this with Lemma 5 gives
2km · 2π < P ≤8π, thus km < 2. Since km is a positive integer, we get km = 1 and P = 2T.
On the other hand, each boundary arc from βi to βi+1 has length P/8, while the corresponding
chord has length 2. Thus P/8 ≥2, and so T = P/2 ≥8. This contradicts the upper bound
T < 8 in Proposition 8. Thus K must be a disk for σ = 1/8.
Now consider σ = 3/8. By Proposition 8, T > 5π. Since P = 2kmT and km ≥1, we get
P > 10π. This contradicts Lemma 5. Thus K must also be a disk for σ = 3/8.
