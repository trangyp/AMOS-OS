REDUCTIVE MONOIDS OVER GENERAL BASE
JINGREN CHI AND SIMON JACQUES
Abstract. We develop a theory of affine algebraic monoids over general base schemes whose unit
groups are split reductive groups.
Our main result is a classification theorem for such objects,
generalizing works of Vinberg and Rittatore over a field. As applications, we obtain combinatorial
descriptions and normality properties of orbit closures, prove a Steinberg-type theorem on adjoint
quotients of reductive monoids over general base schemes, and construct finite type integral models
of the Vinberg monoids. A main tool in our construction is Lusztig’s theory of modified quantum
groups and their canonical bases.

1. Introduction
1.1. Backgrounds and motivations. The theory of reductive monoids over a field, especially
the Vinberg monoids, has been playing an increasingly foundational role in a number of areas,
including geometric representation theory ([DG16, Bou15, Chi22, Wan25, BZG22]), automorphic
representation theory ([BNS16, Ngˆo14, Ngˆo20]) and number theory ([XZ19, Zhu20]), to name a few.
It is a natural outgrowth of the theory of reductive algebraic groups and affine toric varieties, aiming
to achieve the construction and classification of algebraic monoids with reductive unit groups and
study the geometric properties of orbits under the unit groups.
This theory has been established over any algebraically closed fields, mainly through the works
of Putcha, Renner, Vinberg and Rittatore. The early studies of general linear algebraic monoids
over a field were primarily conducted in the works of Putcha and Renner (see [Put88], [Ren05]
and the references therein). For reductive monoids, there has been (at least) three approaches
to its classification problem. First, according to Renner’s “extension principle” (see [Ren05, §5.1]
and references therein), the normal reductive monoids over a field are uniquely determined by a
cone in the weight lattice that is stable under the Weyl group action. Second, Vinberg [Vin95]
systematically studied reductive monoids over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero.
His classification is in terms of certain submonoid of the dominant weight monoid. Besides, another

speaking, they can all be constructed from a distinguished monoid, the universal enveloping monoid

theory of spherical varieties. His classification is in terms of colored cones in the coweight lattice,
which is in some sense dual to the viewpoint of Renner and Vinberg.
Our goal in this paper is to establish a theory of reductive monoids over arbitrary base schemes
and generalize the above-mentioned works. First let us introduce the basic definitions.
Fix a base scheme S.
Definition 1.1. A monoid scheme over S is a scheme M over S, equipped with

●the composition law, or product morphism πM∶M ×S M →M, which is a morphism of

Simply speaking, this notion is obtained by relaxing the condition on the existence of inverse

The basic example of the monoid schemes we are interested in is the multiplicative monoid of n

We will study generalizations of matrix monoids, whose most important feature is that the unit
group is a reductive group scheme.
Definition 1.2. A reductive monoid scheme over S (or simply, a reductive monoid over S) is a
monoid scheme M over S such that the unit group M× is a reductive group scheme1 over S and
the structure morphism M →S is affine flat, of finite presentation, and all its geometric fibers are
integral and normal.
In particular when S = Spec(k), where k is a field, our definition is slightly more restrictive than
the usual definition and coincides with the notion of an “irreducible normal reductive monoid” in
the sense of [Rit98]. The first basic question we want to address is the following:
How to classify reductive monoids over a general base scheme?
The general classification of reductive group schemes is in terms of (based) root data, and is one of
the major achievements in [SGA 3III]. So the remaining task is to classify reductive monoids whose
unit group is isomorphic to a fixed reductive group scheme G. In this paper we will give a complete
answer to the question above in the case where the unit group G is a split Chevalley group scheme.

be the Weyl group. Let X ∶= X∗(T) and Y ∶= X∗(T) be respectively the weight and the coweight
lattice. Let X+ ⊂X be the submonoid consisting of dominant weights (with respect to B). Also, we
have the partial order ≤on X determined by the positive roots. As in the classical theory of toric

The approach of Rittatore is to regard M as an affine embedding of the group G, now viewed
as a homogeneous space for G × G, and apply the general theory of spherical embeddings in this
setting. This theory is first developed by Luna and Vust [LV83] over an algebraically closed field
of characteristic zero, and generalized by Knop [Kno91] to algebraically closed fields of arbitrary
characteristic.
In particular, Rittatore’s approach works in arbitrary characteristic and, unlike
Vinberg’s work, his classification of reductive monoids is in terms of cones in YQ (which turns out
to be dual to the cone Q≥0L in XQ, see §9.3).
Finally, the approach of Renner is conceptually the most elegant in our opinion.
The basic
principle is that a reductive monoid M with unit group G is uniquely determined by the closure
of the maximal torus T in it, which is an affine toric variety corresponding to a W-stable cone
in XQ. This is a more canonical invariant of the reductive monoid since it only depends on the
underlying root system, whereas the weight monoid L in Vinberg’s approach and the colored cone
in Rittatore’s approach also depend on the extra choice of simple roots. However, it is not hard to
show that this W-stable cone is precisely W ⋅Q≥0L and we have L = W ⋅Q≥0L ∩X, see Corollary
6.13. This clarifies the relation with Vinberg’s theory.
In this paper we will mainly adopt Vinberg’s viewpoint since it makes the construction of (the
coordinate rings of) reductive monoids more straightforward. In the same spirit of the classification
of reductive group schemes in [SGA 3III], our classification of reductive monoids has two major
steps: existence and uniqueness.
For the existence, we need to construct a reductive monoid explicitly from the datum of a weight
monoid L ⊂X+ (or equivalently the corresponding W-stable convex polyhedral cone in XQ). Of
course, it suffices to do the construction over Spec(Z) (and then base change to arbitray schemes).
To achieve this we apply Lusztig’s machinery of the (dual) canonical bases (cf. [Lus10]) and build
on his construction of the Chevalley group schemes in [Lus09].
Since the canonical bases are
“quantum” in nature, we actually construct quantizations of the reductive monoids, i.e. objects
over Z[v,v−1] that specialize to the integral models when v ↦1.
For the uniqueness, we need to show that all reductive monoids over a scheme S with given unit
group can be constructed in this way. In particular, this shows that, just like reductive groups,

1.2.4. Adjoint quotients. Let L ⊂X+ be a weight monoid and let M = M(L)S be the corresponding
reductive monoid over S. Let W = NG(T)/T be the Weyl group and let W ⋅L ∶= ⋃w∈W w(L). Then

group T. Here OS[W ⋅L] is the sheaf of monoid algebras for W ⋅L with coefficients in OS.
Theorem 1.6 (Theorem 6.16 and Theorem 6.24). The scheme MT is the schematic closure of T
in M and the natural embedding MT ↪M induces an isomorphism between GIT quotients
MT //W ≅M//Ad(G)
where Ad(G) denotes the adjoint action of G on M.
The adjoint GIT quotient of a semisimple group over a field was first described by Steinberg
[Ste65]. Later Lee [Lee15] generalized Steinberg’s result to reductive group schemes over a general
base scheme. Our result is a further generalization of Lee’s result.

1.2.6. Orbit closures. Once we have the classification theorem available, the next problem we ad-
dress is to describe the G × G orbit closures in a reductive monoid. Let M be a reductive monoid
over S with unit group G and weight monoid L ∶= L(M). So we have OS[M] = ZO(L) ⊗Z OS.
For any ideal J ⊂L (see Appendix A, especially Definition A.3, for related notions), one can
show (using properties of the canonical bases) that ZO(J ) ⊂ZO(L) is an ideal. Then we get the
following closed subscheme:
M(L/J ) ∶= Spec OS ⊗Z (ZO(L)/ZO(J )) ⊂M.
Theorem 1.10 (Theorem 8.4). With notations as above, for any prime ideal J ⊂L the scheme
M(L/J ) is flat over S with integral normal geometric fibers.
Suppose moreover that the base
scheme S is connected. Then the assignment J ↦M(L/J ) defines an order-reversing bijection
between the following pre-ordered sets:
(i) downward closed prime ideals of L;
(ii) G × G-stable closed subschemes of M that are flat with integral geometric fibers over S.
In particular, the geometric S-fibers of the schemes in (ii) are automatically normal.
Here we say that an ideal J ⊂L is prime if the complement L ∖J is a submonoid of L, and we
say that J is downward closed if for any λ,µ ∈L with µ ≤λ and λ ∈J we have µ ∈J .
In particular, when working over an algebraically closed field (of any characteristic), we recover
the classical result on the normality of orbit closures of reductive monoids (see for example [Vin95,
Theorem 3 (5)] in the characteristic zero case).
1.3. Organization of the article. The article can be roughly divided into two parts: the first part,

sections §6-§9, is “classical” (i.e. specialize v ↦1 in “quantum objects”) and contains our main
results on reductive monoids.

Appendix A. Recollection on commutative monoids
We summarize some basic facts on commutative monoids that are used throughout the article.
The main reference is [Ogu18, Chapter I].
Definition A.1. A monoid is a triple (M,∗,eM) consisting of a set M, an associative binary
operation ∗on M and a two-sided identity element eM ∈M for ∗. The monoid is commutative if
the binary operation ∗is commutative. For a commutative monoid M, we will use the additive
notation “+” to denote the binary operation and use 0 = 0M to denote the identity element. We
have obvious notions of submonoids and homomorphisms of monoids. The collection of monoids
form a category Mon and the commutative monoids form a subcategory CMon ⊂Mon.
The forgetful functor Ab →CMon from the category of abelian groups to the category of com-
mutative monoids has a left adjoint M ↦Mgp. The associated natural transformation λM∶M →Mgp
is a universal monoid homomorphism from M to a group. The forgetful functor also has a right
adjoint M ↦M∗where M∗is the maximal subgroup of M, i.e. the group of invertible elements in
M.
Definition A.2. A monoid M is
●finitely generated if there exists a finite subset S ⊂M such that the only submonoid of M
containing S is M itself.
●integral if it is commutative and whenever any elements m,m′,m′′ ∈M satisfy m + m′ =
m + m′′, we have m′ = m′′.
●fine if it is integral and finitely generated.
●saturated if it is integral and if whenever nx ∈M for some x ∈Mgp and some positive integer
n, then x ∈M.
When M is integral, the map λM∶M →Mgp is injective and identifies M with a submonoid of
the abelian group Mgp. In this case, we also denote Mgp ∶= M −M and view its elements as formal
differences between elements in M. Moreover we have M∗= M ∩(−M).
Definition A.3. Let M be a monoid. An ideal of M is a subset I ⊂M such that m + x ∈I for all
x ∈I and all m ∈M. A prime ideal of M is a proper ideal I ⊂M such that the complement M ∖I
is a sub-monoid of M, i.e. m1 + m2 ∉I for all m1,m2 ∈M ∖I. (We must also have 0 ∈M ∖I since
otherwise we would get I = M.)
We note that arbitrary unions of (prime) ideals of M are (prime) ideals. Also, arbitrary inter-
sections of ideals are ideals. But an intersection of prime ideals may not be a prime ideal.
Definition A.4. Let M be a monoid. An M-set is a set S with an action of M, i.e. a monoid
homomorphism M →End(S). In particular, the monoid M is an M-set over itself. For each element
m ∈M, the endomorphism on an M-set S induced by m will be denoted by s ↦m ⋅s,∀s ∈S.
An M-set S is free (or more precisely, M-free) if there exists a subset T ⊂S such that the action
map M × T →S defined by (m,t) ↦m ⋅t is bijective. In this case, we say that T is a basis (or more
71


## Page 72


precisely, an M-basis) for S. More generally, for any M-set S, we say that a subset T ⊂S generates
S if the action map M × T →S is surjective.
An M-set S is finitely generated if there exists a finite subset T ⊂S that generates S. It is
noetherian if any of its M-stable subsets is finitely generated. The monoid M is noetherian if it is
noetherian as an M-set over itself.
For example, M itself is an M-set and any ideal of M is an M-set.
Definition A.5. Let M be a monoid and let S be an M-set. We define a pre-order ⪯and an
equivalence relation ∼on S by:
●s1 ⪯s2 if there exists m ∈M such that m ⋅s1 = s2,
●s1 ∼s2 if s1 ⪯s2 and s2 ⪯s1.
Let Smin ⊂S be the subset of minimal elements under ⪯. In other words, s ∈Smin if whenever an
element s′ ∈S satisfies s′ ⪯s we have s′ ∼s. Then Smin is a union of M∗-orbits.
For example, when S = M we have Mmin = M∗. In general Smin might be empty (for instance
when M = N and S = Z). However, it will be nonempty under some finiteness assumptions.

follows from Proposition 2.1.5 (4). By the argument of the first paragraph on Page 25 (which is
in the proof of Proposition 2.1.5), any set of representatives of Smin/M∗generates S. If S is free,
let T ⊂S be a basis so that the action map M × T →S is bijective. Then clearly T is a set of

Definition A.7. Let k be a commutative ring and M be a commutative monoid. The monoid
algebra (with coefficients in k) is the free k-module with basis {em,m ∈M}, endowed with the
unique k-algebra structure whose multiplication is determined by the rule em1 ⋅em2 = em1+m2 for
all m1,m2 ∈M. In particular, the multiplicative unit is 1 ∶= e0 ∈k[M].
For any M-set S, we let k[S] be the free k-module with basis S and endowed with the unique
k[M]-module structure compatible with the action of M on S. In particular, for any ideal I ⊂M,
the k-submodule k[I] ⊂k[M] spanned by I is an ideal of k[M].
Lemma A.8. Let M be an integral monoid such that Mgp is torsion free and let k be an integral

(1) The monoid algebra k[M] is an integral domain and for any prime ideal I ⊂M, the ideal
