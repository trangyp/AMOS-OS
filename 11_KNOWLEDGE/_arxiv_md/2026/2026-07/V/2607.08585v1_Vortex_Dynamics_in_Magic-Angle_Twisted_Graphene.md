We use a gate-defined Josephson junction (JJ) device made from twisted-layer graphene for study-
ing vortex dynamics in two dimensions. The JJ sensor signals the presence of individual vortices in
the superconducting leads nearby the junction through shifts in the Fraunhofer interference pattern
of the magnetic-field-dependent critical current Ic(B) across the junction. Rapid vortex fluctua-
tions manifest as telegraph-type noise in time traces of the junction voltage V (t). Measurements of
Ic(B) and V (t) are interpreted in terms of multi-vortex processes where fast vortex fluctuations in
the leads are modulated by quasi-stationary vortices trapped in the leads. The different timescales
associated with these processes allow for their disentangling and quantitative analysis. Tracking the
temperature dependence of the vortex-dynamical rates between T = 7 mK and T = 120 mK, we find
that the creep type vortex motion is thermally activated above T ≈100 mK, while the saturation
of rates below T ≈80 mK is suggestive of a sharp transition to macroscopic quantum tunneling of
vortices.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Twisted graphene films have emerged as novel super-
conductors with remarkable tunability, enabling manip-
ulation of the superconducting state through a mere
change in carrier density [1–12]. Local gates allow for
tuning different regions of the same sample into distinct
phases, superconducting, metallic, or insulating; using
such technique, the homogeneous material can be struc-
tured into a functional device [13–20].
Numerous ap-
plications in superconducting electronics have emerged
by now [13–22]; here, we report on a device made
from twisted-layer graphene that allows for the study of
the material’s superconducting phenomenology as deter-
mined by vortex physics [23].
Superconducting structures define inductive devices
governed by magnetic fields and currents rather than
electric fields and charges. In type II superconductors,
topological defects, i.e., vortices, determine the mate-
rial’s response to electrical currents and magnetic fields
[24, 25].
These vortices and their dynamics are usu-
ally measured as bulk ensembles, with notable exceptions
such as single-vortex imaging through magneto-optical
imaging [26], scanning tunneling- [27] and Lorentz mi-
croscopy [28], or the SQUID on Tip technique [29]. Bulk
ensemble measurements of magnetic moments and their
time decay inform about vortex penetration and their
creep dynamics [30–32] in a material. The topic gained
much interest with the cuprate high-Tc materials [33, 34],
where fluctuations are particularly relevant and glassy

vortex dynamics has been uncovered [35, 36]. The satura-
tion in the decay of magnetization to a finite value at low
temperatures [37–40] was attributed to quantum creep
[41, 42], the macroscopic quantum tunneling of vortices.
Besides magnetic relaxation, the analysis of the voltage–
current V –I characteristics below critical field or temper-
ature provides information on vortex creep [43], e.g., the
quantum dynamics of Pearl-vortices [44] in high-Tc thin
films [45].
Here, we take a next step in the observation of vortex
creep by studying the dynamics of individual vortices.
To do so, we use a Josephson junction (JJ) in a thin-film
device made of twisted-layer graphene as a vortex sensor
[46] enabling detection of individual Pearl-vortices [44]
penetrating or leaving the superconducting leads. Our
study adds a new transport-based probe for observing in-
dividual vortex penetration and dynamics, yielding rates
of vortex motion as a function of experimental param-
eters such as carrier density, temperature, or magnetic
field. Our findings show a complex vortex dynamical be-
havior involving different time scales, with vortices pen-
etrating a twisted-layer graphene film by thermal activa-
tion over barriers at high temperatures and by quantum
tunneling through barriers at low temperatures.
In our previous work using the single-vortex Josephson
sensor, we have identified single-vortex entry- and exit-
processes in superconducting leads through the observa-
tion of abrupt jumps in the Fraunhofer-type interference
pattern of the Josephson junction [46]; furthermore, the
observation of vortex fluctuations at high temperatures
has been used to infer the film’s superfluid density ρs. In
Ref. [47], we have reported on the single-vortex dynam-
ics manifested through the observation of telegraph-type

JOSEPHSON JUNCTION AS
SINGLE-VORTEX SENSOR
Our single-vortex detector is a gate-defined Joseph-
son junction (JJ) made of magic-angle twisted four-layer
graphene (MAT4G), previously studied in Refs. [46, 47].
Gating and geometry of the device are shown in Fig. 1(a)
and (b); the film width W is much smaller than the length

density is tuned to the edge of the electron superconduct-

in the junction region is tuned to the resistive state at full
filling in both cases (see Appendix A for tuning details).

influence on the Josephson phase of the junction [48].

Summarizing, vortex penetration into the supercon-

Vortices trapped not too close to the
junction mimick a reduction in the field magnitude and
thus a rightward shift of the FP. The observed shifts ap-
pear as sharp events (without internal structure). The
field scale ∆B defining the separation between zeroes in
the Fraunhofer pattern is determined by the unit flux
Φ0 = h/2e penetrating the area W 2 of the lead [46, 49],
∆B ≈1.8Φ0/W 2; the observed shifts in the FP then
correspond to a change in flux measuring a fraction of
the flux quantum Φ0, see Fig. 1(c). From this observa-
tion, we conclude that we deal with single-vortex events
[48, 50–52].
The strong-leads tuning involves a large superfluid
density ρs and correspondingly large energy barriers
U ≈ε0d = πρs for vortex entry (exit) into (out of)
the film as previously demonstrated in Ref. [46]. Here,
ε0 = (4π/µ0)(Φ0/4πλL)2 is the vortex line energy, µ0
is the vacuum permeability, and d is the film thickness.
With these large energy barriers, temporal vortex fluctu-
ations are suppressed, with typical lifetimes on the order
of ∼103 s.
As a result, individual vortex jumps into
and out of the film are well resolved, as documented in
Fig. 1(c).
In order to study vortex dynamics, we wish to reduce
the time-scale of vortex entry and exit by reducing the
creep barriers, i.e., the superfluid stiffness ρs.
This is
achieved by tuning the film to the edge of the supercon-
ducting dome, i.e., into a weak-leads tuning regime, see
Appendix A for tuning details. This opens the way for
faster vortex dynamical events on a time scale around
and below ∼1 s that we are going to study in the follow-
ing. Before doing so, let us briefly pause and discuss an
alternative scenario for potential jumps in the Fraunhofer
pattern.
A.
Pearl-Josephson- versus Pearl-vortices
An interesting question is the location of the topo-
logical defects triggering the jumps in the Fraun-
hofer pattern—are these jumps due to Pearl-vortices
in the leads or rather due to metastable configura-
tions of Josephson-vortices in the junction?
First,
we have to distinguish between one period along y
(see Fig. 1(b)) in the junction current density j(y) =
jc cos[2π(Φ(B)/Φ0)y/W], with Φ(B) ≈2λLWH, within
a standard (short) Josephson junction [25] versus the
solitonic solution of extension λJ ≫W of the sine-
Gordon equation in a long junction [25] (with λJ =
(Φ0/4πµ0λLjc)1/2 the Josephson length)—both are re-
ferred to as ‘Josephson-vortices’. While the current pat-
tern in a short junction of length W ≲λJ is pinned to the
edges, the solitonic Josephson-vortices in a long junction
(W ≫λJ) can be easily shifted along the junction, i.e.,
along y; hence, the latter have their own dynamics and
can be pinned to the edges as well as defects along the
junction, leading to (multiple) metastable junction-states
[53] that could produce jumps in the Fraunhofer pattern.
Thus we have to check whether we deal with a ‘short’ or
a ‘long’ Josephson junction.
Our junction device involves thin, weakly-screening
leads and a rather strong-coupling junction. This com-
bination renders the structure of (dynamical) Josephson
vortices non-trivial. The situation is similar to the one in
bulk junctions as discussed by Gurevich [54]. In strongly
coupled junctions, the Josephson vortex involves two
scales: the size of the phase-core 2ξJ = 2ξ/D ≈λ2
J/λL
(where the phase changes by ∼2π, D denotes the junc-
tion transparency D = jcJ/j0, the ratio of the junction
and depairing critical current densities), and its mag-
netic size λ (where currents vanish).
In a bulk situa-
tion [54], the so-called Abrikosov-Josephson vortex with
two scales persists until ξJ ∼λ, with λ = λL.
For
our weak-screening film [55], the corresponding crite-
rion involves the Pearl-length λ = Λ = 2λ2
L/d.

III.
NOISE IN FRAUNHOFER PATTERN
In Fig. 2(a), we show the Fraunhofer pattern measured
at the temperature T = 7 mK for the weak-leads tuning.
As the critical current Ic(B) drops to zero in the vicin-
ity of the first zero at B0 in the Fraunhofer pattern (i.e.,
Ic(B0) = 0), we observe a noisy pattern that exhibits a
characteristic shape reminding of a shifted FP as seen
previously in Fig. 1(c). The magnification in Fig. 2(b)
identifies this noise as a series of white, light- and dark-
blue dots resulting from jumps between dissipative and
superconducting states in the V –I traces (values R < 0
associated with downward jumps are set to zero, result-
ing in dark-blue dots). Examples of these jumps are dis-
played in the V −I characteristics, where the voltage
switches between the superconducting and the dissipative
state while the bias d.c. current I is swept at fixed field B,
see right panel in Fig. 2(b). With typical current sweep-
ing rates of order ΓI = 0.16 nA/s, such jumps appear
on the time scale of seconds. We associate these jump-
signals (involving dark- and light-blue dots describing su-
perconducting and resistive states separated by steep up-
and downward jumps identified as white and very-dark
blue dots) with vortices entering and exiting the leads
on the time scale of few seconds. We note that the volt-
age measured in the V –I characteristic arises from the
switching of the junction to the resistive state when the
driving current I surpasses the critical junction current
Ic(B). The presence or absence of the vortex in the lead
then alters the critical current Ic(B) via changing the
junction’s phase pattern [48, 50–52].
The overall structure of the noise,
ending at a
shifted slope of the Fraunhofer pattern (see red lines in
Fig. 2(a)), has an immediate explanation: the vortices
penetrating the leads follow the same path through the
leads that defines a unique maximal shift in the Fraun-
hofer pattern. Such a vortex-induced shift can be cal-
culated, see Refs. [50–52] and our previous discussion in
Ref. [46]. The red line in Fig. 2(a) is a fit to the data
involving a vortex at the position xv = 0.67 W, yv = 0,
i.e., in the lead middle close to the junction. The vortex
changes the phase difference ∆γJ(y) across the junction,
leading to a rightward shift of the Fraunhofer pattern by
about 0.4 mT. This interpretation is further confirmed
by the V –I trace in Fig. 2(b) which exhibits jumps be-
tween the critical current values Ic and Iv
c (> Ic) without
and with a vortex present. We note, however, that not
all jumps visible in the V -I traces of Fig. 2(b) are as-
sociated with vortex entry or exit processes in or out of
the leads; specifically, the first and last upward jumps
may be associated with the junction going resistive upon
surpassing the critical currents Ic and Iv
c , respectively.
Furthermore, the opposite applies as well: e.g., vortex-
dynamical processes that preserve the superconducting
or dissipative state do not produce a jump in the V –I
trace and thus are not trivially detected by our vortex
sensor.
While we interpret the noisy shift of the Fraunhofer
pattern with rapid vortex fluctuations in the leads, we
associate the second maximum around B ≈3.3 mT with
a quasi-stationary vortex trapped very close to the junc-
tion. Specifically, the fitted magenta lines in Fig. 2(a)
originate from a calculation accounting for a vortex
trapped at xv = 0.055 W, yv = 0.12 W. The presence
of such a vortex reduces the main peak at B = 0 and
enhances the second peak in the Fraunhofer pattern, re-
sulting in the magenta lines shown in Fig. 2(a). Admit-
tedly, an alternative interpretation of this second peak
could be given in terms of a non-uniform distribution of
the critical current density jc(y) along the junction with
maxima near the junction edges and no vortices present
[56]. However, such an interpretation is less suitable to
fit the main peak of the Fraunhofer pattern and we do
not follow this possibility further on.

IV.
NOISE IN VOLTAGE
To study single-vortex motion, we measure time-
traces, see Fig. 3, of the voltage across the Josephson
junction, recorded as the output of a data acquisition
card, Vd(t) (for setup details, see Appendix B). We fix
the magnetic field at B∗= 2 mT and choose a bias cur-
rent I∗= 4 nA that lies within the dynamical regime
above the pristine critical current Ic(B∗); time records
over 4 hours ensure statistical significance of our mea-
surements. For each temperature (between T = 7 mK
and T = 120 mK), we observe voltage switchings between
distinct levels, that we associate with vortices entering
or exiting the leads.
The assignment of voltage levels
to corresponding vortex or no-vortex states depends on
the relative position of B∗and B0(T) and changes with
temperature as described above; special care has to be
taken in the vicinity of B0 with the rounded V –I charac-
teristic due to the vanishing critical current Ic(B0) = 0.
Here, we describe in detail the measurement outcomes
at low- (T = 7 mK) and high- (T = 100 mK) tempera-
tures which together capture the essential features of our
analytic procedure.

V (B) shows a negative slope, see Fig. 4(b); as the field
is decreased below B∗, the voltage first goes through a
maximum and then steeply drops to zero. When a vortex
enters the lead, the effective field seen by the junction is
reduced. A consistent association of voltage levels and
lead states in Fig. 3(b) is obtained if we associate the
state with lower dissipation (blue in Fig. 3(b)) with the
pristine lead. A distant quasi-stationary vortex decreases
the effective B-field by a small amount and the junc-

(see Fig. 4(b)) as compared to the pristine state—hence,
we associate the state with the higher dissipation (cyan

the other hand, a (fluctuating) vortex nearby the junc-
tion reduces the effective B-field quite strongly (by about
0.4 mT, as follows from the shift between orange and red
lines in the Fraunhofer pattern of Fig. 2(a)) and the dis-
sipation drops to zero, i.e., the fluctuating vortex pushes
the junction into the superconducting state (red or or-
ange in Fig. 3(b)).
Summarizing, the following overall picture emerges:
a distant quasi-stationary vortex (timescale ∼103 s)
slowly modifies the energy landscape for vortices from
pristine (blue dissipation level in Fig. 3(b)) to vortex-
penetrated (cyan dissipation level).
Fast (fluctuating)
vortices (timescale ∼1 s) entering/exiting the leads closer
to the junction produce a larger reduction in the effective
field seen by the junction and switch the system into the
superconducting state (red and orange).
Coming back to the discussion in Sec. II A, we remark
that the existence of different dissipative states (blue
and cyan) speaks against an interpretation of switch-
ing events due to metastable states in a long junction:
while the superconducting junction may reside in differ-
ent metastable states with different critical currents Ic,
one expects the dissipative state of the junction to be
unique.

Kelvin- and actions S/ℏin the few-tens- range make
physical sense in the situation at hand. The free-energy
landscape G(y) for a vortex crossing a thin-film strip in
the weak-screening limit has been calculated in Ref. [63].
The free energy rises steeply at the film edges due to the
Bean-Livingston surface barrier [64]. A finite magnetic
field B ≈µ0H produces a minimum in the film center,
while the transport current I tilts the landscape. In the
tilted landscape, a vortex cannot tunnel in the reverse di-
rection, what lets us associate the observed fluctuations
at low temperatures with vortices traversing the film.
Barrier heights U are given by the vortex energy ε0d,
while barrier widths are a fraction of W, see Figs. 6(e)
and (f) for typical shapes of the free-energy landscape.
Comparing our experimental result U/kB ≈2.6 K with
the theoretical finding that U ≈ε0d, we find [46] a su-
perfluid density ρs/kB ≈0.8 K and a value λL ≈2.7 µm
for the London penetration length, in agreement with
other results [65, 66].
The action S for a dissipative
tunneling process is determined by the tunneling dis-
tance ∼qb and the film resistance R□= ρn/d, S ≈
ℏ(π/8)(qb/ξ)2(RK/R□), where qb is the distance between
the minimum (G = 0) and maximum (G = U) of the
tunneling barrier as modelled by a cubic parabola [62].
Here, RK = h/e2 ≈25.8 kΩis the von-Klitzing constant
and ξ is the coherence length. With R□≈2 kΩand a
coherence length [46] ξ ≈40 nm, we find a tunneling dis-
tance (3/2)qb ≈W/8 ≈3.5 ξ that compares well with
the typical barrier thickness in the free-energy landscape
G(y).
VI.
SUMMARY
In summary, we have demonstrated the versatility of
our Josephson junction sensor as a Pearl-vortex detector
in a thin film. Our transport-based method enables the
detection of individual vortices, providing a framework
to trace and characterize single-vortex motion as a func-
tion of temperature and magnetic field.
The observed
Pearl-vortex dynamics exhibits a complex structure that
we have attributed to the joint action of two types of
vortices: those rapidly penetrating the superconducting
leads in the junction vicinity and other quasi-stationary
vortices trapped further away from the junction.
The
latter control the former through a change in the pinning
landscape, specifically, their entry and exit barriers. The
fast vortex fluctuations and slow control vortices produce
distinct segments in the voltage traces. We could disen-
tangle the various processes and find the rates for vor-
tex entry- Γnv and exit Γv events in pristine and vortex-
penetrated lead states. The temperature dependence of
these rates hints to creep-type vortex motion, thermal
activation over barriers at high temperatures and quan-
tum tunneling through barriers at low temperatures. We
have extracted key phenomenological parameters, includ-
ing the edge barrier U, the tunneling action S, and the at-
tempt frequency ν0. We conclude that our single-vortex
Josephson sensor provides us with valuable insights into
the behavior of Pearl-vortices in twisted-layer graphene
and opens the possibility to integrate them into the func-

Appendix B: Measurement setup
All measurements were performed in a dilution refrig-
erator at a base temperature of 7 mK, see setup sketch
in Fig. A1.
We employed a current-biased Ibias, two-
terminal configuration, subtracting contact resistances
during post-processing.
Bias was generated using a
home-built d.c source in series with a R1 = 100 MΩre-
sistor. The resulting voltage drop was amplified (×1000)
via a low-noise d.c. amplifier (see [68]) and recorded by
a HP 3441A multimeter, voltage output V . This voltage
output was used for all the measurements in this work,
except the vortex time trace for statistical analysis. For
vortex time trace measurements, the voltage was further
amplified (×30000), low-pass filtered at 1.1 kHz and sam-
pled at 20 kHz using an NI BNC-2110 Data Acquistion
(DAQ) card. The voltage output from the data card Vd
was then recorded by a computer. These setup parame-
ters were optimized for signal-to-noise ratio. Gate volt-
ages for the top, finger and bottom gates were controlled
by home-built low-noise d.c. voltage sources.
Appendix C: Data analysis of voltage traces Vd(t)
1.
Post-processing
Voltage traces Vd(t) are first filtered using a notch filter
at fcable = 661(20) Hz to remove cabling noise. Since the
relevant signal information is confined below 100 Hz, we
apply a second-order digital low-pass filter with a cut-
off of fLP = 200 Hz.
The data are then resampled at
fresample = 3 kHz to optimize computational efficiency;
this preserves the time-domain signal integrity as the
Nyquist frequency (1.5 kHz) remains well above the fil-
tered bandwidth [69]. Discrete switching events are ex-
tracted by digitizing the signal using the Yuzhelevski al-
gorithm [57]. Five iterations of this process [69] yield a
clean two-state trace, from which we determine the wait-
ing times for both voltage states.
