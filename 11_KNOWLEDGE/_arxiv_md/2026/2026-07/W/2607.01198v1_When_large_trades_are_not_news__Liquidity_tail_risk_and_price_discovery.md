When large trades are not news: Liquidity tail risk and
price discovery
Umut Çetin ∗Mingwei Lin† Giulia Livieri‡

Abstract
When is a large trade news, and when is it a liquidity shock? We study this question
in a sequential competitive limit order book with asymmetric information. In our model,
liquidity suppliers observe aggregate order flow but not its decomposition into informed
demand and uninformed liquidity demand.
We model uninformed order flow with
Student-t tails, interpreted as a reduced form for rare liquidity regimes. The tail index
of liquidity demand determines how informative large trades are. With thin-tailed noise,
large order imbalances are quickly interpreted as private information. With heavy-tailed
liquidity demand, the same imbalances remain plausibly liquidity-driven. This liquidity-
tail ambiguity flattens and concavifies price impact, slows learning from order flow, and
delays the decline of adverse-selection premia. We characterize equilibrium through
a fixed-point equation for the marginal-cost schedule. Heavy-tailed liquidity demand
changes the mathematics of equilibrium: the Gaussian monotonicity and compactness
arguments fail because remote liquidity states remain pricing-relevant at polynomial
order. We construct fixed points on a tail-controlled compact class and study learning
and large-order asymptotics along selected monotone branches. Repeated order flow
reveals the fundamental value under stable information-rate conditions, but heavier
liquidity tails slow finite-horizon price discovery. Large-order impact obeys regular-
variation asymptotics whose exponents depend on the liquidity-tail index, informed
competition, and posterior beliefs. The model identifies liquidity tail risk as a state
variable for market impact, spread resilience, and the informativeness of large trades.
Keywords: limit order books; market impact; heavy tails; asymmetric information; Bayesian
learning; informed trading.

Introduction
Large trades (in general) move prices of assets. However, not all (large) trades have the same
informational content: a block of, e.g., buy orders may reflect private information about the
fundamental value of the asset, but it may also reflect a rare liquidity shock (for instance,
linked to fund flows, margin pressure, or dealer inventory adjustment). In particular – and
crucially – liquidity suppliers observe the aggregate order flow, not the traders’ motives. Our
interest is in understanding how a competitive limit order book (LOB) prices large trades
when the liquidity demand itself has heavy tails.
We achieve this objective by developing an equilibrium model of trade-market impact in a
sequential trade framework, in which privately informed investors (i.e., informed traders)
execute trades through a trading desk. We will show that (what we will name) the tail risk
of uninformed (i.e., noisy) liquidity demand greatly influences the above-mentioned pricing
problem. Precisely, we show that when liquidity demand is thin-tailed, a very large order
is strong evidence of private information. Instead, when liquidity demand is heavy-tailed,
the same order may be a (rare) liquidity shock; hence, large trades are less (immediately)
informative, price impact is more concave, and learning from the order flow is slower. In
other words, we argue that the (liquidity) tail risk is a state variable for price discovery.
We model aggregate uninformed order flow via a Student-t distribution with ν ą 2
degrees-of-freedom (see also Remark 2.1). Importantly, the assumed distribution represents
a parsimonious way to capture rare liquidity regimes through one interpretable parameter
(ν). In particular, the uninformed order flow can be viewed as a Gaussian order flow with a
random latent liquidity variance: the parameter ν controls how often rare states of large liq-
uidity demand occur. We note that the main departure from the equilibrium model proposed
in [5] is – indeed – the presence of heavy-tailed uninformed order flow. On the other hand,
we share with [6] the view that liquidity conditions are central to information revelation, but
– importantly – we shift our attention from their stochastic volatility of uninformed trading
volume to the tail risk of liquidity demand.
Our model operates in discrete-time. A single risky asset has a fundamental value (de-
noted by V ), fixed by nature at time zero and revealed at the terminal trading date. In each
trading round, liquidity suppliers face adverse selection from myopic, risk-neutral informed
traders who submit market orders based on their private information (see Remark 2.4 for a
discussion on the myopia assumption). Noise traders submit uninformed market orders. As
mentioned earlier, aggregate uninformed order flow follows a Student-t distribution, which
nests the Gaussian benchmark – of [5] – as the degrees-of-freedom parameter tends to infinity.
In this sense, the assumed specification is more flexible than the Gaussian one, where very
large uninformed orders are exponentially unlikely and, therefore, large order imbalances are
quickly interpreted as informative. We note that each period should be interpreted as a short
execution window or meta-order-level imbalance, not as an individual message-level order.
Consistently with [5], insiders execute after noise traders in order to reduce the price impact
of their trades; see Remark 2.3 for a discussion of this timing assumption. In contrast to the
auction-style pricing in [18], we adopt, as mentioned earlier, a LOB structure in which liq-

uidity suppliers post limit orders before trading begins, as in [12]. Orders are then executed
against the standing book.
Limit orders are competitively priced to yield zero expected profit conditional on exe-
cution. The ask-side marginal price at a positive depth is the conditional expectation of
the fundamental value of the asset V given that aggregate order flow exceeds that depth.
Similarly, the bid-side marginal price at a negative depth is the conditional expectation of V
given that aggregate order flow falls below that depth. Informed demand is determined by a
one-period first-order condition equating the informed trader’s valuation to the marginal cost
of execution. Combining the insider optimality condition with competitive execution-based
pricing yields a fixed-point equation for the marginal cost schedule.
Below, we characterize the equilibrium for our model.
First, we find that the informativeness of large trades depends on the tail index of liquidity
demand. In heavy-tail regimes, a large order imbalance is less diagnostic of private informa-
tion because it may be a rare liquidity shock. The model therefore predicts a larger “crossover
depth”, namely the order size at which a trade becomes more likely information-driven than
liquidity-driven; a lower ν shifts, to the right, the (conditional) probability that an order is
informed. In particular, the economically relevant region is the pre-asymptotic range of large
but finite executions, where liquidity suppliers must still distinguish rare liquidity shocks
from informed trading.
Second, we derive tail asymptotics for large-order pricing and informed demand. We show
that (under endpoint regularity conditions for the fundamental distribution) the marginal-
cost schedule exhibits power-law-type scaling at depth. The associated exponent is explicitly
determined by the tail risk, the number of informed traders, and liquidity suppliers’ beliefs.
As mentioned earlier, heavy-tailed liquidity shocks reduce the informativeness of extreme
order flow, because large trades may also be attributed to rare uninformed shocks. As a result,
price impact is flatter and more concave at depth. As the degrees-of-freedom parameter ν
increases, noise-trader tails become thinner, learning is faster, residual adverse-selection risk
declines more quickly, and price impact is lower in later periods. Competition among informed
traders attenuates impact and compresses spreads, but it does not eliminate the heavy-tail
signature in large-order regions.
Third, we find that heavy-tailed liquidity demand slows price discovery. Liquidity suppli-
ers update their beliefs about V from (aggregate) order flow, but Student-t likelihoods treat
extreme observations as possible liquidity outliers. As a result, order-flow surprises have
lower marginal informational content than in a Gaussian model. Beliefs still concentrate in
the long run under (plausible) stability conditions, but finite-horizon adverse selection and
spreads persist for longer. We achieve this by using tools for Bayesian updating with depen-
dent data (e.g., [28] and related work) and establish belief consistency under economically
plausible regularity restrictions. Importantly, this learning result is asymptotic, as in, for
instance, [23]; this observation is empirically relevant and clarifies what kind of information
aggregation our model does and does not imply.
Finally, the Student-t case is not a routine distributional extension. Because Student-t
tails decay polynomially, remote liquidity states remain relevant for execution-conditional
expectations. This breaks the Gaussian monotonicity and compactness arguments used in

heavy-tailed liquidity shocks do not make the marginal-cost problem ill posed. In particular,
we formulate the equilibrium results conditionally on monotonicity of the selected pricing
schedule, and we study analytically and numerically when this monotonicity is recovered.
Our theoretical fixed-point analysis assumes that the fundamental value has bounded
support. Since a full analytical treatment of unbounded fundamentals is more difficult, we
also solve the model numerically for unbounded specifications, including Pareto and Gaussian
fundamentals. The numerical findings confirm the key qualitative predictions of the model:
nonlinear and concave marginal price schedules, learning-driven dynamics of spreads and
impact, and power-law scaling in large-order regions consistent with the asymptotic theory.
We summarize our main technical results as follows.
Assuming bounded support for the fundamental value, Theorem 3.1 establishes the exis-
tence of a Student-t fixed point for the marginal-cost equation on a tail-controlled compact
class. The argument formulates the pricing problem as a fixed point for the marginal cost
schedule in (3.10) and applies Schauder’s fixed-point theorem on a class adapted to the poly-
nomial tails of the Student-t kernel. Theorem 3.1 is a fixed-point existence theorem. The
passage from a fixed point to a limit order book equilibrium requires the associated pricing
schedule to be nondecreasing and nonconstant. Lemma 3.2 shows that any monotone fixed
point has the correct endpoint limits, and that if the associated pricing schedule is nonde-
creasing and nonconstant, then the marginal-cost schedule is strictly increasing. This yields
a well-defined informed demand schedule.
We study learning by liquidity suppliers.
Proposition 4.1 proves that the normalized
log-likelihood ratio converges almost surely to the negative Kullback–Leibler divergence rate,
uniformly on compact interior parameter sets. Combined with the posterior consistency the-

value along the selected monotone solution. Thus adverse selection and liquidity evolve en-
dogenously over time through belief updating. As discussed above, this learning result is
asymptotic.
We characterize market impact at depth. Theorem 5.1 derives tail asymptotics for the
marginal-cost schedule. Under endpoint regularity of the distribution of the fundamental
value, the marginal-cost schedule in (3.10) exhibits regular variation in the tails. The resulting

Contributions and Related Literature
This paper primarily contributes to the literature on LOB models. In particular, it integrates
three strands: (a) equilibrium LOB models with asymmetric information; (b) dynamic in-
formed trading and competition; and (c) heavy-tailed order flow with nonlinear market im-
pact. The review below explains how the current paper relates to these literatures.
Our model builds on the adverse-selection framework and Bayesian pricing logic of [13],
as well as execution-based pricing principles for open LOBs (e.g., [12]). The most direct
theoretical antecedent is [5], who derive power-law results in a static, one-period compet-
itive LOB setting. We extend this framework to a dynamic, multi-period environment in
which liquidity suppliers update their beliefs from observed aggregate order flow.1 This dy-
namic approach allows us to characterize how depth, spreads, and market impact evolve as
information is gradually incorporated into prices.
Relative to [5], the main modeling departure is the introduction of Student-t uninformed
order flow. This specification captures rare liquidity shocks while retaining tractability. It
also changes the structure of the equilibrium problem. Under Gaussian noise, the standard
proof strategy relies on monotonicity and broad-class continuity properties of the pricing
operator. Under Student-t noise, these properties fail in general: the pricing map need not
preserve monotonicity, and the fixed-point operator is not continuous on the broad Gaus-
sian compact class. We therefore prove fixed-point existence on a tail-controlled compact
class adapted to the polynomial tails of Student-t noise. The passage from a fixed point to
an admissible LOB equilibrium is then treated separately through the monotonicity of the
associated pricing schedule.
Our framework also complements the foundational Kyle model [18] and the extensive
literature on multi-period information revelation and strategic competition among informed
traders, such as [2, 17]. We show how the number of informed traders affects the informative-
ness of order flow and generates time variation in spreads and market impact. In our model,
stronger informed competition compresses spreads and attenuates impact, but it does not
eliminate the heavy-tail signature created by rare liquidity shocks. These findings provide
implications for market participants and regulators concerned with information diffusion,
trading costs, and liquidity provision.
A substantial empirical literature documents the prevalence of heavy tails in trading
volume and order flow, as well as pronounced nonlinearities in market impact (e.g., [4, 9, 15,
16, 19, 29]). Our model provides an equilibrium mechanism that connects these empirical
facts. Under execution-based competitive pricing, heavy-tailed uninformed order flow makes
extreme trades less immediately informative, because large order imbalances may also be
generated by rare liquidity shocks. This leads to flatter and more concave price impact at
depth and to power-law asymptotics for large transactions. The resulting comparative statics

asset, with time periods indexed by t “ 0, 1, . . . , T. Each period can be interpreted as a short

We allow the time horizon T to be either finite, in which case the
fundamental value V of the traded asset becomes public at the end of period T, or infinite,

Some remarks on our modelling choices are in order:
Remark 2.1 (Noise traders’ distribution). The assumption about the aggregate market
orders of noise traders is a new concept compared to [5], which assumes that these aggregated
market orders are Gaussian. This assumption results in a richer equilibrium structure and
broader economic implications, as we shall see later in more detail in Section 5.
In particular, large order imbalances are less immediately revealing of private information,
because they may also be generated by rare liquidity shocks. The tail parameter ν provides
7


## Page 8


a disciplined one-dimensional way to vary the degree of this ambiguity. Smaller values of
ν imply heavier-tailed uninformed order flow and, therefore, make extreme liquidity shocks
more likely. We will see that, in equilibrium, this changes both the level and the shape of
the book. First, when liquidity shocks are more heavy-tailed, the marginal price schedule
is flatter and more concave at depth. Second, stronger competition among informed traders
reduces spreads and attenuates impact, but it does not eliminate the heavy-tail signature
in large orders. Third, as liquidity suppliers learn from repeated rounds of trading, both
spreads and impact slopes decline over time, but this decline is slower when noise trading is
more heavy-tailed.
The role of the Student-t specification is therefore structural. In addition, this assumption
is also in line with other recent theoretical works and empirical findings in the econophysics
literature (e.g., [4, 9, 15, 26]). For example, in [26, Appendix D.2] the uninformed trading
volume has an even distribution where the corresponding probability density function (p.d.f.)
has the tail decay given by the density ppzq „ C|z|´p1`νq with ν ą 0 being the tail index.
The typical value therein for this index is shown to be ν « 5{2.
From a mathematical point of view, the departure from a Gaussian noise setting leads
to significant technical difficulties in the proof of existence of an equilibrium, even if one
tries to leverage the Gaussian scale-mixture representation of the Student-t; see Section 3.
In this respect, notice that our heavy-tailed noise is equivalent to a model in which, at each
round of trading, the market draws an unobserved liquidity state that scales the variance
of non-informational order flow. An upshot of this observation is that in our theory we can
consider noise distributions admitting other Gaussian scale-mixture representations.
Remark 2.2 (Random participation of informed traders). In our model, the number of
informed traders Nt is deterministic and common knowledge. This assumption isolates the
informational content of order flow from uncertainty about strategic participation. We leave
for future research the possibility of a random Nt and refer the reader to [20] for a potential
approach.
Remark 2.3 (Within trading round execution priority). Within each trading round t we
assume that aggregate liquidity noise market orders are executed before the informed block.
This timing has two complementary interpretations. First, it is a reduced-form way to imple-
ment the standard camouflage mechanism of asymmetric-information models: the execution
price schedule depends on aggregate order flow, but informed traders cannot condition their
orders on the realized contemporaneous liquidity shock (as in [18]). In our execution-based
LOB environment, this implies that an informed market order is executed on top of a random
background flow Zt (unobserved by insiders), which weakens the informativeness of extreme
order flow and slows information revelation. Second, the timing captures the idea that in-
formed traders optimally concentrate trading when background liquidity is high, because
doing so lowers price impact and reduces the information revealed per unit traded (e.g., [1]).
Equivalently, one can interpret each period as a short trading window during which exoge-
nous liquidity demand arrives first; the strategic informed order is submitted later within the
window and therefore executes against a book that has already absorbed a random net flow.
This protocol also avoids within-period feedback effects (trade-by-trade inference and book

Remark 2.4 (Myopic informed traders and informed traders’ distribution). The model as-

and [8]; see, also, the more recent work [23]. This behavior aligns also with Seppi’s 1990
study [27], which demonstrates that even when large orders can be divided into a series of
smaller orders, informed traders may still choose to place large orders. We leave for future
research the possibility of endogenous re-trading and its implications on the conclusion of our
model. Regarding informed trades’ distribution, in our model trade sizes can vary and we do
not confine our analysis to two trade sizes only since our focus is more on the intertemporal
equilibrium dynamics. In this respect, our model extends, similarly to [23], the framework
in [13].

3.5
Existence of equilibrium
Theorem 3.1 is a fixed-point theorem, not an equilibrium one. It guarantees that the Student-
t marginal-cost equation is well behaved on the class in Definition 3.2. In order to obtain an
equilibrium as in Definition 3.1, we need the global monotonicity of the associated pricing
schedule. As discussed earlier, when the distribution of noise trades is Gaussian the existence
of a fixed-point and the monotonicity problem collapse because of the global monotonicity
preservation of g ÞÑ ϕg. Under Student-t noise trade, instead, these two questions must be
separated. Admittedly, we found it difficult to prove the global monotonicity of the pricing
schedule, but as mentioned earlier in Remark 3.2, in Section 5 we are able to show that
monotonicity can be recovered in the tails. Importantly, in our numerical section, we show
that we have global monotonicity for a broad range of (economically meaningful) choices
of the parameter ν and of the specification of the fundamental value’s prior.

4
The liquidity suppliers’ belief posterior consistency
The dynamics of informed strategies and the liquidity suppliers’ learning process about the
fundamental asset value V are closely related (cfr. (3.3)). Because informed traders’ time
scope for trading decision is limited to one period (they are myopic), the only link between
consecutive periods is the liquidity suppliers’ belief on the asset fundamental value.
In
this section, we investigate whether the liquidity suppliers’ beliefs on the risky asset payoff
converge to the realization chosen by nature at time zero as the number of trading periods
tends to infinity. In particular, this is in line with the convergence result in [13], although in
their model the result holds true if and only if the trade size is the unit trade size, and in
[23], where instead multiple trade sizes are allowed. We interpret the time-zero realization of
V , say v0, as the parameter that governs the history of aggregated orders Y1, Y2, . . .. Given
this interpretation, to prove the just-mentioned convergence, we use the theory of “dynamics
of Bayesian updating with dependent data and misspecified model” proposed in [28]; we
direct the reader to the extensive coverage on this topic in the introduction of the previous
paper. In our case, we have dependence between consecutive aggregated orders (i.e., we deal
with non-i.i.d. data) but the model is specified, in the sense that the sequence Y1, Y2, . . . is
governed/parameterized by the true value v0. We therefore have to simplify the framework in
[28]. In an effort to keep this section as self-contained as possible, we now recall any essential

Future directions
The analysis in this paper opens several directions for future research. A first direction is
empirical.
The model delivers a set of testable implications linking the tail thickness of
liquidity shocks to the shape of price impact and the speed of information aggregation. In
particular, the theory predicts that markets with heavier-tailed residual order flow should
display more concave price impact at depth, slower post-event spread compression, and a
longer range of order sizes over which large trades remain ambiguous between informed de-
mand and liquidity demand. These predictions can be tested using high-frequency order-book
data by estimating the tail index of residualized order imbalance and relating it to impact
curvature, spread persistence, and the permanent component of price changes after large
trades or news events. A second direction is quantitative calibration. The Student-t tail
parameter ν has a clear economic interpretation in the model: it governs the plausibility of
rare liquidity shocks. Estimating ν from liquidity-driven order-flow residuals would allow one
to map observed tail thickness into model-implied price-impact curves and learning speeds.
Such a calibration could be used to compare markets, assets, or trading regimes, and to
study whether periods of market stress are associated not only with higher volatility but also
with a lower informational content of order flow. A third direction is to enrich the source
of liquidity demand. The present model treats Student-t noise as a reduced-form represen-
42


## Page 43


tation of aggregate liquidity shocks. A more structural approach could derive heavy-tailed
liquidity demand from heterogeneous execution needs, fund redemptions, margin constraints,
inventory shocks, or benchmark rebalancing. This would make it possible to connect the tail
parameter ν to observable institutional features of the market. A fourth direction concerns
the monotonicity of the selected solution. The paper shows that the Gaussian monotonicity
argument does not extend directly to Student-t noise, and it separates fixed-point existence
from the monotone limit order book interpretation. The tail analysis shows that monotonic-
ity is recovered asymptotically, and the numerical evidence suggests that the economically
relevant solution is globally monotone in broad parameter regions. A full global monotonic-
ity theorem for the selected Student-t solution remains an important theoretical question.
Such a result would close the gap between fixed-point existence and unconditional equilib-
rium existence. A fifth direction is to introduce additional frictions on the liquidity-supply
side. In the baseline model, liquidity suppliers are competitive and risk neutral, so prices
are conditional expectations. In practice, intermediaries face inventory costs, capital con-
straints, latency, and risk-management limits. Adding these frictions would allow the model
to study how rare liquidity shocks affect not only adverse selection but also liquidity provi-
sion capacity, inventory premia, and resilience after large trades. Finally, the model can be
extended to richer market structures. Multi-asset versions would allow one to study cross-
impact and correlated liquidity shocks. A version with heterogeneous informed traders could
distinguish between short-lived and persistent information. A version with endogenous order
splitting could connect the tail of liquidity demand to execution algorithms and optimal trad-
ing. These extensions would preserve the central insight of the paper: heavy-tailed liquidity
shocks change the inference problem faced by liquidity suppliers, and therefore change the
shape and dynamics of the limit order book.
8
Conclusion
This paper develops a dynamic competitive limit order book model in which uninformed liq-
uidity demand is heavy-tailed. The main economic message is that large order imbalances are
not automatically informative when liquidity shocks have Student-t tails. Liquidity suppliers
observe aggregate order flow, not its decomposition into informed demand and uninformed
liquidity demand. When liquidity shocks are heavy-tailed, rare large liquidity events remain
plausible over a wider range of depths. As a result, large trades remain ambiguous for longer,
price impact is flatter and more concave, and posterior learning from order flow is slower.
The Student-t specification is therefore structural rather than cosmetic. It does not simply
increase the variance of noise trading. It changes the market maker’s inference problem.
Under Gaussian noise, extreme uninformed orders are exponentially unlikely, so large order
imbalances are quickly interpreted as informative. Under Student-t noise, the market must
continue to assign meaningful probability to rare liquidity shocks. This changes both the
economics of price impact and the mathematics of the equilibrium problem. On the theo-
retical side, the paper shows that the Gaussian proof strategy cannot be imported directly.
The Student-t pricing map does not preserve monotonicity on the broad class of bounded
43


## Page 44


increasing schedules, and the fixed-point operator is not continuous on the broad Gaussian
compact class. These failures are not technical accidents. They reflect the same economic
force that motivates the model: remote liquidity states remain relevant at polynomial or-
der. To handle this difficulty, the paper separates the analysis into distinct steps. First,
it proves existence of Student-t fixed points on a tail-controlled compact class. Second, it
distinguishes fixed-point existence from the monotone limit order book interpretation. A
fixed point gives a candidate marginal-cost schedule consistent with informed-trader opti-
mality and competitive zero-profit pricing; monotonicity of the associated pricing schedule
is the additional condition that makes the solution an admissible limit order book. Third,
the paper studies learning and tail behavior along the selected solution. The learning result
shows that, despite heavy-tailed liquidity shocks, repeated order flow continues to reveal
the fundamental value under suitable stability conditions. Heavy-tailed noise slows learning,
but it does not eliminate information aggregation. The tail analysis then identifies explicit
regular-variation exponents for the marginal-cost schedule, the price schedule, and the in-
duced informed demand. These exponents show how the number of informed traders, the
endpoint behavior of beliefs, and the tail thickness of liquidity shocks determine the shape of
price impact. The model also clarifies the role of rare liquidity shocks in the far tail. In the
bounded-support environment studied here, sufficiently extreme executions are ultimately
dominated by the informed side of the market. This does not contradict the liquidity-shock
interpretation. Rather, it identifies the economic role of heavy-tailed liquidity demand: it
determines the crossover scale at which large order flow stops being plausibly attributable
to liquidity demand and becomes overwhelmingly informative. The economically relevant
region is precisely the pre-asymptotic range in which large trades are substantial but still
ambiguous. Overall, the paper provides a theory of how heavy-tailed liquidity shocks reshape
the limit order book. They flatten impact, slow learning, and require a fixed-point approach
adapted to tail behavior. The results suggest that empirical models of price impact and
market learning should treat the tail thickness of liquidity demand as a structural feature of
the trading environment, not merely as a volatility parameter.

In this appendix, we provide a microeconomic foundation for the myopic trading behavior
assumed in the the main text’s model; henceforth, baseline model. In the baseline model,
insiders behave as if they perfectly observe the fundamental value V and trade aggressively
in each round of trading. While this assumption yields a tractable fixed-point characteriza-
tion, it abstracts from the fact that in many real markets informed traders typically act on
imperfect and short-lived signals rather than on direct knowledge of the fundamental value.
To address this point, we extend the baseline model by assuming that in each round of trad-
ing insiders receive a noisy private signal about the asset fundamental value. Importantly,
insiders behave overconfidently: they treat the signal as if it were the true fundamental when
choosing their market order. Liquidity suppliers, however, correctly understand both the
signal structure and the traders’ behavioural bias. This creates a richer inference problem
in which extreme order flow may arise either from large signal realizations or from extreme
fundamentals. This extension provides two economic benefits. First, it gives a behavioural
micro-foundation for aggressive myopic trading: traders exploit transient signals before they
decay. Second, it resolves a potential interpretational issue of the baseline model, namely that
arbitrarily large orders can arise even when the fundamental value has bounded support. We
note that this extension preserves the tail expectation pricing logic in (3.4), but changes the
inference problem and, as a consequence, the equilibrium marginal cost at depth. Specifically,
