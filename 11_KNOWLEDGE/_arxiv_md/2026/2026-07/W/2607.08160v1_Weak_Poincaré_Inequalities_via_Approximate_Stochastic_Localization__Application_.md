WEAK POINCARÉ INEQUALITIES VIA APPROXIMATE STOCHASTIC LOCALIZATION:
APPLICATION TO SAMPLING THE SHERRINGTON–KIRKPATRICK MODEL
EWAN DAVIES, HOLDEN LEE, JUSPREET SINGH SANDHU, AND JONATHAN SHI
Abstract. We develop a new method for proving a weak functional inequality by first proving it
for a sufficiently regular sequence of distributions approximating the stochastic localization (SL)
process, and then transferring it to the desired distribution via regularity of the SL process and
conductance arguments. We use this strategy to prove a weak Poincaré inequality (WPI) holds for
the Gibbs measure of the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model when β < 1
2. A prior result of the au-
thors [DLSS26] proves the ASL process for the SK model satisfies the required regularity conditions.
A consequence of the WPI is that a much simpler algorithm—Glauber dynamics with a warm-
start—efficiently samples the Gibbs measure of the SK model at β < 1/2.
This is a significant
structural step towards resolution of the conjecture that Glauber dynamics mixes fast in the replica-
symmetric regime for the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model [BKMR25, Open Problem 15].

1. Introduction
Let µ be a probability measure on Ω⊆r ⋅Sn−1 ⊆Rn, for instance, Ω= {±1}n or √n⋅Sn−1. Consider a Markov
chain or process on Ωwith µ as its stationary distribution, for instance, Glauber dynamics or Langevin on
the sphere, respectively. A standard problem is to prove functional inequalities for µ, which would imply a
number of useful properties such as concentration of Lipschitz functions and fast mixing of the associated
dynamics. Simulating these dynamics would then give an efficient sampling algorithm.
Stochastic localization (SL) [Eld13] is a measure-valued stochastic process which almost surely converges to
a point mass δy, where y is a draw from the target measure µ. In the framework of sampling algorithms,
this has been used in two ways: first, as a framework for proving functional inequalities [CE22], and second,
as a sampling algorithm itself through a computational approximation; this is the framework of algorithmic
stochastic localization (ASL) [EAMS25]. While the former gives stronger results—namely, efficient sampling
from running the natural Markov chain or process—it also requires stronger conditions. When µ is a Gibbs
distribution for a spin glass model, these conditions can be very challenging to show. The appeal of ASL
has been that it gives an efficient sampling algorithm under weaker conditions.
This motivates the following question:
Can we show functional inequalities under weaker conditions than required in the SL framework,
namely those that suffice for ASL?
We show an affirmative answer to this question for weak Poincaré inequalities (WPI) under natural conditions
where an ASL process can track the SL process up to O(1) error. In particular, this will apply for us to
prove a weak Poincaré inequality for the Gibbs measure of the the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (SK) model up
to β < 1

1.2. Discussion. Prior work on spherical spin glass models used uniform bounds on Dym(y) = Cov(µy)
(which amounts to Assumption 1.2 on the true magnetization) to prove functional inequalities via SL, with
this being weakened to high-probability bounds to prove a weak Poincaré inequality [HMRW25]. This is
far from what is currently known for the SK model for 0.295 < β < 0.5, namely second moment bounds on
Cov(µy) [DLSS26]; such bounds were “integrated” via Grönwall’s inequality to show Assumption 1.1.
The parameters of our weak Poincaré inequality are substantially weaker than what is known for the spherical
p-spin model at high temperature via SL [HMRW25]. A (n−O(1),o(n−1))-WPI would allow using simulated
annealing to obtain negligible guarantees in TV distance (using O(n) temperatures); however, taking ε =
O ( 1
n) in Theorem 1.4 already gives a trivial weak Poincaré constant of exp(−O(n)).
Our weak Poincaré inequality allows showing mixing from a warm start in L∞divergence, up to ε in time
nexp(O(1/ε)). We obtain a higher complexity for the end-to-end algorithm, as we only approximately obtain
a warm start in L∞divergence. We find it surprising that a weak Poincaré inequality can be proved under
such minimal assumptions, and expect that stronger assumptions—left to future work—will be necessary to
strengthen it.
We note that while Algorithm 1 for the SK model simplifies the former algorithm of [DLSS26], the proof of its
correctness relies on the same assumptions. Hence, for the SK model, our result shortcuts the algorithm but
not the proof. The bulk of [DLSS26] is occupied by showing these assumptions (implied by the desiderata
therein)2. We find it remarkable that although the tools introduced there—ASL and Jarzynski’s equality—
are algorithmic in nature and intended as such, they can (1) function to prove a purely mathematical result,
and (2) be absorbed into the analysis of an algorithm that does not rely on simulating these processes.
1.3. Related work. In the context of sampling, stochastic localization (originally developed in [Eld13])
has been used in two ways: first as a way of proving functional inequalities [CE22] and hence establishing
rapid mixing of the standard Markov chain (e.g., Glauber dynamics), and second, algorithmically through
approximating a SDE [EAMS22]. For spin glasses, this has been applied both to the SK model on the
hypercube and spherical p-spin models.
Functional inequalities and efficient sampling algorithms for the SK model for β < 1 has been a longstanding
open problem [SZ81, MPVT88, BKMR25]. Using localization schemes, a modified log-Sobolev inequality
has been proved up to β ≈0.295 [EKZ22, AJK+22, AKV24]. Algorithmic stochastic localization has been
used to give efficient sampling for the SK model for the entire regime β < 1, but with error measured under

sampling guarantee for algorithmic stochastic localization to one for simulated annealing? We show that an
ASL guarantee essentially implies a WPI—just not of the strength required for vanilla simulated annealing.
Given the difficulty of proving rapid mixing, various works have consider weaker notions of mixing. [LMR+24]
show that locally stationary distributions with respect to Glauber dynamics are also locally stationary with
respect to restricted Gaussian dynamics (RGD). [RW25] relate the behavior or RGD to approximate message
passing (AMP). We note that their use of localization schemes to relate the behavior of Glauber dynamics
and RGD bears resemblance to our decomposition relating Glauber dynamics with the proximal sampler;
their RGD can be thought of as a rank-1 version of the proximal sampler.
We rely on a decomposition theorem for the weak Poincaré inequality, following a line of work on decom-
position theorems, including for the spectral gap or Poincaré inequality [MR02, ALV18, GLR18], restricted
spectral gap [GBZ25], and s-conductance [Zho25]. [GRW26] give related comparison results for the weak
Poincaré inequality. Decomposition theorems have been used to analyze sampling from multimodal distribu-
tions [GLR18], as well as data augmentation and Gibbs samplers including hybrid (Metropolis-within-Gibbs)
variants [Qin25]. They enable local-to-global results for Markov chains [Opp18] and appear in another guise
in localization schemes [CE22]. [Qin25] gives a unifying framework for many of these decomposition theorems.
For distributions on Rn, the proximal sampler was introduced in [LC22], with improved analyses given in
[CCSW22, FYC23]. The (A)SL process is a reparameterization of a standard denoising diffusion model [Mon23],
and these models have been independently discovered by the machine learning community for use in gener-
ative modeling. A long line of work has established efficient sampling under access to an approximate score
function [CCL+22, CLL23, BDBDD23]. Jarzynski’s equality [Jar97] is an identity in statistical mechanics
based on the Feynman-Kac formula [Kac49], which has recently seen wide application in sampling and in
machine learning [VPBN23, AVE24], with theoretical guarantees given in [GTC25].
1.4. Technical overview and organization of paper. We first give preliminaries in §2.
In §3, we present the keystone of the proof, a decomposition theorem for the weak Poincaré inequality
(WPI) which reduces the task of proving a WPI for Glauber to proving a WPI (1) for the time-T0 localized
distribution (X ∣Y ) and (2) for the time-T0 proximal sampler (Y ). The proximal sampler is the sampler
that iteratively alternates between sampling X ∣Y and then the posterior Y ∣X, where X is a Gaussian
noised observation of Y given by the SL process. The result follows as a corollary (Corollary 3.2) of a more
general decomposition theorem for a joint distribution on (X,Y ) (Theorem 3.1), where a WPI for conditional
distributions X ∣Y and for the alternating Gibbs chain on Y implies a WPI for the joint chain; projecting
onto X then gives the WPI for the marginal distribution.
In §4, we prove a WPI for the time-T0 proximal sampler (Lemma 4.5) by first showing it for Langevin for
the SL distribution pT0 via an isoperimetric argument, namely s-conductance (Theorem 4.4). The idea of
the proof is given in Figure 1.1. First, choose T1 > T0; regularity of ASL gives a log-Sobolev inequality for
̂pT1. Drift error gives bounded KL divergence to pT1, which allows pT1 to have smaller density, but not too
much larger density. Unfortunately, regions of smaller density can “disconnect” pT1 and inhibit proving a
functional inequality for pT1. Instead, we note that by construction of the SL process, pT0 for T0 < T1 is a

The remaining sections turn the weak Poincaré inequality for the SK model into an algorithmic result by
establishing a warm start that allows efficient sampling, which may be of independent interest.
To motivate §6, note that the ASL distribution satisfies a LSI and approximates the SL distribution; however
its density is not explicit. [DLSS26] define explicit distributions ϱt in terms of the TAP free energy which
is a reweighting of the ASL distribution (via Jarzynski’s equality), which also well-approximate the SL
distribution. We will refer to ϱt as annealed distributions. We show that under the general Assumption 6.1
(in the special case of a constant initial distribution y0 = 0)—that ϱt is a reweighting with weights defined
by a ODE with Lipschitz drift—which holds for the SK model, ϱt also satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality. To
show this, we first prove a perturbation result for finite dimensional Gaussian space (Lemma 6.6), and then
extend it to the infinite-dimensional path space of Brownian motion via a limiting argument that uses the
decomposition of the reweighed measure into a finite-dimensional cylindrical part and an infinite-dimensional
Gaussian tail (Theorem 6.7). Then, using the fact that the end-point map to evaluate ϱT is Lipschitz, we
“transport” this path-space LSI and show it holds for ϱT in standard Eucliden space (Theorem 6.11). Hence,
we can sample ϱt directly, and this is a suitable candidate for a warm start.
To obtain a warm start to the SK measure, we first obtain a warm start for the ASL distribution ̂pT0 and
then approximately sample from the localized measure. In §7, we show that ϱT0 is a warm start for ̂pT0
through a similar scaffolding approach as in §4, thinking of these as approximate Gaussian noisings of ϱT1
and ̂pT1 for some T1 > T0.
Finally, in §8, we put everything together to prove our main algorithmic result (Theorem 1.6).
