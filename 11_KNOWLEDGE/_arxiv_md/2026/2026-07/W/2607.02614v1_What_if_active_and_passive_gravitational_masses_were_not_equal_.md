What if active and passive gravitational masses were not equal?
Domenico Giulini
Institute for Theoretical Physics
Leibniz University of Hannover
Appelstraße 2, 30167 Hannover, Germany
(Dated: July 7, 2026)
At first glance, combining Newton’s laws of motion with his law of gravitation seems straight-
forward.
Students learn to distinguish inertial from gravitational mass and that their empirical
equality is a remarkable fact about nature that will later serve as the conceptual gateway to general
relativity. However, a closer look reveals a further and often neglected distinction within Newto-
nian gravity: that between active and passive gravitational mass. A common textbook argument
maintains that these must be equal, for otherwise Newton’s third law would be violated. We review
and critically re-examine this familiar reasoning and show that the supposed theoretical proof is not
compelling. Our analysis highlights subtle structural assumptions within Newtonian mechanics and
offers physics teachers and researchers a fresh opportunity to explore foundational questions with
potentially interesting applications in observational astronomy. In addition, an extended appendix,
which is not part of the published AJP paper, offers some mathematical background material on the
Galilean group and its action as group of dynamical symmetries for the type of dynamical equations
considered here.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Presumably Hermann Bondi was the first person to
point out in 1957 that Newton’s laws of motion combined
with Newton’s theory of gravitation gives rise to three
concepts of mass that should be carefully distinguished.1
Let us start by briefly recalling what they are.
Newton’s second law states that any force F acting
on a (pointlike) body leads to a change in its state of
motion. It also states that this change is characterized
by an acceleration a = ¨x proportional to F. The constant
of proportionality is called the body’s inertial mass mi;
hence
F = mi a = mi ¨x .
(1)
Newton’s law of gravitation states that a (pointlike)
body placed at position y in space produces a gravita-
tional field g whose strength at position x is
g(x) = G ma
y −x
|y −x|3 .
(2)
Here G is Newton’s universal constant and ma is the
body’s active gravitational mass. This name is chosen
because ma characterizes the body’s ability to act as the
source of a gravitational field.
Finally, given a gravitational field g and a (pointlike)
test-body at position x. The force felt by the body will
be proportional to the gravitational field at the position
of the body,
F = mp g(x) .
(3)
The constant of proportionality, mp, is called the body’s
passive gravitational mass.
From observations of suitable quantities one may then
hope to obtain upper bounds on the possible inequal-
ity of the masses attributed to a body.
Assuming for
the moment a strict equality between active and passive
gravitational mass, i.e. ma = mp =: mg, any variation
of the ratio mi/mg between physical bodies would lead
to different free-fall orbits with identical initial condi-
tions and hence to a violation of the universality of free
fall (UFF), also known as the weak equivalence principle
(WEP). WEP lies at the foundation of General Relativ-
ity and has been tested again and again. Upper bounds
on relative variations are currently at the 10−15 level, like
in the recently completed MICROSCOPE mission.2
In contrast, far fewer observations exist concerning the
equality of ma and mp, though meanwhile their accu-
racy almost matches that of WEP.3–5 The considerably
smaller interest devoted in the literature to the ques-
tion of the equality of the two gravitational masses is al-
most certainly connected with the widespread belief that
equality allegedly follows from the equations themselves
for reasons of internal consistency.
The present paper
examines this claim in some detail.
We start with a review of the argument in section II.
In section III we show that the gravitational two (or N)
body problem is, in fact, completely free of any of the
anomalies mentioned, so that no issue of inner consis-
tency exists. In sections IV and V we consider compound
bodies held in rigid shape by non gravitational forces. In
that case anomalies may exist, depending on the nature
of the binding forces. All this leads to a deeper under-
standing of Newtonian concepts on the level of an intro-
ductory course on analytical mechanics.

V.
THE SELF-GRAVITATING HANDLE:
NATURE OF CONSTRAINING FORCE
In the second case we have seen that Xµ moves along
a straight line according to (36) and Xi rotates around
Xµ with constant angular velocity ω on a circle with
radius S12 d(mred|i/µtot). This just means that it is the
point Xµ rather than Xi on the line-segment between
the two masses about which the handle rigidly rotates.
The difference between these two points is proportional
to S12.
In the first case, however, Xµ as well as Xi are accel-
erated. The acceleration of Xµ has two contributions,
one proportional to ω2 and the other independent of
ω.
The acceleration of Xi consists just of that latter
part and can be understood as due to a net total force
Ftot := F12 + F21 acting on the total system, which it
then accelerates according to Newton’s second law:
Ftot = mtot|i ¨Xi .
(45)
In particular, if initially released at rest without rota-
tion, the whole handle will start a constantly accelerated
motion along a straight line in the direction x1 −x2 for
S12 > 0, and in the opposite direction x2−x1 for S12 < 0.
The explanation of this apparently unphysical behav-
ior is based on (45) and has often been repeated following

Bondi1. However, it may not be as obvious a consequence
of Newtonian physics as it might seem at first. In fact, it
uses the first and second axiom to calculate the motion
caused by forces that violate Newton’s third law. And,
indeed, as we have seen above, it rests on the tacit as-
sumption that the constraint force that keeps the masses
a fixed distance d apart is of a particular form, namely
that corresponding to the λ terms in (26) and not that
corresponding to the λ′ terms in (27). Looking at these
two equations it is clear what the difference between these
constraint forces is: The λ terms in (26) correspond to
constraint forces that cause equal accelerations to equal
inertial masses, whereas the λ′ terms in (27) cause equal
accelerations to equal µ-masses. The first case will pre-
sumably be argued to be the case if the binding forces
are “standard” Newtonian forces, like a spring or, more
fundamentally, electromagnetic forces.
Finally, coming back to observational tests of the
equivalence between active and passive gravitational
mass3–5,7, one may wonder how observationally a dis-
tinction is made between the “positions” Xi and Xµ for
extended objects like the Moon. In other words: which
is the right position observable whose anomalous motion
reveals S12 ̸= 0, and how do we realize it operationally?

Now, what is a semidirect product? We will not give
the general definition here since this would lead us more
deeply into group-theoretic concepts that we wish to
avoid. Rather, we concentrate on a specific class of exam-
ples within which the Galilean group falls. Let GL(Rn)
be the group of invertible n×n matrices with real entries
and G ⊂GL(Rn) any subgroup. G acts on Rn as usual by
multiplying an n component column-vector v ∈Rn with
an n × n matrix g ∈G. So we know what gv means.
Now, next to G we consider another group, namely just
Rn, which is indeed an abelian group if group multiplica-
tion is defined to be vector addition. We consider it as the
group of translations. The zero vector 0 being the group’s
neutral element and the inverse of v being just −v.
Whenever we have two groups we can form the Carte-
sian product of their underlying sets; in our case Rn ×G.
One way to make this into a group is to define multipli-
cation component-wise, i.e. (v′, g′)(v, g) = (v′ + v , g′g).

also denoted by Rn × G. Now, the semidirect product

(54)

that v has an additional linear map g′ applied to it. So
it is this additional action of G on Rn that modifies the
addition of vectors in the first slot.

SYMMETRIES OF DYNAMICAL LAWS
The general statement of a dynamical symmetry needs
the following input34:
1. A set K of kinematically possible trajectories.
2. A subset D ⊂K of dynamically possible trajecto-
ries.
3. A group G that acts effectively35 on K.
The statement that a group G acts as dynamical symme-
tries then just means that G maps D to itself; that is, it
maps solutions to solutions.
