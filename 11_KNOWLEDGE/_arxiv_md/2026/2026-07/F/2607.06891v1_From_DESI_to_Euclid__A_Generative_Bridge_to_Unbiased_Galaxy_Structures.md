From DESI to Euclid: A Generative Bridge to Unbiased Galaxy Structures
Renhao Ye (叶人豪)
1, 2 and Shiyin Shen (沈世银)

ABSTRACT
Ground-based seeing imprints a size-dependent bias on galaxy structural parameters, yet the space-
based imaging needed to remove it currently covers only a small fraction of the sky. We close this
gap with a generative model that translates DESI imaging of Bright Galaxy Survey (BGS) targets
into Euclid VIS images. A Fourier-domain analysis confirms that it recovers structure down to 0.′′37
(from the 1.′′41 DESI r-band baseline), a ∼3.8× improvement in resolution. Although it stops short of
the 0.′′16 Euclid VIS resolution, this recovery already de-biases the structural parameters relative to
the DESI r-band structure measurements: the Petrosian radius bias falls to +0.′′075 (from −0.′′870),
independent of galaxy size; the S´ersic-radius (Re) bias drops to −0.′′018 (from −0.′′322); and the S´ersic-
index bias to +0.093 (from +0.262). We release these translations over the Euclid DR1 footprint as
the Euclid-resolution BGS (E-BGS), which can be blindly validated once DR1 is public.

1. INTRODUCTION
Galaxy structure traces the spin, gas cooling, and as-
sembly history of dark matter halos.
At the popula-
tion level, this physics is read off scaling relations such
as the mass–size relation (S. Shen et al. 2003), along
which star-forming and quiescent galaxies follow sepa-
rate sequences that evolve with redshift (A. van der Wel
et al. 2014; L. A. Mowla et al. 2019), marking out chan-
nels of structural growth. Mapping these channels re-
quires accurate structural measurements of the compact
and dwarf galaxies at their extremes. The Dark Energy
Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) Bright Galaxy Survey
(BGS; C. Hahn et al. 2023) now provides far larger sam-
ples of both at low redshift than were previously avail-
able.
These BGS galaxies, however, are imaged from the
ground, where atmospheric seeing limits resolution: a
quarter of BGS galaxies have r-band half-light radii4
smaller than the median PSF FWHM (≈1.′′3; A. Dey
et al. 2019), leaving them under-resolved. In this regime,
structural parameters acquire size-dependent systematic
biases (I. Trujillo et al. 2001). Space-based imaging re-

moves this limit directly: Euclid’s Wide Survey reaches
FWHM = 0.′′16 in the VIS band (E. Collaboration et al.
2024, 2025a) and will ultimately cover the full BGS foot-
print, but so far overlaps only ∼63.1 deg2 of it, rising to
the ∼1,900 deg2 of the first Euclid Data Release (DR1)
in November 2026. The vast majority of BGS galaxies
therefore still lack the high-resolution imaging needed
for unbiased structural measurements.
Obtaining unbiased structure from under-resolved
imaging has long meant accounting for the PSF at the
measurement stage. Analytic prescriptions correct raw
S´ersic parameters for the seeing-convolved profile (I.
Trujillo et al. 2001); two-dimensional forward modeling
instead fits a PSF-convolved parametric profile directly
(H. Gao & L. C. Ho 2017; J.-H. Wang et al. 2024); and
artificial-degradation experiments calibrate the residual
biases empirically (M. Giavalisco et al. 1996; S.-Y. Yu
et al. 2023; E. Sazonova et al. 2026). These methods
recover galaxy sizes and other geometric quantities to
within ∼10% (E. Sazonova et al. 2026), but they share
a common floor: once a galaxy falls below the PSF scale,
its central light profile is overwhelmed by the PSF and
the concentration that separates a compact bulge from
a disk (C. J. Conselice 2003) collapses toward a sin-
gle PSF-set value (J.-H. Wang et al. 2024). This lost
central information cannot be restored by any post-

Data-driven generative models take a different route:
rather than correcting a single degraded image, they
learn the joint distribution of ground- and space-based
imaging and reconstruct structure beyond the classi-
cal deconvolution limit (K. Schawinski et al. 2017; U.
Akhaury et al. 2022; D. Zhang et al. 2026). But this
raises a new question—whether the recovered struc-
ture is genuinely constrained by the data or invented
by the learned prior. Probabilistic reconstructions can
be largely prior-driven at low signal-to-noise (A. Adam
et al. 2024), and recovering high-resolution structure
from a single degraded image is a one-to-many inverse
problem, with no guarantee that higher-order physical
information survives (H. Ruan et al. 2025). The relevant
criterion is therefore not pixel-perfect reproduction, but
whether the recovered structure is strictly constrained
by the ground-based data and remains unbiased across
multiple structural parameters.
In this work, we train a bridge diffusion model (G.-H.
Liu et al. 2023) on observed DESI–Euclid image pairs
over their 63.1 deg2 overlap, learning a conditional map-
ping from DESI to Euclid-resolution images. We vali-
date it against the two criteria above—that the recov-
ered structure is data-constrained and that the param-
eters measured from it are unbiased—and release the
resulting translations over the Euclid DR1 footprint as
E-BGS, an advance prediction testable once DR1 is pub-
lic. The paper is organized as follows: Sections 2 and 3
describe the data, sample construction, and the method;
Section 4 presents the validation against these two cri-
teria in turn; Section 5 concludes.
2. DATA
2.1. DESI
The DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys (LIS; A. Dey et al.
2019) comprise DECaLS (DR10, southern footprint)
and BASS+MzLS (H. Zou et al. 2017; D. R. Silva
et al. 2016) (DR9, northern footprint), imaged in grz at
0.′′262 pixel−1. We use only the r and z bands (Fig. 1):
the g band lies almost entirely below the Euclid VIS
bandpass, and the i band is dropped for footprint uni-
formity, being unavailable over BASS/MzLS.
We draw our sample from the DESI BGS targets (C.
Hahn et al. 2023), which split into BGS Bright (r < 19.5,
flux-limited) and BGS Faint (19.5 < r < 20.175, with a
fiber-magnitude–color cut for redshift efficiency).
Figure 1. Transmission curves for the four DECam bands
(g, r, i, z; representative of the Legacy Surveys filters) and
the Euclid VIS channel (∼550–900 nm).
2.2. Euclid
The Euclid mission (R. Laureijs et al. 2011; E. Collab-
oration et al. 2024) performs a wide-area survey with the
VIS imager (E. Collaboration et al. 2025a) and the Near-
Infrared Spectrometer and Photometer (NISP). Quick
Data Release 1 (Q1; E. Collaboration et al. 2025b) pro-
vides 63.1 deg2 over three Euclid Deep Fields at nominal
Wide Survey depth (IE = 26.2 mag, 5σ, point source).
We use the VIS broadband (IE, ∼550–900 nm) on a
0.′′1 pixel−1 grid, a single channel spanning the DESI r, i,
and z bands (Fig. 1). These space-based VIS images are
the ground truth (GT) against which we evaluate all re-
covered structure and parameters. Rather than running
our own source extraction, we adopt the astrometrically
calibrated mosaics and source positions of the Multi-
Epoch Resampled (MER;
Euclid Collaboration et al.
2025) data product.
2.3. Sample Construction
Each BGS target in the Q1 footprint is matched to
a VIS-detected, non-spurious, non-stellar MER source
(full flag list in Appendix C), giving paired DESI–Euclid
imaging for every galaxy. We partition the pairs by sky
region rather than by random draw. The test set is the
region where Q1 and DESI DR1 overlap; the remaining,
spatially disjoint pairs form the training and validation
sets.
For each galaxy, DESI r and z bricks are repro-
jected onto the Euclid VIS pixel grid (0.′′1 pixel−1) and
128 × 128-pixel (12.′′8) cutouts are extracted from both
surveys with Cutout2D (T. A. Collaboration et al.
2022), all centered on the common Euclid MER position
(reprojection and multi-brick handling in Appendix D).
This yields 82,830 photometrically selected pairs for
training and validation, split 80:20 into 66,359 and
16,471; the test region contributes a further 4,165 pairs.

4. RESULTS
4.1. Example Predictions
Figure 3 illustrates examples of randomly selected
test-set galaxies, demonstrating a consistent enhance-
ment in both spatial resolution and imaging depth across
all cutouts. Our model successfully recovers structures
that appear highly blurred or highly uncertain in the
DESI r- and z-band images—including spiral arms, close
companion galaxies, and disk substructures—as con-
firmed by GT. The pixel-level residuals remain minimal
overall, with large values primarily reflecting minor spa-
tial mismatches or slight blurring of fine substructures.
These visual results are qualitative; the following section
quantifies the spatial scale down to which the recovered
structure is genuinely data-constrained. A random se-

4.2. Structural Fidelity
To quantify this limit, we must determine the spa-
tial scale down to which the recovered structures are
genuinely data-constrained. A direct pixel-to-pixel com-
parison is mathematically unfeasible, however, because
our prediction is a conditional expectation—an ensem-
ble average over degenerate solutions that need not re-
produce the exact phase realization of any single GT
image.
We therefore assess structural fidelity in the
frequency domain using two complementary diagnostics
(Figure 4): the azimuthally averaged power spectrum to
quantify power restoration, and the Fourier Ring Corre-
lation (FRC; M. van Heel & M. Schatz 2005) to measure
phase coherence with the GT.
The power spectrum (Figure 4a) measures the am-
plitude of structure at each spatial scale. Atmospheric
seeing strips the DESI r and z bands of up to three or-
ders of magnitude of power below ∼1′′ relative to the
Euclid GT; E-BGS adds this power back, tracking the
GT spectrum well above both DESI bands. It falls a
constant ∼0.5 dex short only below ∼1′′, the residual
scatter of the averaged solutions where the input is most
degraded. Matching the GT in power, however, shows
only that the right amount of structure is present, not
that it sits in the right place: the power spectrum is
blind to phase. We therefore turn to the FRC, which
tests phase agreement directly.

4.3. Structural-Parameter Recovery
We evaluate three structural parameters: the ellipti-
cal Petrosian radius (RPet; V. Petrosian 1976; A. W.
Graham & S. P. Driver 2005), the S´ersic effective radius
(Re), and the S´ersic index (n). For each, the residual ∆
is the value measured on the GT minus the value mea-
sured on the method under test. Because the prediction
reproduces only the single GT channel, the DESI resid-
uals below carry an unavoidable band-pass offset that is
not our target. What we target is the value-dependent
bias, the dependence of the residual on the parameter
value, alongside how closely the recovered measurements
match the GT.
We measure size first with the Petrosian radius (top
row of Figure 5). It is non-parametric, assuming no form
for the light profile, but it cannot account for the way the
PSF spreads the galaxy light outward, so the Petrosian
radius overestimates the size (I. Trujillo et al. 2001; R.
Geda et al. 2022). The DESI r band shows this directly
(∆RPet = −0.′′870), and the overestimate is larger for
smaller galaxies. E-BGS removes it (∆RPet = +0.′′075),
decoupling the measurement from galaxy size.
The S´ersic radius Re and index n are fitted with
statmorph on a clean subset (S´ersic-fit χ2

a constant; E-BGS reproduces the GT and recovers Re
with near-zero bias (∆Re = −0.′′018, from −0.′′322 for
DESI r). For n (bottom row), the DESI offset is value-
dependent rather than a constant shift, larger at high n,
where DESI r underestimates and z overestimates. E-
BGS reduces the bias to ∆n = +0.093 and removes this
value-dependence, because it restores the central light
profile that the fit needs to constrain n.
The scatter of these three parameters, by contrast, is
not reduced. For the radii it lies between the two DESI
bands (σNMAD = 0.′′371 and 0.′′165). For n it is larger
(0.609) for two reasons: r and z diverge to opposite sides
of the GT toward high n, so the VIS target itself carries a
wider n distribution; and for the unresolved central pro-
file E-BGS predicts one plausible solution rather than
the unique truth, so n, which depends on that profile, is
correspondingly more uncertain. For the mass–size re-
lation, though, it is the bias, not this scatter, that shifts
the slope and zero-point. The E-BGS parameters are
therefore ready for population-level studies of the BGS
at Euclid resolution.
5. CONCLUSION
We have trained an I2SB on real DESI–Euclid Q1
image pairs to translate DESI imaging to Euclid VIS
resolution, and validated it against two criteria: that
the recovered structure is constrained by the data, and
that the parameters measured from it are unbiased. A
Fourier-domain analysis confirms the first, establishing
a trusted scale of 0.′′37.
This recovery is physically
bounded, stopping short of the 0.′′16 VIS resolution, yet
it is already enough to meet the second: across the Pet-
rosian radius, S´ersic radius, and S´ersic index, E-BGS
removes the systematic bias and its dependence on pa-
rameter value, recovering measurements unbiased at Eu-
clid resolution. The scatter is not reduced—narrowing
it would require information the under-resolved DESI
input does not contain—but for population studies such
as the mass–size relation it is the bias, not the scatter,
that shifts the inferred relations.
We release these translations over the Euclid DR1
footprint as E-BGS, providing Euclid-resolution struc-
tural parameters for population-level studies of the full
BGS ahead of Euclid DR1, to be blindly validated once
DR1 is public.

The masked loss (Equation 3) applies a binary validity map that excludes tile-edge padding and upstream artifacts,

A.4. Network and Optimization
The UNet uses base channel width 64, multipliers (1, 2, 4, 4), 2 residual blocks per resolution, and cross-attention
at relative resolutions 1/16 and 1/8. We optimize with AdamW (I. Loshchilov & F. Hutter 2017) at learning rate
10−4, batch size 256, with a 1000-step linear warm-up followed by linear decay to 10% over 105 steps, and EMA decay
0.999. We apply no data augmentation: each pair is centre-cropped to 128 × 128 with no rotation or flipping. Full
hyperparameters are listed in Table 2.
A.5. Model Selection
The configuration in Table 2 was fixed by a two-stage selection, both stages carried out on data disjoint from the
test set. Within a training run, the checkpoint is taken at the epoch of minimum validation per-pixel χ2
ν between the
prediction and the GT. Across runs, the model size (channel width, multipliers, residual blocks, attention heads) and
the noise schedule (T, βmax, and the number of reverse steps) were tuned over 25 trials with Optuna (T. Akiba et al.
2019), each trial scored on a held-out 5,000-galaxy validation subset by the bias and scatter of its recovered S´ersic and
photometric parameters. The released model is the trial minimizing this score.

B. S´ERSIC FITTING CONFIGURATION
Single-component S´ersic profiles are fitted to the Euclid GT images, E-BGS images, and DESI observations using
statmorph (V. Rodriguez-Gomez et al. 2019), with bounds identical across the three datasets. The fit is bounded as
60 ≤x, y ≤68 pixel for the center in the native 128 × 128 cutout frame, 0.01 ≤Re ≤100 pixel, 0.25 ≤n ≤8 for the
S´ersic index, and 0.001 ≤ϵ ≤0.95 for the ellipticity; position angle, total magnitude, and central amplitude are fitted
unbounded.
C. SAMPLE SELECTION FLAGS
Training and validation use BGS targets selected photometrically from the Legacy Surveys (BGS BRIGHT/BGS FAINT;
r < 20.175; C. Hahn et al. 2023), without a redshift requirement, while the spectroscopic test set is drawn from the DESI
DR1 catalog (DESI. Collaboration et al. 2025) as primary galaxy entries with no redshift-fitting failure (ZCAT PRIMARY
= True, SPECTYPE = "GALAXY", ZWARN == 0). The corresponding Q1 MER sources are required to satisfy VIS DET =
1, DET QUALITY FLAG = 0, SPURIOUS FLAG = 0, SPURIOUS PROB ≤0.1, and POINT LIKE PROB ≤0.1.
D. MULTI-BRICK REPROJECTION DETAILS
For each Euclid MER tile, overlapping DESI bricks are identified from the LIS brick index, and their r- and z-band
images and inverse-variance maps are reprojected from the native 0.′′262 pixel−1 scale onto the tile grid (0.′′1 pixel−1)
via WCS-based target-to-source coordinates computed with scipy.ndimage.map coordinates (order=1). Where a
tile pixel is covered by multiple bricks, contributions are combined per-pixel: direct assignment for one brick, mean
for two, and median for three or more. DESI flux is preserved in native units with no photometric rescaling.
