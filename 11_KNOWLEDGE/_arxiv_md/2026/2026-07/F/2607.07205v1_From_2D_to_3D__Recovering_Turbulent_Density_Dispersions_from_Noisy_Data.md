ABSTRACT
Turbulence plays a central role in shaping the structure and dynamics of the interstellar medium
(ISM), governing the star formation rate (SFR) and the initial mass function (IMF). A key conse-
quence of turbulence is the generation of density fluctuations, which regulate the amount of dense
gas available for star formation. Accurate measurements of the three-dimensional (3D) turbulent den-
sity dispersion are therefore essential for understanding molecular-cloud structure and star formation.
However, observations typically provide only two-dimensional (2D) column densities and are often af-
fected by measurement/detector noise. The Brunt method estimates the 3D density dispersion from
2D column-density maps, but it does not account for finite signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Here, we ex-
tend the method to recover the 3D turbulent density dispersion from noise-contaminated observations.
Using numerical simulations spanning a range of density perturbation amplitudes and noise types, we
identify a characteristic noise wavenumber, knoise, corresponding to the intersection of the signal and
noise spectra. Restricting the Brunt reconstruction to wavenumbers below knoise yields a denoised
density-dispersion estimate that closely reproduces the noise-free result. We provide a practical pre-
scription to determine knoise directly from the measurement SNR and image resolution. Alternatively,
if the noise spectrum is known, it can be subtracted directly from the observed spectrum, eliminating
the need to estimate knoise. The proposed correction recovers the noise-free density dispersion with
errors of ≲5% for SNR ≥3 and ≲15% for SNR ≥1, enabling substantially more reliable estimates of
turbulent density fluctuations from noisy column-density data.
Keywords: Hydrodynamics — Interstellar medium — Star Formation
1. INTRODUCTION
Turbulence plays a key role in star formation in the
interstellar medium (ISM). On one hand, the turbu-
lence creates overdensities in shocks on small scales, set-
ting the seeds for gravitational collapse. On the other
hand, it counteracts the effects of gravity on large scale,
preventing the clouds from collapsing as a whole and
thereby making star formation inefficient (see, e.g., Lar-
son 1981; Elmegreen & Scalo 2004; Mac Low & Klessen
2004; McKee & Ostriker 2007).
Turbulence may be
driven by interstellar events such as supernova explo-
sions, jets, galactic spiral arm dynamics, radiation from
massive stars, and accretion, with the ability to create
different mixtures of compressible gas motions, shearing
motions, and vorticity (Elmegreen 2009; Federrath et al.
2017).

Two main quantities that characterize turbulence are
the density variance σρ/ρ0 and the sonic Mach number
M. Their interrelation gives rise to the turbulence driv-
ing parameter, b, which is controlled by the turbulence
driving mechanism, and takes the form
b = σρ/ρ0/M,
(1)
where M = σv/cs, with the standard deviation of the
turbulent velocity fluctuations σv and the sound speed cs
(Padoan et al. 1997; Passot & V´azquez-Semadeni 1998;
Price et al. 2011; Konstandin et al. 2012). It is well doc-
umented that the driving parameter ranges from purely
solenoidal (divergence-free) at b = 1/3 to purely com-
pressive (curl-free) at b = 1, with a ‘natural mixture’
represented by b ∼0.4 (Federrath et al. 2008, 2010).
The driving parameter plays a key role for the star for-
mation rate (SFR) (Federrath & Klessen 2012) and the
initial mass function (IMF) (Mathew et al. 2023). Thus,

The key problem is that σρ/ρ0 quantifies the volumet-

typically only have access to the projected (column; 2D)
density fluctuations. Methods have been developed to
estimate the 3D fluctuations from the 2D density varia-

Kainulainen et al. 2014; Brunt & Federrath 2014; Yoon

instrument/measurement. Thus, the goal of this work
is to quantify the effects of the SNR for reconstructing
σρ/ρ0 from observational data, and to provide a correc-
tion to account for finite SNR.
The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 begins by
presenting the simulation setup and analysis regions
used to systematically quantify the effect of the SNR on
σρ/ρ0 reconstruction. Section 3 reviews the well-known
case of SNR →∞, accounting for different positions and

cation of the dependence of σρ/ρ0 on SNR is presented
in Section 4. Section 5 introduces the σρ/ρ0 reconstruc-
tion by a new denoising procedure. We conclude and
summarize in Section 6.
2. SIMULATIONS AND BASIC METHODS
We use the simulations from Dhawalikar et al. (2022),
which mimic a laboratory experiment of laser-induced,
shock-driven turbulence conducted at the National Igni-
tion Facility (NIF). The experiment takes place within
a shock tube target for which these simulations have
been made to aid in the understanding of hydrodynamic
shock-driven turbulence (Davidovits et al. 2022; Dhawa-
likar et al. 2022; Hew & Federrath 2023). Here we use it
as a testbed for developing a new denoising technique.
While the simulations and setup are designed for the
experiment, the data taken are sufficiently general and
representative of typical turbulent media, including the
star-forming interstellar medium, that the new method
developed here is applicable beyond the particular case
studied.
2.1. Hydrodynamic simulations
The simulations involve a cylindrical tube (symmetry
axis in the y direction) with a total length of 3.8 mm and
an outer diameter of 2.0 mm – see details of the setup in
Dhawalikar et al. (2022). The tube is filled with foam,
and the foam is constructed to contain voids, which gen-
erate turbulence when the shock passes through the tube
(for possible experimental setups that can achieve this,
see Hamilton et al. 2016; Nagel et al. 2017). Here we
compare 3 simulations that use foam voids with diame-
ters of 12.5, 50, and 100 µm. All materials are in pres-
sure equilibrium at the start of the simulation.
The
laser beam is incident and initialized at the bottom of
the shocktube along the y-direction inducing a plane-
parallel hydrodynamic shock wave.
Once the shock
breaks out of the cylinder in all spatial directions, it
is free to leave the computational domain, i.e., there is
no influence of the numerical boundaries on the results.
The simulations use a modified version of the adap-
tive mesh refinement (AMR) code FLASH (Fryxell et al.
2000; Dubey et al. 2008; Federrath et al. 2021) to solve
the 3D compressible hydrodynamical equations. They
use the robust HLL5R approximate Riemann scheme
(Waagan et al. 2011).
The computational grid has
768 × 1024 × 768 cells, capturing the turbulent dynam-
ics and the statistical properties of the turbulence as
demonstrated in a sample of works (Kitsionas et al.
2009; Price & Federrath 2010; Kritsuk et al. 2011b).
Figure 1 shows slices through the material density in
the simulation with 50 µm foam voids at the time when
the shock wave is about to exit the tube (at y ∼1.8 mm),
and there is a sufficiently large post-shock region of fully-
developed turbulence. More details on the simulations
can be found in Dhawalikar et al. (2022). For the present
study, the details of the experimental setup are not cru-
cial, since we extract information sufficiently far away
from the boundaries.
2.2. Turbulence analysis regions
Five different turbulence analysis regions are selected
(see Fig. 1), in order to study the variations of our results
with choice of region.
The dimensions of each region
are 0.4 mm × 0.4 mm × 0.4 mm, located in an analysis
plane centered around the symmetry axis of the tube
and at y = 1.5 mm, where the post-shock turbulence is
mostly isotropic and sufficiently far away from the edges
of the cylinder. We use cubic regions to reconstruct the
3D density dispersion from 2D (projected) data (Brunt
et al. 2010a).
We note that extensions of the Brunt
et al. (2010a) method to non-cubic domains have been
explored recently in Yoon & Cho (2024), and our results
can be extended to such cases, accordingly.

4. THE CASE OF FINITE SNR
Having confirmed the basic workings of the Brunt
method in the limit of a perfect signal without noise,
we are now ready to study the influence of a finite SNR
of the instrument/measurement on the density disper-
sion reconstruction.
4.1. Adding noise
To mimic instrument/measurement noise, we begin by
adding Gaussian (white) noise to the density-field pro-
jections of the simulations, to obtain 7 cases with the
following SNR levels: 0.01, 0.1, 1, 3, 5, 10, and 100.
The SNR is defined as the ratio of the intrinsic (pure
signal) standard deviation of the column density field
under consideration, divided by the standard deviation
of the respective noise field, generated with the Fourier
method outlined below.
Figure 2 shows examples of
density projections with SNR = 0.1, 1, 3, and 10. We
consider these four to be of greatest interest both vi-
sually and for analysis purposes as we will show in the
following sections.

with β = 0. Noise with different β can be generated
by producing a spherically symmetric real-valued power
law in Fourier space, kβ/2, such that when squared gives
a power spectrum of kβ.
The phase of each point in
Fourier space is then randomized by multiplying each
element by eiθ, where θ is random and uniformly dis-
tributed in [0, 2π]. The inverse Fourier transform gives a
random field with the target power-law noise spectrum,

4.2. The effect of noise on the Brunt method
Having added noise, we can now obtain the Brunt es-
timate, σρ/ρ0,Brunt, as described in Section 2.5, for each
noise level, which is shown in Figure 3. First, we note
that as SNR →∞, the σρ/ρ0,Brunt estimate approaches
the value from Sec. 3, as expected.
With decreasing
SNR, we see that σρ/ρ0,Brunt strongly increases, ulti-
mately overestimating the true σρ/ρ0 by ∼2 orders of
magnitude at SNR = 0.01.
Thus, σρ/ρ0,Brunt is com-
pletely dominated by noise in the limit SNR ≪1. How-
ever, even for SNR ∼1, the noise leads to an over-
estimate by factors of a few.
Considering the overall
uncertainties of ∼20% in the Brunt method in the
case of noise-free data, biases by factors of a few con-
stitute a major issue when considering measurements
with SNR ∼1, which are commonly encountered in real
observations and/or experiments. Thus, we need to de-
velop a method to correct for this noise bias.

6. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION
We revisited the Brunt et al. (2010b) method to esti-
mate the volumetric (3D) density dispersion of a turbu-
lent medium from projected (2D) column-density data.
Such situations are frequently encountered in observa-
tions of interstellar clouds, where only line-of-sight inte-
grated information is available, but also arise in terres-
trial experiments where detector systems provide only
integrated imaging data.

A key limitation of the original Brunt method is
that finite signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) leads to system-
atically overestimated density-dispersion measurements
(c.f. Fig. 3). We therefore developed a new technique
that accounts for finite-SNR effects and provides a de-
noised estimate of the 3D density dispersion.
Using simulation data representative of turbulent me-
dia found in the ISM and recent laser experiments, we
analyzed five turbulence regions (c.f. Fig. 1) and three
different initial density perturbations to assess the ro-
bustness of the method. We first verified the applicabil-
ity of the original Brunt method in the limit of a perfect
signal (SNR →∞), and then added white and brown
noise at SNR levels of 0.1, 1, 3, 5, 10, and 100.
By comparing noise-free, noise-contaminated, and
pure-noise spectra (c.f. Fig. 5), we identified a char-
acteristic wavenumber, knoise, corresponding to the in-
tersection of the signal and noise spectra (c.f. Figs. 6
and 7). Restricting the Brunt reconstruction to scales
k ≤knoise yields a denoised density-dispersion estimate
via Eq. (15) that closely matches the SNR →∞result
(c.f. Fig. 9) for SNR ≳1.
For practical applications, the denoised density disper-
sion can be obtained directly from Eq. (15) by summing
only to knoise. The required knoise follows from Eqs. (13)
and (14) once the measurement SNR and kmax are speci-
fied. Alternatively, if the noise spectrum is known, it can
be subtracted from the noise-contaminated spectrum be-
fore summation, in which case knoise is not required.
Although the calibration of knoise was obtained empir-
ically from the simulations studied here, we found that
it is largely insensitive to the amplitude of the density
perturbations (Tab. 2) and to the type of noise con-
sidered (Appendix C). Nevertheless, the density power
spectrum is known to depend on additional physical pa-
rameters, including the turbulent Mach number (Kim
& Ryu 2005), the driving mode of the turbulence, and,
in self-gravitating systems, the evolutionary state of the
cloud (e.g. Kritsuk et al. 2011a; Federrath & Klessen
2013; Girichidis et al. 2014). Future work should there-
fore test the robustness of the present noise-correction
method across a broader parameter space relevant to
molecular-cloud observations.
Overall, the proposed noise-correction method recov-
ers the SNR →∞density dispersion with an error of
≲5% for SNR ≥3 and ≲15% for SNR ≥1, mak-
ing the Brunt method applicable to substantially noisier
datasets than previously possible.
