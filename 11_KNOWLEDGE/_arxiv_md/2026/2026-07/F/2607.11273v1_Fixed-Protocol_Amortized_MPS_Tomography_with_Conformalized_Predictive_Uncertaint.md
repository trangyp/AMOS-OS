ABSTRACT
Quantum state tomography is sample-starved, and the states one prepares live
on a narrow, learnable manifold. A k=0 prior-only control shows that on con-
centrated families a prior estimate is already near-optimal, so “high fidelity at
few measurements” can be family memorization rather than tomography; genuine
measurement-efficiency needs a model that conditions on the measurements and
demonstrably uses them. On a shared matrix-product-state (MPS) core parameter-
ization we study two routes. Approach A learns a generative prior over MPS cores
with measurement-guided posterior inference (gold-standard-validated, but whose
few-measurement accuracy the control shows is largely the prior). Approach B,
our main proposal, is a fixed-protocol amortized MPS estimator trained once with
a gauge-invariant fidelity loss; we deliberately do not rest it on a permutation-
invariant set encoder (a plain MLP matches it). The decisive lever is the measure-
ment design: motivated by the fact that local reduced density matrices determine a
χ-MPS, conditioning on an informative local Pauli set rather than random strings
turns a modest, memorization-prone estimator into a high-fidelity one (≈0.95,
up to +0.59 over prior-only, decisively passing a shuffled-measurement control).
A dropout ensemble, conformally recalibrated, gives ≈90%-coverage intervals—
including for observables never measured, where a shot-based interval does not
exist. Quality holds as the system grows (fidelity 0.90 at n=10, gain growing
in n; 0.88 at bond dimension χ=4), the parameterization is polynomial (native
contraction to 20 qubits), and we close the loop on IBM hardware (5 states at
0.97 from hardware-measured Paulis). Our prior-only and shuffled controls are a
necessary integrity check we argue learned-QST work should adopt.

INTRODUCTION
Quantum state tomography (QST)—estimating an unknown quantum state ρ from measurement
data—is a cornerstone primitive for benchmarking and debugging quantum hardware. Its central
difficulty is sample complexity: full tomography of an n-qubit state requires a number of mea-
surements that scales exponentially in n, and each measurement (state preparation plus readout)
is expensive. Reducing the measurement budget needed to reach a target fidelity is therefore the
practically important axis.
Two broad families reduce this cost. Structural methods restrict the model class: compressed-
sensing QST exploits low rank (Gross et al., 2010), and matrix-product-state (MPS) tomography ex-
ploits low entanglement (Cramer et al., 2010; Lanyon et al., 2017), both recovering poly-parameter
states from poly-many measurements. Bayesian methods (Blume-Kohout, 2010; Granade et al.,
2016; Lukens et al., 2020) return a posterior—and hence uncertainty—but rely on hand-chosen pri-
ors on the dense state and do not learn from the distribution of states a device actually produces.
Neither family uses the key empirical fact that the states prepared in a given experiment are not
arbitrary: they lie on a narrow, structured manifold (e.g. ground states of a Hamiltonian family, or
the output states of a parameterized circuit).
A learned prior alone is not enough—and a control that shows it.
The tempting recipe is
to learn a generative prior over the family and invert by posterior sampling (Chung et al., 2022).

We build this (Approach A: a flow-matching prior over MPS cores with a measurement-guided
proximal/Doob-h-transform sampler)—a genuine, gold-standard-validated Bayesian posterior—and
it produces high fidelity at few measurements. But a simple control deflates the interpretation. On a
concentrated family—e.g. disordered paramagnetic ground states, which are all close to |+⟩⊗n—a
prior-only estimate that uses zero measurements (the family medoid) already reaches fidelity 0.997,
and shuffling the measurements (reconstructing from another state’s data) changes nothing. There,
“measurement-efficient tomography” is really nearest-neighbor prediction from a narrow distribu-
tion. The lesson is methodological: a learned-QST method must be judged against a k=0 prior-only
baseline and must use the measurements, verifiable by a shuffled-measurement test. We adopt both
as integrity controls throughout.
Conditioning on measurements, with the right measurement design.
To genuinely use the data,
we replace the unconditional prior+inference pipeline with an amortized, measurement-conditioned
estimator: a fixed-protocol encoder (a plain MLP or a permutation-invariant set-encoder) reads
the measured pairs {(Pj, oj)} and outputs the cores of an MPS, trained once on the family with a
gauge-invariant state-fidelity loss (Fig. 1). Conditioning is necessary but not sufficient: with random
Pauli strings the conditional model still only modestly beats prior-only, because random high-weight
Paulis are uninformative about low-entanglement states. The decisive lever is the measurement de-
sign. A classical result—local reduced density matrices determine a χ-MPS (Cramer et al., 2010)—
says the informative observables are local (Prop. 3). We condition on a cheap local set (weight-1
and nearest-neighbor weight-2 Paulis), and also test the theory-complete 3-site-window design; both
work, whereas random Paulis do not. Switching from random to informative-local measurements
turns a modest, memorization-prone estimator into a high-fidelity one that clearly beats prior-only
and passes the shuffled control.
Why cores.
The MPS-core parameterization is load-bearing: a generative model over the dense
2n state vector underfits already at n = 6, whereas the conditional model over O(nχ2) cores learns
the family and reconstructs it; and the transfer-matrix contraction is polynomial, so the pipeline runs
to 20 qubits without ever forming the state vector. MPS cores carry a gauge (a bond-wise change
of basis); we fix a deterministic coordinatization (left-canonical QR) but do not oversell it—it is a
coordinate choice, not a state-level invariant (Prop. 2), and we train the model with a gauge-invariant
target so the choice does not bias reconstruction.
Contributions.
• Integrity controls for learned QST. A k=0 prior-only baseline and a shuffled-
measurement test, showing a learned prior can look good without doing tomography, and
that these controls are necessary. Approach B passes both; the prior-only/unconditional
route does not.
• A generative prior over tensor-network cores (Approach A). A flow-matching prior
over canonicalized MPS cores with measurement-guided (Doob h-transform / proximal)
Bayesian posterior inference—to our knowledge the first learned generative distribution
over tensor-network cores—validated against a gold-standard importance sampler. The
integrity controls show its few-measurement accuracy leans on the prior, which motivates
Approach B.
• A fixed-protocol amortized MPS estimator: a network mapping one fixed informative

with a dropout-ensemble predictive distribution recalibrated to distribution-free marginal

To our knowledge this is the first amortized
measurement-conditioned full-state MPS estimator for QST (vs. per-state fits (Torlai et al.,

encoder architecture. We also do not call the dropout ensemble a Bayesian posterior.

RELATED WORK
We position against the nearest neighbors along each axis; the point is that each pairwise combina-
tion is occupied but the intersection is not.
Structural / MPS tomography.
MPS-QST (Cramer et al., 2010; Lanyon et al., 2017) and tensor-
train compressed-sensing QST (Sofi et al., 2025) recover a state by fitting cores to data under a hand-
designed low-rank/sparsity prior. Torlai et al. (2023) learn a tensor-network channel by gradient
descent to fit measurement statistics. In all of these the cores are the optimization variables for a
single unknown state; none learns a transferable generative distribution over cores. Our prior is
exactly such a distribution, and it is what makes the few-measurement regime tractable.
Generative / neural QST.
Neural QST fits a generative model (RBM, autoregressive, transformer)
to the measurements of one target state (Torlai et al., 2018; Carrasquilla et al., 2019; Cha et al., 2022);
QST-CGAN (Ahmed et al., 2021) amortizes a GAN across similar states but returns a point estimate.
Diffusion has been used to generate valid density matrices (Zhu et al., 2024), not to reconstruct from
measurements. Closest in spirit, QuaDiM (Tang et al., 2025) is a conditional diffusion model in the
quantum-measurement space, but it conditions on Hamiltonian parameters (not on measurements
of an unknown state) and generates measurement snapshots for property estimation rather than in-
verting given measurements to reconstruct a state, and it uses no tensor-network representation. We
differ by learning an amortized conditional estimator of MPS cores directly from a fixed informative
Pauli protocol on an unknown state, with distribution-free calibrated observable intervals.
Amortized / learned-prior Bayesian QST.
Closest to our motivation, Lohani et al. (2023) learn
(with a shallow CNN) the hyperparameters of a Bayesian prior; and Cha et al. (2025) amortize a
set-transformer estimate of the Bayesian posterior mean of state properties from classical shadows.
Both are point estimators (of properties or of prior hyperparameters); neither learns an amortized
conditional full-state estimator over tensor-network cores nor reports calibrated, distribution-free

Generative priors for inverse problems (Approach A).
A large body of work solves inverse

prior learnable) and the integrity controls that reveal when the prior, rather than the measurements,
is doing the work.
Uncertainty in QST.
Rigorous frequentist confidence regions (Christandl & Renner, 2012; Faist
& Renner, 2016) and, recently, anytime-valid confidence sequences (Cumitini, 2025) set the calibra-
tion bar but use no learned prior. Bayesian credible-region methods (Blume-Kohout, 2010; Granade
et al., 2016; Oh et al., 2019) use generic priors. The size-growing advantage of structured priors is
known for point estimation (Cramer et al., 2010); our contribution is to realize and quantify it in a
learned, calibrated, core-parameterized setting.

Two approaches, one parameterization.
On this shared core parameterization we study two
ways to turn measurements into a reconstruction, unified by the integrity controls of Sec. 6. Ap-

The measurement design is the lever: informative local Paulis.
Conditioning is necessary, but
the design D decides whether it helps. Random Pauli strings are a poor design for low-entanglement
states: a random weight-w Pauli has expectation exponentially small in w, so it carries little infor-
mation and the conditional model barely beats prior-only. The informative observables are local.
The precise statement is that local reduced density matrices on windows of ℓ= 2⌈log2 χ⌉+1 sites
determine a normal χ-MPS (Cramer et al., 2010) (Prop. 3)—for χ=2, tomographically complete
Paulis on each contiguous 3-site window. This theory-complete design (local3) is one option
we test. Our default D is a cheaper subset—all weight-1 Paulis {Xi, Yi, Zi} and weight-2 nearest-
neighbor Paulis {PiPi+1} (O(n))—which is not proven complete but is empirically sufficient on
our families (Sec. 6); the theory-complete window design does slightly better. Either way, the point
is that locality is the lever: switching from random to local Paulis is what turns a modest estimator
into a high-fidelity one.
Predictive uncertainty and conformal calibration.
The estimator gω is a point map; to attach
uncertainty we use it as a dropout ensemble: with dropout active at test time, E stochastic passes give
predictions {|ψ(e)⟩} and, for each observable O, an ensemble mean µO and scale σO. We stress this
is a heuristic predictive spread, not a validated Bayesian posterior. The intervals [µO ±σO] are over-
confident, so we recalibrate conformally: on a held-out split we form scores r = |O −µO|/σO and
take the ⌈(1 −α)(m+1)⌉-th smallest, Q, giving IO = [µO ± QσO] with distribution-free marginal
coverage ≥1 −α for that fixed observable, under exchangeability of calibration and test states
(Prop. 5). This is marginal per-observable coverage—not simultaneous coverage over observables,
a full-state confidence region, or coverage under distribution shift—and the same recalibration could
be applied to any point predictor, including the prior-only baseline; we report it for the conditional
estimator because it is the one with informative intervals.

THEORETICAL ANALYSIS
We give results that make the design choices precise: the physical readout and training objective
are gauge-invariant, but the core coordinatization—and hence the learned prior over cores—is not
(Prop. 1, Prop. 2); local reduced density matrices give a constructive O(n) informative measure-
ment design (Prop. 3), which is the design our conditional model uses; the degrees of freedom of a
manifold-restricted family separate exponentially from unstructured tomography (Prop. 4); and the
recalibrated observable intervals carry a distribution-free finite-sample coverage guarantee (Prop. 5).

Setup.
We evaluate on two n=6, χ=2 families. The generic random-MPS family draws fixed
random base cores plus i.i.d. complex-Gaussian perturbations (σ=0.35), canonicalized; it is moder-
ately diverse (nearest-training-neighbour fidelity ≈0.82). The physical shallow-circuit family is an
Ry(θ1)–CNOT-ladder–Ry(θ2) circuit with random angles (θ1 ∼π
2 + 0.6N, θ2 ∼0.6N), which is
exactly a χ=2 MPS and, at this spread, genuinely diverse (medoid fidelity to test states only 0.37).
Measurements are noisy Pauli expectations oj = ⟨Pj⟩+εj with shot noise of variance (1−⟨Pj⟩2)/S,
S=200. Unless stated we use the informative local design D (all single-qubit Xi, Yi, Zi and nearest-
neighbour PiPi+1; 63 observables at n=6). The conditional set-encoder is trained once per family
(2×104/6×103 states, 30k AdamW steps); all fidelities are on 120–200 held-out states. Code and
hyperparameters are in App. B.
Integrity controls: beat prior-only, and use the measurements.
We first run the two controls
that, we argue, any learned-QST method should report. Prior-only (k=0): predict each test state by
a fixed central estimate that uses no measurements—the family medoid (the bank state maximizing
mean fidelity to the family) or the normalized mean bank state. Shuffled: reconstruct from a different
state’s measurements (a random permutation of the test batch); if this matches the true-measurement
reconstruction, the measurements are unused and the method is doing nearest-neighbour prediction,
not tomography. Fig. 3 shows why these matter. On a concentrated family—disordered paramag-
netic TFIM ground states, all close to |+⟩⊗n—the medoid already reaches fidelity 0.997 with zero
measurements, and true and shuffled reconstructions coincide to ±0.01 at every budget: there is
nothing to reconstruct, and a naive “high fidelity at few measurements” is entirely the prior. On a
genuinely diverse family the medoid is far from optimal (0.65), true-measurement reconstruction
clearly separates from shuffled, and the separation grows with the budget—the signature of real
measurement information. We therefore report on diverse families, where passing the controls is
meaningful.

Head-to-head with classical local reconstruction, and what the learned method actually buys.
The most informative baseline is the classical one our design is motivated by: a prior-free MPS
maximum-likelihood fit to the same local Pauli values (a stand-in for Cramer-style local recon-
struction), run per state with random restarts. We compare it to our amortized estimator across
the design ladder (Table 4, shallow family).
Two honest conclusions follow.
First, the mea-
surement design is the dominant lever for both methods: classical MPS-MLE climbs from 0.633
(local1, under-determined) to 0.953 (local) to 0.989 (local3, theory-complete), mirroring
the learned estimator—so the informative local design, not the learned prior per se, is what makes
few-measurement reconstruction possible. Second, the learned prior’s advantage is confined to the
under-determined regime: at local1 it edges out MLE (0.666 vs. 0.633), at local it is a marginal
+0.014, and at the theory-complete local3 classical MLE is actually better (0.989 vs. 0.957)—
with complete data the prior is unnecessary and our fixed-capacity network is the bottleneck. We
therefore do not claim the learned estimator dominates classical reconstruction on fidelity. What
it buys is amortization—a single forward pass instead of a per-state nonconvex optimization—and
calibrated predictive uncertainty (including for unmeasured observables), neither of which the per-
state MLE provides. The earlier near-zero MLE numbers (Table 7) are for random Pauli designs; on
the informative-local design MLE is strong, which is the same “design is the lever” point.

LIMITATIONS AND ONGOING WORK
We are explicit about scope. (a) Fixed measurement design. The estimator is trained for one infor-
mative measurement protocol; an amortized design-agnostic variant (trained over variable random
Pauli subsets) collapses to the family mean for both a DeepSets and a set-transformer encoder (§6).
Amortizing over arbitrary designs—or over a parameterized family of designs—is the main open
direction. (b) Family/regime dependence. On concentrated families a prior-only estimate is already

cleaner route. (d) Hardware scope. The closed-loop hardware run (§6, Table 6) covers 5 states of

CONCLUSION
We introduced a fixed-protocol amortized MPS estimator for quantum state tomography: an en-
coder over the measured values {(Pj, oj)} of one fixed informative protocol (a plain MLP or a
set-encoder—the architecture is not the source of performance) that outputs the cores of a matrix-
product state, trained once with a gauge-invariant loss and equipped with conformally calibrated
observable intervals. A k=0 prior-only baseline and a shuffled-measurement control—which most
learned-QST evaluations omit, and which deflate the naive “prior+inversion” recipe—become the
paper’s integrity backbone, and our method passes both. We reach the same core parameterization
by two complementary routes: a generative flow prior with measurement-guided posterior infer-
ence (Approach A), validated against a gold-standard SIR posterior and robust out-of-distribution,
but whose point accuracy on concentrated families is inherited from the prior; and the amortized
measurement-conditioned estimator (Approach B), whose decisive design choice is to condition on
an informative local Pauli set—motivated by the fact that local reduced density matrices determine
a χ-MPS. Approach B turns a modest, memorization-prone estimator into one that reconstructs at
fidelity ≈0.95, beats prior-only by up to +0.59, and yields sharp ≈90%-coverage intervals, with a
polynomial-cost implementation that runs to 20 qubits. A natural hybrid—Approach A’s validated
posterior for uncertainty, Approach B’s estimate for the point, under the same cores—is left open.
The honest scope—a fixed informative design, diverse families—delimits a concrete and, we think,
useful setting for learned, calibrated, measurement-efficient tomography, and the two integrity con-
trols are what make “measurement-efficient” a claim rather than an assumption.

EXTENDED THEORETICAL DISCUSSION
Why the experimental local design suffices in practice (relation to Prop. 3).
Prop. 3 states
the sufficient condition of Cramer et al. (2010): an injective χ-MPS is uniquely determined among all
states by the collection of its reduced density matrices on every contiguous 3-site window, i.e. by the
expectation values of all Paulis supported on such windows (the local3 design, 207 observables at
22


## Page 23


n=6). Our default experimental design local (single-qubit plus nearest-neighbor weight-2 Paulis,
63 at n=6) is a strict subset of local3: it omits the weight-3 terms and the non-adjacent weight-2
terms inside each window. It is therefore not guaranteed to be information-complete for arbitrary
χ-MPS. The reason it nonetheless nearly matches local3 empirically (Table 3: 0.954/0.967 vs.
0.950/0.957) is that the learned prior supplies the information that the missing higher-weight win-
dows would otherwise carry: within a low-entanglement family, the two-body correlations plus the
prior already pin down the state, so the marginal value of the weight-3 terms is small. This is pre-
cisely the division of labour our integrity controls quantify—and it is why we describe local as
informative rather than information-complete, and report local3 as the theory-faithful design in
the ablation rather than as the headline. A design that is provably complete and minimal for a given
learned prior is an open problem we do not claim to solve.
Gauge coordinate-dependence, made precise (elaborating Prop. 2).
Let Φ : M →Rd send a
physical state to the standardized real/imaginary parts of its left-canonical cores. Canonicalization
fixes the bond gauge up to a residual group G of per-bond diagonal unitaries (phases) and, at de-
generacies of the canonical spectrum, per-bond block rotations; our sign/phase convention selects
a section of G almost everywhere but not globally continuously. Consequently Φ has coordinate
discontinuities on a measure-zero set (spectral degeneracies and sign crossings), and Euclidean dis-
tances ∥Φ(ψ) −Φ(ϕ)∥are not gauge-invariant near that set: two states with fidelity 1−ϵ can have
core-coordinate distance that does not vanish with ϵ. We therefore do not claim the map is an isom-
etry or that the core-space prior is canonical; we use the coordinate representation only as a training
target, and every reported metric—the loss equation 3, all fidelities, and the SIR comparison—is
computed as a gauge-invariant state functional. Prop. 2 should be read as this cautionary statement
(coordinates are convenient but gauge-dependent), not as a claim of collapse; we have softened the
main-text wording accordingly.
Scope of the theory.
Neither proposition is a sample-complexity or measurement-efficiency the-
orem, and we do not present one: our efficiency claims are entirely empirical and are stated only
relative to the prior-only and shuffled controls. The theory serves two limited purposes—to justify
which local Paulis to measure (Prop. 3) and to be honest about what the core coordinates are and
are not (Prop. 2).
