ABSTRACT
Context. Outflows in active galactic nuclei (AGN) are recognized as a fundamental mechanism driving the co-evolution of supermas-
sive black holes (SMBHs) and their host galaxies by regulating the gas reservoir for the SMBH growth and the host star formation.
Although powerful outflows are frequently detected in gas-rich, active star-forming galaxies, their existence and potential impact
within gas-poor, quiescent galaxies remain poorly understood.
Aims. We report the first detection of a powerful ultra-fast outflow (UFO) in a nearby quiescent galaxy KUG 1208+386, providing a
multiscale analysis of AGN winds from nuclear to galactic scales.
Methods. We performed a Bayesian-based X-ray spectroscopy of archival XMM-Newton and NuSTAR observations to characterize
the circumnuclear materials and UFOs. Using optical spectra from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), we constrained
the kinematics of the galactic-scale outflows and the star formation history (SFH) of the host galaxy. Additionally, we derived the
galaxy properties, such as stellar mass (M⋆) and star formation rate (SFR), via multi-wavelength spectral energy distribution (SED)
fitting of photometric data.
Results. We detect a nuclear X-ray UFO with a velocity of vout ≈−0.07c and a kinetic power of ˙EUFO = (0.8–6.5) × 1043 erg/s.
This power is sufficient to drive effective AGN feedback ( ˙EUFO/LEdd = (1–8)%) and far exceeds the galactic [OIII] outflow power
∼1040 erg/s. Host galaxy analysis reveals a massive quiescent system (specific star formation rate ∼3 × 10−12 yr−1) that has been
quenched ∼9 Gyr ago. The central AGN is obscured by a line-of-sight (LOS) column density of log(NLOS
H
/cm−2) ∼23 and the
circumnuclear scattering material is Compton-thick log(Nscatter
H

−0.5, indicating a dense nuclear environment.
Conclusions. The discovery of a nuclear UFO in a long-quenched massive galaxy challenges the paradigm that UFOs are exclusive
to gas-rich, star-forming systems, suggesting instead that they are governed by the local circumnuclear environments, rather than the
global gas reservoir. Our results indicate that episodic, powerful winds can maintain the quiescent state of KUG 1208+386 over Gyr
timescales, supporting a wind-driven ‘maintenance’ mode of AGN feedback that is distinct from the classical jet mode. Future deep
X-ray and sub-mm observations are essential to definitively characterize the cold gas reservoir and the coupling efficiency among
multi-phase and multi-scale outflows.

1. Introduction
Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) are widely considered to play
a pivotal role in shaping the evolution of their host galax-
ies. The observed correlations between the mass of su-
permassive black holes (SMBHs) and host galaxy proper-
ties, such as the MBH–σ⋆relation (σ⋆is the stellar dis-
persion velocity), suggest a co-evolutionary process regulated
by AGN feedback (e.g., Kormendy & Ho 2013; Harrison et al.
2018; Harrison & Ramos Almeida 2024). This feedback typi-
cally manifests through outflows that can either expel/heat or
compress the interstellar medium (ISM), thus suppressing or en-

hancing the star formation of the host galaxy (e.g., Fabian 2012;
Zubovas & King 2014; Maiolino et al. 2017).
Among various manifestations of feedback, ultra-fast out-
flows (UFOs) are the most extreme AGN winds. Characterized
by mildly relativistic velocities (v ≳10000 km/s) and typically
detected via strongly blueshifted absorption lines in X-ray spec-
tra (e.g., Tombesi et al. 2010), UFOs are thought to be launched
from the innermost regions of the accretion disk. These winds
are likely accelerated by radiation pressure (Proga et al. 2000;
Nomura et al. 2016; Matzeu et al. 2017; Xu et al. 2021b, 2023)
or magnetic fields (Fukumura et al. 2010, 2015; Gu et al. 2025;
Reeves et al. 2026b), with recent high-resolution insights from
missions like XRISM suggesting a potential hybrid mechanism

Gofford et al. 2013; Chartas et al. 2021; Matzeu et al. 2023),
classified as Seyfert galaxies or quasars depending on the AGN
contribution to the galaxy luminosity. Most host galaxies of these
AGN are gas-rich galaxies with non-negligible star formation ac-
tivity, i.e., star-forming (SF) or green-valley (GV) galaxies, char-
acterized by the specific star formation rate sSFR ≡SFR/M⋆≥
10−11 yr−1, where SFR is the star formation rate and M⋆is
the galaxy stellar mass (e.g., Veilleux et al. 2013; Tombesi et al.
2015; Koss et al. 2021; Travascio et al. 2024). The abundant gas
reservoirs can fuel the AGN accretion (λEdd ≡Lbol/LEdd ≳0.01,
LBol: bolometric luminosity) necessary to launch winds.
However, the presence and role of powerful AGN winds
in quiescent galaxies remain poorly understood. Most massive
galaxies in the local Universe reside on the ‘red sequence’, char-
acterized by old stellar populations and negligible star forma-
tion (sSFR < 10−11 yr−1; Faber et al. 2007). The recent discov-
ery of an unexpected abundance of massive quiescent systems
at z > 3 by JWST (e.g., Carnall et al. 2023a,b) further empha-
sizes the need for a continuous heating source to prevent halo gas
from cooling and re-igniting star formation over Gyr timescales.
Current models typically invoke ‘radio-mode’ feedback, where
mechanical energy from collimated jets in low-luminosity AGN
(LLAGNs, λEdd < 0.01; Yuan & Narayan 2014) balances cool-
ing in the ISM (Fabian 2012). Nonetheless, it remains an open
question whether wide-angle winds can provide an alternative
mechanism to jets for sustaining quiescence and regulating the
MBH–σ⋆relation long after galaxy formation.
Besides nuclear and short-lived X-ray UFOs, broad (i.e.,
exceeding the galaxy dynamics) [OIII] λ5008Å emission has
become one of the most widely used diagnostics of galactic-
scale outflows, associated with Myr-timescale AGN activity
(e.g., Harrison et al. 2014). As a forbidden transition, the [OIII]
λ5008Å emission line is suppressed in the high-density sub-
pc scales of the AGN broad-line region (BLR) and thus serves
as a clean tracer of gas on pc to kpc scales in the narrow-
line region (NLR) (e.g., Boroson 2005; Greene & Ho 2005a;
Mullaney et al. 2013). While the bulk of the outflowing mass
often resides in the molecular phase, [OIII] offers unparalleled
advantages in tracking the kinematics and energetics of kpc-
scale outflows due to its high luminosity in optical spectra (e.g.,
Fischer et al. 2018).
Nevertheless, establishing a direct observational link be-
tween nuclear winds and galactic outflows remains a chal-
lenge. The primary limitation is the detection of nuclear winds
(e.g., Veilleux et al. 2013), requiring high signal-to-noise (SNR)
ultraviolet (UV) / X-ray spectroscopy from space-based ob-
servatories. The UFO detection rate and its transient na-
ture (e.g., Reeves et al. 2023; Gu et al. 2025) further compli-
cate the detection. Consequently, by far only a small number
of systems (∼10) have been confirmed to host UFOs and
kpc-scale winds simultaneously. Studies of these rare sources
have revealed complex outflow behaviors, from momentum-
conserving (IRAS F11119+3257, PG 1211+143, I Zw 1;
Tombesi et al. 2015; Pounds & King 2013; Reeves & Braito
2019) to energy-conserving winds (Mrk 231, IRAS 17020+4544
Feruglio et al. 2015; Longinotti et al. 2018), with several ex-
ceptions deviating from both modes (APM 08279+5255, PDS
456, MCG-03-58-007; Feruglio et al. 2017; Braito et al. 2018;
Sirressi et al. 2019; Bischetti et al. 2019; Travascio et al. 2024;
XRISM Collaboration et al. 2025), highlighting the need for
more case studies.

3.2. Model B
The Fe-K emission line implies the reprocessing of X-ray pho-
tons emitted from the hot corona off colder materials (e.g.,
Fabian et al. 1989; Ross & Fabian 2005). Due to its narrow line
width, σ < 0.14 keV (derived from fitting a Gaussian line), it
is likely that the Fe-K emission line originates from the distant
reflection from the SMBH, rather than from the inner accretion
disk (e.g., Mushotzky et al. 1995; Fabian et al. 2009). Therefore,
we included a physical torus model borus12 (Balokovi´c et al.
2018, 2019) into Model A for the X-ray reflection, named as
Model B. The borus12 model calculates the transmitted and
scattered X-ray photons in the cold, neutral, and uniformly dis-
tributed gas with a toroidal geometry. The photon index and
the electron gas temperature were linked to those of the hot
Comptonization. The angle of inclination of the torus is fixed
at θ = 60◦(i.e., cosθ = 0.5) due to its poor constraint. The scat-
tering column density (NTor
H ) and the covering factor (CF) of the
torus were left free.
The inclusion of a torus model significantly improves the
fitting statistics by ∆C −stat = 78 with 3 degrees of freedom
(d.o.f.). Both ∆AICc = −72 and ∆logZ = 32.5 strongly favor
the presence of a distant reprocessing component. The scattering
column density, log(NTor
H /cm−2) = 24.4+0.8
−0.2, indicates an intrin-
sic Compton-thick torus. The covering factor is CF = 0.54+0.11
−0.08,
broadly consistent with typical values found for AGN tori (e.g.,
Marchesi et al. 2019; Zhao et al. 2021). However, we caution
that the posterior distribution (see Fig.A.3 for the reference)
shows that the covering fraction CF is not constrained at the 3σ
level, while NTor
H
remains in the Compton-thick regime over the
same confidence range. Therefore, while the detailed torus ge-
ometry cannot be determined with the present data, the fit sup-
ports the presence of dense distant reprocessing material.
The alternative distant reflection model from the accretion
disk (xillverCp; García & Kallman 2010; García et al. 2013)
was also tested, yielding a statistically indistinguishable fit. We,
therefore, keep the torus interpretation.

4. UV-optical-MIR Data Analysis
In this section, we present a comprehensive analysis of optical
DESI spectra and multi-wavelength photometric data to charac-
terize galactic-scale outflows and host properties of KUG 1208.
4.1. Optical Line Fitting
DESI spectra (3600–7500Å) were modeled using PyQSOFit
(Guo et al. 2018; Shen et al. 2019). The code performs the fit
with χ2 statistics in the rest frame after automatically correcting
for the Galactic extinction using the Schlegel et al. (1998) dust
map. The spectrum is modeled as a superposition of an AGN
power-law, Fe II emission, various Gaussian emission lines,
and a host stellar component fitted with simple stellar popula-
tion (SSP) templates from the E-MILES library (Vazdekis et al.
2016). Parameter uncertainties are estimated via Markov chain
Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling.
For the line fittings, the velocity shifts and dispersions of all
narrow lines (e.g., [OIII] λλ5008, 4960Å, [NII] λλ6585, 6550Å,
and [SII]λλ 6718, 6733Å, and the narrow cores of Balmer lines)
were tied, assuming they originate from NLR with similar kine-
matics. Coronal lines (e.g. [FeVII] λ3760, λ5721, λ6087Å) were
also linked with the narrow lines to ensure the fitting stability
given their limited SNR. For the Balmer series, broad compo-
nents of Hα and Hβ were modeled using three sets of Gaussians,
with their dispersions and shifts tied between two lines for phys-
ical consistency. Additionally, an outflow component with free
kinematics was included for the [OIII] doublet to probe galactic-
scale outflows. Following theoretical quantum ratios, the flux ra-
tios of [OIII] λ5008/λ4960Å and [NIII] λ6585/λ6550Å were
fixed at 2.95:1, and 3.06:1, respectively.
The PyQSOFit decomposition results for the AGN and
offset-AGN spectra are presented in Fig.C.1 and C.2, respec-
tively, along with zoom-in plots of the Hα and Hβ regions. The
peak wavelengths, velocity dispersions, and Galactic-extinction-
corrected fluxes for the lines of interest are summarized in
Tab.C.1. Generally, moving from the on-AGN to the offset-AGN
pointing, both the line fluxes and the AGN continuum contribu-
tion decrease, and coronal lines are no longer detectable. In both
spectra, the Hα line is well modeled by one narrow, two interme-
diate, and one broad component, which are classified based on
their velocity dispersions (σ < 300 km/s, 300−2000 km/s and >
2000 km/s, respectively). Although Balmer lines can sometimes
trace galactic outflows (e.g., Travascio et al. 2024), the non-
narrow Balmer components in KUG 1208 may not serve this
purpose. Their dispersions remain nearly constant across both
pointings (a separation of ∼1.2 kpc), indicating that these broad
and intermediate components originate from the PSF spillover
of BLR emission, rather than a kpc-scale outflow.
Including an [OIII] outflow component significantly im-
proves the fit for both spectra (∆χ2/d.o.f. = −3075/3 and
−229/3 for the AGN and offset-AGN spectra, respectively), con-
firming the presence of [OIII] outflows (e.g. Liu et al. 2013). The
velocity dispersion of this component decreases from σ[OIII];O =
240 ± 4 km/s at the core to σ[OIII];O = 187 ± 11 km/s at the off-
set location, supporting a physical origin for the kpc-scale [OIII]
outflows, rather than an instrumental artifact.
4.1.1. Host Extinction
We utilized the narrow-line Balmer decrement to estimate the
dust extinction on the gas in the host galaxy. Assuming the in-
trinsic Hα/Hβ ratio of 2.86, corresponding to Case B recombi-
nation for the NLR (characterized by a temperature of T = 104K
and an electron density of ne = 102 cm−3; Osterbrock & Ferland
2006), we derived the color excess of E(B −V)gas = 0.23 ± 0.02
using the (Cardelli et al. 1989) extinction law with RV = 3.1. A
consistent value of E(B −V)gas = 0.24 ± 0.12 was also obtained
from the offset-AGN spectrum, albeit with larger uncertainties.
However, we caution that using the Balmer decrement as a proxy
for gas extinction entails intrinsic systematic uncertainties, par-
ticularly given the potential for complex dynamical or geometric
variations of the BLR (e.g., see Fig.1 in Li et al. 2024).

4.2. SED Fitting with CIGALE
We measured the galaxy properties (e.g. stellar mass and SFR)
through the Code Investigating GALaxy Emission (CIGALE
v2025.1; Boquien et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2020, 2022), which is
designed to use a library of models to fit the SED of galaxies con-
sidering the energy balance conservation. Our input data com-
prised the FUV-optical-MIR photometries detailed in Sec.2.5
and the hard X-ray flux (2–10 keV) from the X-ray spectroscopy.
We adopted a delayed star formation history (SFH) and SSP
models from Bruzual & Charlot (2003) assuming an initial mass
function from Chabrier (2003). We incorporated the standard
nebular emission model of Inoue (2011) and the dust attenua-
tion model based on the modified Calzetti et al. (2000) starburst
attenuation curve. The reprocessed dust emission was charac-
terized by the Draine et al. (2014) model. We assumed solar
metallicity through the fitting. The AGN contribution was ex-
plained by the Fritz et al. (2006) models, while the X-ray emis-
sion was self-consistently integrated using the module developed
by Yang et al. (2020), which connects the X-ray luminosity to
the AGN UV/optical emission.
The best-fit broadband SED and the corresponding residu-
als are shown on the left panel of Fig.D.1. CIGALE yields a
satisfactory fit with a reduced χ2 of 3.3. The primary residuals
manifest as an excess in the WISE2 (4.6µm) band, a discrepancy
likely stemming from the complex MIR emission characteristic
of passive galaxies. This excess can be attributed to contamina-
tion from circumstellar dust associated with evolved stars (e.g.,
asymptotic giant branch stars). The galaxy stellar mass and SFR
are well-constrained at M⋆= (3.1 ± 0.6) × 1010 M⊙and SFR =
0.09±0.05 M⊙yr−1, respectively. While the absence of FIR/sub-
mm data may lead to an overestimation of SFRs, typically by a
factor of two (Kim et al. 2022), this potential bias does not alter
our conclusions. These parameters imply an exceptionally low
specific SFR of sSFR ≡SFR/M⋆≈3 × 10−12 yr−1, categorizing
KUG 1208 as a massive, quenched galaxy residing on the red
sequence (e.g., Weinmann et al. 2006; Schiminovich et al. 2007;
Salim et al. 2007).
Furthermore, the inferred stellar attenuation, E(B −V)star ∼
0.3, is comparable to the gas extinction derived from the Balmer
decrement. This similarity suggests a diffuse and relatively uni-
form dust distribution across the galaxy, a hallmark of quiescent
galaxies (e.g., Wild et al. 2011; Price et al. 2014). In such sys-
tems, dense clouds associated with active star formation have
largely dissipated, leaving both stars and ionized gas to be atten-
uated by the same diffuse ISM.

6.2. Impacts of Ionized Outflows
Results from both SFH analysis and SED modeling (Sec.4.3 and
4.2) identify KUG 1208 as a quiescent galaxy. Although the ori-

the present data alone, AGN feedback is a plausible mechanism
for suppressing the star formation (e.g., Fabian 2012). We there-
fore examine the energetic feasibility of whether AGN-driven
winds can affect the galactic gas reservoir by either expelling or
heating the gas.

that UFOs are predominantly hosted by gas-rich galaxies
(Schinnerer et al. 1998; Pounds et al. 2003; Riechers et al. 2009;

nuclear environment. This is supported by the observed high
column densities of both scattering and LOS host materials
(log(NH/cm−2) ≳23; Sect.3.2), which indicates a localized
reservoir capable of fueling the central SMBH.
Integrating these findings into the broader framework of
galaxy evolution (Heckman & Best 2014; Fischer et al. 2018),
AGN feedback in KUG 1208 likely operates in a ‘maintenance’
mode, dominated by AGN winds instead of radio jets (e.g.,
Fabian 2012; Gaspari et al. 2013; Heckman & Best 2014). Al-
though global star formation ceased ∼9 Gyr ago, the presence
of an AGN indicates that the nucleus is not entirely dormant
but rather undergoes intermittent re-triggering. Instead of driv-
ing large-scale gas ejection, AGN winds likely sustain the quies-
cence by injecting mechanical energy into the gas reservoir via
large-scale turbulence. This scenario is further supported by our
finding that the bulk of the [OIII] outflow remains gravitation-
ally bound, implying that the wind energy is dissipated within
the host galaxy. Such wind-driven turbulence can effectively pre-
vent gas from cooling and collapsing, thereby inhibiting star for-
mation. This ‘maintenance’ scenario provides a self-consistent
explanation for the coexistence of powerful feedback and dense
nuclear gas in a galaxy that has remained dormant since the early
universe.
7. Conclusions
We present a multi-wavelength study of the Seyfert 1.2 galaxy
KUG 1208+386, combining X-ray (XMM-Newton+NuSTAR)
and optical (DESI) data to probe the coupling between nuclear
and galactic outflows. Our findings are summarized as follows:
– First UFO detection in a quiescent host: We report the
first detection of a highly ionized UFO (vUFO ∼0.07c) in
a massive galaxy that has been quenched for ∼9 Gyr. This
discovery challenges the convention that UFOs are exclusive
to gas-rich, star-forming systems.
– Circumnuclear triggering: The high column densities
(log(NH/cm−2) ≳23) from X-ray spectroscopy reveal a
dense, localized gas reservoir fueling the SMBH. This im-
plies that UFO triggering is decoupled from global host prop-
erties and is instead governed by the nuclear environment.
– A possible momentum-conserving outflow: The [OIII] out-
flow extends to ≳1.2 kpc despite its modest energetics, in
contrast to the powerful nuclear UFO. Comparing the UFO,
ionized [OIII] outflow, and inferred cold molecular gas indi-
cates a momentum-conserving outflow in KUG 1208.
– Wind-driven maintenance mode of AGN feedback: The
coexistence of a powerful UFO and a local dense reservoir in
a quenched host suggests a ‘maintenance’ feedback mode in
KUG 1208. Intermittent wind-driven turbulence, rather than
radio jets, suffices to suppress gas cooling and sustain quies-
cence without requiring bulk gas expulsion.
Future deep high-resolution X-ray (XMM-Newton and
XRISM) and sub-mm observations will be crucial to constrain
the multi-phase outflow coupling and provide a definitive vali-
dation of this wind-driven maintenance-mode scenario.

To estimate the line significance, we performed two Gaussian line search methods on model A. Briefly, the first method is a
sliding Gaussian line scan applied directly to the observed spectrum. We added a Gaussian component to model A and stepped its
centroid energy through the 2–10 keV energy band. For each trial, both the line energy and width were fixed, while the normalization
was allowed to vary. The improved statistics were recorded at each trial energy bin and width. The square root of the ∆C-stat
(
√
∆C–stat) can serve as a rough proxy for the line significance (Cash 1979).
This method is efficient but only provides the single-trial significance without considering the look-elsewhere (LE) effect, where
the significant signals without known energies may originate from the noise. The second method is the cross-correlation-based MC
Gaussian line search accounting for the LE effect (e.g., Kosec et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2022, 2024). Classically, one would simulate
spectra from the best-fitting continuum model, repeat the Gaussian line scan on each simulated spectrum, and record the strongest
10
spurious feature found anywhere in the searched band. The global significance of the observed feature is then obtained by comparing
its strength with the distribution of these maximum noise-induced features. The difference between our approach and the classical
MC method (e.g., Tombesi et al. 2010) is that instead of performing the spectral fitting with C-stat (or χ2-stat) , this method adopts
the cross-correlation (CC) between the Gaussian model spectra and residual spectra against the continuum model, which speeds up
the computational efficiency by a factor of 104. The true significance is derived from the comparison between the observed line and
any significant features over the entire simulated spectra, not limited to the same energy bin, in case of random shifts of UFOs.
For both methods, we tried various Gaussian line widths from 100–2000 km/s and took a step of 0.01 keV among the 2–10 keV
band. 10000 MC simulations were performed in the second approach, corresponding to the upper limit of 3.9σ. Results are shown
in Fig.A.2, where in both methods, Fe-K emission is robustly detected at > 3.9σ, and an absorption line is located at 7.25+0.07
−0.07 keV
with a significance of ≳3σ. The absorption feature below 6 keV is present only in the EPIC-pn spectrum and is therefore attributed
20
to an instrumental or statistical artifact.
