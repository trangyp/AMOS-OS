Failure Privacy and Safe Collective Expression
By Matthew Cashman∗
Widely held views can go unspoken when speaking out alone invites retalia-
tion. I recast such silence as a problem of safe coalition formation. When
safety comes in numbers, there is a largest group that could speak safely. Open
organizing must stay safe every step the way, making for a cascade of speak-
ing up that stops short of that largest group. Social assurance contracts need
safety only at the destination, and get there by keeping commitments private
until enough people have joined: a tunnel to the largest safe group.
If no
member may ever regret joining, such failure privacy is necessary—not merely
sufficient. (JEL D71, D82, D83, J51)
I.
Introduction
Many people hold views they are not willing to share publicly—if they have to do it alone.
They may be outside the Overton window, strongly opposed by an employer, a regulator,
a regime, or another powerful audience, or attached to allegations that invite retaliation
against whoever speaks alone. The worry is not only that the speaker may be wrong about
how many people agree with her, it is that being publicly identified as a supporter, signer,
complainant, witness, dissident, or accuser is dangerous unless enough others are identified
at the same time. However, a crowd can dilute a reprisal that would be concentrated on a
lone first mover, and a big enough crowd can even deter retaliation in the first place.
This paper studies that problem as one of safe coalition formation. Many statements that
would command wide private support are never made. Employees who would all sign a
complaint stay silent; citizens who privately favor an unpopular position do not express it;
victims who would each come forward if others did never learn that the others exist. The
classical explanations turn on information: people misperceive how many agree with them
(Kuran, 1997; Noelle-Neumann, 1974), and a device that corrected that misperception would

The exposure at issue is material retaliation: discharge, prosecution, blacklisting, harass-
ment, or other punishments that are borne by identifiable individuals and whose bite a larger
group can dilute or prevent. A person’s payoff from being named publicly as part of the
collective act depends on who else is named with her. A set of names is safe only if everyone
on the public roster is protected. Correcting beliefs about who supports what can change
which rosters are safe, and in favorable cases it can even make a lone first mover safe. What
information alone cannot do, holding fixed who is safe with whom, is assemble a named
coalition when the destination is safe but every smaller group that would have to appear
along the way is not: every road to the safe coalition passes through exposed ground. There
the binding constraint is the path by which names become visible, and a mechanism that
holds commitments privately until the whole coalition can be released relaxes exactly that
constraint. The case in which adding an ill-chosen name can raise other people’s exposure
violates safety in numbers, and is left for future work (Appendix E.1).
A simple example might be a hundred professionals who privately oppose a policy and
would each sign a public letter of dissent if enough colleagues signed it. An early visible
signer, however, risks a reprisal that a later signer, shielded by numbers, does not. If the
letter is circulated in the open, with each name appearing as it is added, the drive grows
only while people are willing to be the next name on a list still too short to protect them.
A campaign that needs a hundred names can die at five. If the same names are gathered
privately and the letter is published only after enough people have committed, no signatory
is ever exposed on an under-protective list. The preferences, risks, and potential supporters
are the same. What differs is whether commitments are visible as they accrue or held until
release.

I call a named coalition self-protecting if every member is safe given exactly the membership
that goes public. A member is safe when, given who else is public with her, being named does
her no net harm. When safety comes in numbers, so that additional public co-participants
weakly help anyone already exposed, self-protecting coalitions combine cleanly: merging two
of them yields another. Two consequences carry the paper. There is a unique largest self-
protecting coalition, and, starting from a seed of committed early movers, a unique smallest
one. The central question is which of these coalitions a given institution can bring about.
The answer turns on a question of implementation: whether the institution must expose
people on the way to the final coalition, or can verify their participation privately before
anyone is exposed.
Cascade versus tunnel.
Decentralized expression and a disclosure mechanism face the same safety requirement,
which I call regret-free safety (in mechanism-design terms, robust ex post safety): no person’s
name is ever public, under any realization of others’ participation, in a coalition that fails to
protect her. This is one of three working axioms, along with safety in numbers (monotone
protection) and the inability to verify, before anyone is exposed, whether the others one
is counting on will also go public (co-movement). I state below which result uses which
axiom. The microfoundation is that exposure harms are individually borne, non-contractible,
and compensable only incompletely and after the fact, so safety must hold realization by
realization rather than only in expectation.
Between silence and safe collective expression stands a mountain: a middle range of coali-
tion sizes at which anyone visible is unsafe. The two institutions differ in how they face it.

Decentralized public expression cannot verify co-movement before exposure. When a person
goes public, she is exposed whether or not the others she was counting on actually follow.
She can therefore speak safely only when the coalition already public, plus her own name,
protects her. Public expression is a safe-expansion process: it advances one visible name at
a time and stalls where the next step turns unsafe, at the smallest self-protecting coalition
reachable from the seed. This is the “cascade”, and the mountain is where it ends.
A social assurance contract works differently. It gathers commitments privately, exposes
no one until release, and reveals names only as part of the coalition finally released, so it
must satisfy safety at the destination rather than along the way. It can therefore implement
the greatest self-protecting coalition: when the pass is blocked, it goes under the mountain.
This is the “tunnel” (Figure 1).
The obstacle also earns a working name, the exposure
barrier: the stretch of coalition sizes at which a public list is too short to protect the people
on it. Open expression is constrained by safe paths; the contract is constrained only by safe
destinations.
The regret-free frontier.
The mechanism’s contribution is the set of hidden supporters: the people the tunnel reaches
but the cascade cannot. One structural result characterizes what is needed to capture them.
We may call a process regret-free if no realization ever names someone in a coalition that
does not protect her. Any regret-free route past the cascade must, at the step that first
exposes a person unsafe relative to the already-public set, condition her exposure on the
verified, non-public commitments of co-participants released together with her. Otherwise,
the realization in which those co-participants fail to appear leaves her exposed.
This is a characterization of the regret-free frontier, not a general impossibility theorem.
Information, equilibrium trust, vanguards, and exposure lotteries can all push past the cas-
cade, but only by giving up regret-freeness in some realization. The theorem is best read as
a taxonomy. It pins down the form the private object must take: completed, verified, and
co-released. It also classifies what familiar institutions lack. Open polls and petitions are not
co-released; “I’ll go if you go” talk is not verified; posted bonds and exposure lotteries are
not safe in the failure realization; fixed-date reveals are not conditioned on completion. Its

When surfacing helps, and how it can backfire.
The party who would retaliate is itself strategic, and making it strategic yields the paper’s
sharpest prediction. A strategic opponent retaliates only when it can plausibly succeed. A
larger, better-protected public coalition is therefore more likely to be left alone. But a larger
coalition can also be a larger threat, which may provoke the opponent to mobilize more
force. Which effect dominates is governed by a clean threshold (Proposition 5). Surfacing
helps when the coalition’s resilience, how likely it is to survive retaliation if the opponent
fights, grows faster than the opponent’s ability to mobilize force against a larger threat.
It backfires when escalation dominates: when a bigger coalition provokes the opponent into

A measurement caution follows on the helping side. By surfacing the hidden supporters

What the model adds is the coalition-formation logic for when such a rise signals a more

safety axiom remains the conservative benchmark for the structural results.
When credibility binds.
The tunnel presumes a contract administrator that keeps commitments private until a
contract succeeds. In many applications this is not the hard part. The administrator may
be chosen by the coalition, such as an organizer or movement nonprofit, or may be a trustless
protocol with nothing to leak. The strategic actor that cannot be engineered away is then the
opponent, not the administrator. When the administrator is strategic, credibility becomes a
dial that moves reach between C−and C+; Appendix D sets out why selection of the keeper
and trustless implementation usually pin the dial at the top, and what remains open when
they do not.

The value of reach.
Whether moving that dial is good depends on whom it enfranchises. Failure privacy pro-
tects the participants most exposed to retaliation. Why should those also be the participants
who most harm the opponent? Because retaliation is not random: an opponent concentrates
punishment on the people who hurt it most—the witness whose testimony is most damning,
the organizer whose defection costs most—so needing the most cover and doing the most
damage are two faces of the same primitive, the harm a participant does to the opponent—at
least where exposure tracks the damage a speaker does rather than her own vulnerability.
Appendix E makes this an explicit assumption and traces what follows from it. Failure
privacy can be used for good or evil, and so is valuable when the voices it frees are socially
valuable and harmful where it is not. In harmful domains, a large gap between cascade and
tunnel destinations is a warning sign rather than a success metric. The same primitive that
sets a participant’s exposure also sets the opponent’s stake in Section III, linking this welfare
reading to the provocation threshold there (Proposition 5). I treat this welfare accounting
as an illustrative extension (Appendix E), not as a general welfare theory of expression.
Why it matters.
Silence for fear of punishment is pervasive. It shapes whether unions form, whether mis-
conduct surfaces, whether dissent appears under repression, and whether professionals voice
unpopular views, views that management may even be better off for hearing. The remedy
the classical accounts point to, correcting beliefs about how many others privately agree (Ku-
ran, 1997; Bursztyn, Gonz´alez and Yanagizawa-Drott, 2020), addresses only one obstacle.
Correcting people’s beliefs about how many others privately agree cannot by itself make iso-
lated expression safe when the danger is being named in too small a coalition (Corollary 4).
Only a sufficiently large coalition can do that. Belief correction still helps when it shifts the
audience’s own reaction, so the two instruments are complements rather than substitutes.
The aim here is to identify the instrument that works when the obstacle is exposure along
the way rather than at the destination, so failure privacy is not a design detail. It is the
feature that lets safe coalitions tunnel through exposure barriers that public cascades cannot
cross. The lens is portable to any setting where speaking is safe in numbers but dangerous

Contributions and relation to the literature.
The paper makes three contributions. First, it recasts expression under retaliation as safe
coalition formation and characterizes the reachable frontier. Self-protecting coalitions form
a lattice with a unique largest C+(S) and least C−(S) (Theorems 1 and 2). The decentral-
ized expression game selects the cascade bound C−(Theorem 3), while the social assurance
contract implements the tunnel C+ (Theorem 4). The gap C+ \ C−is the mechanism’s con-
tribution. A structural converse, read as a taxonomy of what each familiar institution lacks,
shows that crossing the gap requires verified, non-public, co-released participation, namely
failure privacy (Theorem 5); a companion proposition states how that necessity degrades
as participants tolerate calculated risks, with the regret-free class as its zero-tolerance limit
(Proposition 3).
Second, making the opponent strategic yields the striking prediction that the opponent
fights a released coalition only when retaliation is feasible, and folds once resilience clears
its resolve cutoff (Proposition 4).
This generates a threshold (Proposition 5) separating
the regime in which surfacing the hidden supporters protects them because their resilience
outpaces the opponent’s escalation, from the regime in which it backfires because escalation
dominates. Interestingly, observed retaliation can rise while actual suppression falls, because
the contract surfaces people who were invisible before: each newly visible supporter can now
be fought, yet most survive. Among the hidden supporters, then, more recorded retaliation
signals a better-functioning institution, not a worse one (Theorem 6, Corollary 2). This is
a warning rather than an identification strategy, because the people it concerns are exactly
those no dataset records under open organizing. The binary opponent is then shown dis-
pensable rather than defended: in a per-member model where the opponent chooses whom
to punish, the reversal holds member by member, punishment concentrates on the most
damaging participants, and the opponent’s escalation slope is an equilibrium object, with a
capacity cap delivering the helping case (Propositions 6 and 7). Third, the welfare question

The fixed-point machinery is standard. Monotone operators on a lattice have extremal
fixed points (Topkis, 1998), threshold cascades converge to the least fixed point above a
seed (Granovetter, 1978; Schelling, 1978), and adaptive dynamics in games with strategic
complementarities are confined between extremal equilibria (Milgrom and Roberts, 1990).
Regret-free safety, rather than equilibrium selection or learning dynamics, collapses the set
reachable by any uncoordinated, one-at-a-time process to C−. The structural kinship is with
Gale (2001): the same primitives—irreversible, public, monotone participation with positive
spillovers—and both C−and C+ lie in his reachable set (Proposition 9, Appendix B). The
kinship is structural, and that is all it is. Gale’s large limits are carried by equilibrium trust:
a player takes a step that does not yet protect her because the others’ strategies prescribe
that they will follow, and she can afford the wait because payoffs attach only to the limit, so
standing exposed costs nothing. Whatever the merits of that mechanism in theory, the world
reveals its limits in practice. Views with broad private support stay unspoken for years; at
Wells Fargo, employees reported alone and were picked off one at a time while thousands
who agreed stayed silent; and organizers everywhere build precisely the institutions that
working trust would make redundant—the confidential card drive, the allegation escrow, the
privately circulated letter. People keep quiet for lack of a named coalition, not for lack of
company. The problem this paper studies is the one that remains after equilibrium trust
has had its chance: what is reachable when no step may lean on promised follow-through,
and what must substitute when trust is not extended (Theorem 5). On the mechanism
side, conditional release is familiar for money in assurance contracts and provision-point
mechanisms (Bagnoli and Lipman, 1989; Tabarrok, 1998), contingent contracts (Segal, 1999),

A.
Regret-Free Safety
The two institutions the paper compares face the same safety requirement. They differ in
what can be known when a name becomes public. I make the requirement an axiom and the
informational difference a primitive.
Participation is uncertain.

How to read regret-freeness.
The axiom characterizes the frontier of institutions that never expose a participant in
an unprotecting coalition.
It is not a universal prediction of behavior.
Vanguards who
burn themselves to recruit followers, agents who accept exposure lotteries, participants who
receive transfers, and movements that rely on equilibrium trust `a la Gale (2001) can all
reach beyond the cascade. They violate regret-freeness rather than refute the theorems. For
the population the paper concerns, however, the axiom is also behavioral. Exposure-averse
people come forward only when being named is, on net, safe for them. For them the axiom is
the binding constraint, so the cascade bound is a positive prediction: a movement composed
of safety-seekers stalls at the smallest reachable self-protecting coalition even when a much
larger one would be collectively safe, the formal content of Kuran (1997)’s latent bandwagons.
The vanguard is not a counterexample to the bound; she is an agent outside the population
it predicts. The seed, the committed few who move first, is likewise outside it. The model
explains where a movement of safety-seekers stalls, not how it starts.
Coordinated reveals are tunnels.
Call a mechanism failure-private if a completed commitment is made public only as part
of a released coalition that protects its members, and never otherwise. In particular, it is
never revealed on failure and never revealed while the coalition is still too small to protect its
members. Assumption 2 also classifies institutions that appear to beat the cascade without
a formal platform. A signed open letter circulated privately and published once enough
names are on it is a conditional-disclosure mechanism. The circulating draft is the private
holding stage, and each signature is a completed commitment held non-publicly until release.
Two colleagues who exchange credible pledges and then go public together are running a
two-person informal escrow. The theorems do not deny that these institutions exist. They
classify them as tunnels. Informal tunnels are bounded by the reach of pairwise trust; the
formal mechanism’s role is to scale verification beyond the trust network.

C.
Verified Co-Participation Necessity
The tunnel’s advantage rests on one feature: it holds commitments privately and conditions
release on them.
The next theorem shows that this feature is not merely sufficient but
necessary. Any safe institution that beats the cascade must contain it.
Mechanisms.
An expression mechanism is an extensive game form with a platform and the agents in S,
with participation uncertainty as in Assumption 2. Each intended action completes or fails,
and transitions condition only on completed actions. An action is either an exposure action,
whose completion makes the agent’s name public at that history, or a private commitment,
whose completion is verified by the platform but kept private. A private commitment is
a completed, verified act, a signature lodged in escrow, not a mere intention to act. The
working distinction is between commitments that are public and commitments that are
completed but held, exactly the signed-or-not state of a cryptographic escrow.
The public state Ch at history h is the set of agents publicly named on the path to h,
increasing along play. A mechanism is public-only if the public state’s transitions condition
only on exposure actions. Such a platform holds no completed private commitment that in-
fluences whether or when any name becomes public. Regret-free safety is as in Assumption 3.
Throughout, mechanisms are consent-respecting: a name becomes public only through that
agent’s own completed exposure action or through a co-release she has authorized, so the
platform never publishes a non-consenting agent. The bounds below, and the statement
that no mechanism reaches a public coalition larger than C+, are within this class. The
claim is not that a platform could not print arbitrary names, but that no consent-respecting,
regret-free mechanism does.

What the binary is.
Fight-or-fold, and the single outcome the coalition’s members share, is a statement about
the expression, not about person-by-person punishment. What is decided when a coalition is
released is whether the contested position enters the Overton window—whether it becomes
something one can now say—and that outcome is genuinely collective and close to binary: a
taboo falls for everyone or for no one, a union is certified or it is not, an allegation is credited
or dismissed. The window moves here as the consequence of a released coalition, not of
information alone (Corollary 4). The individual harms hi are keyed to the shared outcome
because being the known holder of a still-unsayable view is the dominant exposure; a member
of a coalition that wins its fight is largely protected by the very shift it achieved. What the
reduced form suppresses is idiosyncratic punishment incident to victory—the organizer fired
in a drive that succeeds anyway—which is real but bounded: a member punished inside a
victorious coalition is punished for a position the outcome has just made sayable, and stands
shielded by the very shift the coalition achieved—contained exposure, against the open-

IV.
Applications and Empirical Content
The mechanism plays out in concrete settings: reporting, organizing, and their harmful
mirror. It also yields distinctive, testable predictions.
A.
Applications
The applications sort by the sign of χ, the social value a planner places on the harm a
coalition does to its opponent. The welfare change from reaching C+ rather than C−is the
participant surplus plus χ times the harm done to the opponent by the high-stakes partici-
pants failure privacy unlocks, with the full accounting in Appendix E. Where the coordinated
action harms an opponent society would want checked, such as a wrongdoer, abusive em-
ployer, or repressive authority, failure privacy is welfare-improving (χ > 0). Where it harms
outsiders, as when a cartel forms against consumers, it is welfare-reducing (χ < 0). I take
the cleanest accountability case first, then organizing and dissent, then the harmful mirror
image.

Open organizing, such as wearing the button, is a cascade, safe at every step.

A dual-use remark: collusion (χ < 0).
The welfare logic is sign-sensitive, and the harmful case is worth a brief remark rather

coordinated action harms outsiders (χ < 0, here harm to consumers), the welfare accounting

V.
Conclusion
Public expression is a problem of safe coalition formation. The governing institutional
question is whether participants must be exposed on the way to the coalition, or whether their
participation can be verified privately first. Under the regret-free safety axiom, decentralized
expression cascades to, and its equilibria select, the least closed self-protecting coalition
C−.
A social assurance contract tunnels to the greatest one, C+.
Beating the cascade

seed-to-threshold exposure tail net of friction. This is the feasibility frontier.
The reachable frontier is only half the story. The other half is what an observer sees.
Administrator credibility often does not bind, because a sympathetic keeper or a trustless

better-protected coalition is more often left alone. Failure-private assembly, by surfacing
exactly the people whom open organizing leaves silent, can therefore raise the retaliation an
observer records on that newly surfaced margin even as it lowers how much expression is
actually suppressed. On that margin, observed retaliation is a negatively signed proxy for
institutional performance. Scoring anti-suppression institutions by counting firings, crack-
downs, or complaints can recover the wrong sign because the institution changes the latent
denominator of visible expression. Whether surfacing helps at all is governed by the threshold
of Proposition 5: the contract protects the people it surfaces when the coalition’s resilience
outruns the opponent’s escalation, and backfires when escalation dominates. The reversal is
the measurement caution that rides on the helping side.
Whether reach should be extended is a welfare question with a sign. Failure privacy unlocks
the most-exposed participants, who are the agents whose action most threatens the opponent
of the collective action—when exposure tracks the damage a participant does rather than
her vulnerability. Since society values that same opponent harm, the welfare effect of failure
privacy is the participant surplus plus the social value of the harm done by the high-stakes
participants it newly reaches. The technology is dual-use: welfare-improving where the tail is
socially valuable, as in whistleblowing and dissent, and welfare-reducing where it is harmful,
as in collusion. In harmful domains, a large reachable gap is a warning sign rather than a
success.

Relation to Dynamic Coordination
The cascade shares its primitives with a known object. Gale (2001) studies monotone
games: a stage game played over discrete dates with actions constrained to be nondecreasing
and publicly observed, irreversible, public, monotone participation, with positive spillovers (a
player’s payoff is nondecreasing in others’ actions). He characterizes the outcomes reachable
in subgame-perfect equilibrium as exactly the approachable profiles, and notes there is no
folk theorem, so the reachable set is sharp. The one feature that separates his environment
from ours is the payoff primitive: in Gale, payoffs are evaluated only at the limit profile and
are undiscounted, so a player who is momentarily in a profile that does not yet protect her
bears no cost for it; the path is sustained by the equilibrium expectation that others follow
to a limit she prefers.
Specialize to the finite binary game in which ai ∈{0, 1} is irreversible public exposure,
payoffs have positive spillovers (Assumption 1), abstention gives zero, and a named agent’s
payoff πi(C) is nonnegative iff C ∈Pi.

Who Runs the Contract
The tunnel delegates one task: hold verified commitments privately, and release the names
exactly when the announced rule says to, and never otherwise. Whether a strategic inter-
mediary will actually run the promised mechanism is the credibility question of Akbarpour
and Li (2020). Three observations explain why this margin, unlike the opponent’s, seldom
binds in the applications this paper concerns, and what remains open where it does.
First, the administrator is chosen, not given. Organizers pick the keeper—the union run-
ning its own card drive, the movement lawyer, the clinic operating the escrow—and they pick
a party whose payoff rises with the coalition’s success. Selection does the incentive work that
mechanism design would otherwise have to do. Second, the task is mechanical enough to need
no keeper at all: verify commitments, test a threshold, release on success. Cryptographic
escrows implement exactly this rule with no trusted party (Arun et al., 2019; Rajan et al.,
2018), so where no sympathetic keeper exists, a trustless protocol substitutes. The residual
case, an administrator neither aligned by selection nor mechanical by construction—say, an
indifferent commercial platform—is then a choice among institutions rather than a binding
constraint: an organizer who would have to trust it can use the protocol instead.
Third, where neither an aligned keeper nor a protocol is available, informality substitutes
at small scale. Two colleagues who exchange credible pledges and go public together run a
two-person escrow; a tight-knit group co-moves on the strength of personal ties. I call this
ambient trust: pairwise confidence in follow-through that substitutes for formal verification.
It implements the same conditional logic as the mechanism, an ambient-trust tunnel, but
its reach is bounded by the reach of pairwise trust rather than by C+. That bound is what
