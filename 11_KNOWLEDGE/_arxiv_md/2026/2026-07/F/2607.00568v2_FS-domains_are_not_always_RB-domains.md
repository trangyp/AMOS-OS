Abstract
We prove that Lawson’s planar closed-disk domain is not an RB-domain. This domain is the dcpo of all closed
disks in the Euclidean plane, together with the whole plane as bottom, ordered by reverse inclusion. Since this
domain is an FS-domain, it gives a concrete example of an FS-domain which is not an RB-domain, answering
negatively the long-standing open problem in domain theory of whether FS-domains and RB-domains are
identical.
Keywords:
closed disk domain, FS-domain, RB-domain, Lorentz cone

1. Introduction
Domain theory was introduced by Dana Scott to give mathematical model to denotational semantics
of programming languages [14–16]. In order to interpret higher order types in programming languages,
a particularly important requirement on categories of semantic domains is that they should be cartesian
closed (i.e. closed under function spaces). The most important objects in domain theory are continuous
domains and algebraic domains. However, the category CONT (resp. ALG ) of continuous domains (resp.
algebraic domains) and Scott continuous maps is not cartesian closed. Hence, looking for cartesian closed
full subcategory of CONT (resp. ALG) is an important task in domain theory. In 1983, Smyth [17] proved
that the category of SFP (aka countably based bifinite domains), which was introduced by Gordon Plotkin
[13], is the largest cartesian closed full subcategory of the category of ω- algebraic pointed dcpos. Following
Smyth’s path, Achim Jung [7, 8] introduced two special domains called L-domain and FS-domain and showed
that, the categories CL and FS consisting of continuous L-domains and FS-domains respectively, are the
only two maximal cartesian closed full subcategories of CONT if one works in pointed continuous domains.
Replacing FS-domais by bifinite domains, the algebraic case also holds. Particularly, bifinite domains are
equal to algebraic FS-domains, and the retracts of bifinite domains, called RB-domains, form a cartesian
closed category [1, Proposition 4.2.12]. Since each RB-domain is an FS-domain, it arises a famous open
problem in domain theory since 1990s [1, 5] as follows : are FS-domains and RB-domains identical? In the
setting of L-domains, Liang and Keimel gave a positive answer [12] and similar results are obtained in [19].
In this paper we give a negative answer to this question by proving that Lawson’s planar closed-disk domain
is not an RB-domain.

2. Preliminaries
In this section, we recall the domain-theoretic definitions and introduce the planar closed-disk domain.
For the convenience of readers more familiar with domain theory than with measure theory, we record the

2.1. Domain-theoretic definitions and the planar disk domain
We use standard terminology from domain theory; see [1, 5, 6]. A subset A of a poset is directed if it is
nonempty and every two elements of A have an upper bound in A. A dcpo is a poset in which every directed
subset has a supremum. A map between dcpos is Scott-continuous if it is monotone and preserves directed
suprema. Let D, E be two dcpos. The pointwise order between two Scott continuous maps f, g from D into
E is defined as follows:
f ≤g
⇐⇒
f(x) ≤g(x), ∀x ∈D.
For elements x, y in a dcpo, one writes x ≪y, called x is way-below y, if, whenever A is directed and
y ≤sup A, there is a ∈A such that x ≤a. A dcpo is continuous if every element is the directed supremum
of the elements way-below it.
Let D be a dcpo. A Scott-continuous map f : D →D is finitely separated from the identity if there is a
finite set M ⊆D such that for every x ∈D there exists m ∈M with f(x) ≤m ≤x.
Definition 2.1 (FS-domain). [1, Definition 4.2.9] A dcpo D is an FS-domain if there a directed family
(ri)i∈I of Scott-continuous maps finitely separated from the identity idD such that W
i∈I ri = idD pointwise.
A deflation on a dcpo D is a Scott-continuous finite-image map r : D →D such that r ≤idD. A dcpo D
is a bifinite domain if the identity map idD is the pointwise supremum of a directed family of Scott-continuous
idempotent deflations. A dcpo is an RB-domain if it is a Scott-continuous retract of a bifinite domain. We
shall use the following standard result from the finite-deflation characterization of RB domains.

3. Order properties of the disk model and the RB local approximation
We first introduce the Lorentz cone and the corresponding cone order. A subset of an Euclidean space
is closed if it contains all limits of convergent sequences of its points, and it is convex if the line segment
between any two of its points is still contained in the subset. A cone is a nonempty closed convex set such
that λC ⊆C for all λ ≥0. A cone is pointed if C ∩(−C) = {0}.
Put C = {(t, z) ∈R × R2 : t ≥|z|} and H = (−∞, 0] × R2. This is the standard three-dimensional
Lorentz cone, also called the second-order cone; see [2, Section 2.2.3]. In particular, it is a standard closed
convex cone. Its closedness is also immediate here: it is closed because it is the inverse image of the closed
interval [0, ∞) under the continuous function (t, z) 7→t −|z|. It is convex by the triangle inequality: if
(t, z), (s, w) ∈C and 0 ≤λ ≤1, then
|λz + (1 −λ)w| ≤λ|z| + (1 −λ)|w| ≤λt + (1 −λ)s.
It is pointed, since (t, z), −(t, z) ∈C implies t ≥|z| and −t ≥|z|, hence t = 0 and z = 0. The self-duality of
C is proved in Lemma 4.1 below.
The cone order induced by C is
x ≤C y
⇐⇒
y −x ∈C.

5. The planar closed disk model is not an RB-domain
