Abstract. Let p be an odd prime, and let G be a finite group whose order is divisible by
p. Suppose that every proper subgroup of G whose order is divisible by p is supersolvable,
while G itself is not. In this paper, we investigate the arithmetic and structural properties
of such groups, addressing both the solvable and nonsolvable cases.
1. Introduction
In this paper, all groups are assumed to be finite and standard notation is followed (e.g.
[14]). Let X be a class of groups. A group G is called a X-critical group if G /∈X and
every proper subgroup of G belongs to X; that is, G is a minimal non-X-group. Well-
known examples of critical groups include minimal non-abelian groups (Miller and Moreno
[20]), minimal non-nilpotent groups (Schmidt [22]), and minimal non-supersolvable groups
(Doerk [8]). Beyond these classical examples, X-critical groups have been studied in a
number of other classes of groups. Following the nomenclature adopted by several authors
[15, 19], we introduce the following definition.
Definition 1.1. Let p be a prime number. We say that a group G is a pd-group if its order
is divisible by p. The group G is called X-semicritical with respect to p if G is a pd-group,
G ̸∈X, and every proper pd-subgroup of G lies in X.
The study of X-semicritical groups with respect to a prime reveals minimal local ob-
structions that prevent a group from belonging to X, thus illustrating how local and
global properties interact in the theory of classes of groups. However, as noted in [19],
determining the structure of an X-semicritical group is, in general, a difficult problem.
A simpler problem arises, requiring only elementary results, when X = N or A, that is,

Recall that a group G is said to be quasi-simple when G is perfect (i.e. [G, G] = G) and
G/Z(G) is a nonabelian simple group.
Theorem A. Let G be a nonsolvable group and p an odd prime. Then G is U-semicritical
with respect to p if and only if G is quasi-simple, Z(G) = Op′(G), and G/Z(G) is U-
semicritical with respect to p.
As mentioned above, [2, Theorem 3.3] precisely determines which nonabelian simple
groups are U-semicritical with respect to 3, namely the groups PSL2(2p) for any odd
prime p. However, establishing a complete classification of U-semicritical simple groups
with respect to an arbitrary odd prime is a significantly more challenging task (see Remark
3.2). Nevertheless, in this paper, we establish a criterion for alternating groups to be U-
semicritical for an arbitrary prime and provide a complete list of those sporadic simple
groups that are U-semicritical with respect to some prime. This has been made possible
thanks to the recently completed classification of the maximal subgroups of the Monster
group [7].

In Section 4, we examine the structure of solvable U-semicritical groups with respect to
an odd prime. As mentioned earlier, U-semicritical groups with respect to 2 are solvable
and, in fact, are 2-nilpotent with Sylow 2-subgroups of order 2 [19, Theorem 1.2]. Note
that supersolvable groups and U-critical groups both have p-length 1 for every prime
divisor p of their order. This follows immediately from the fact that every supersolvable
group possesses a Sylow tower, and from the classification of minimal non-supersolvable
groups (see, for instance, [1]), respectively. Theorem D shows that solvable U-semicritical
groups with respect to an odd prime p also have p-length 1. In fact, our hypotheses are
more restrictive than those of [21, Theorem C] since we focus on a single prime divisor of
the group order. However, we are able to obtain further structural properties. We denote
by π(G) the set of prime numbers that divide the order of a group G.
Theorem D. Let p an odd prime. Suppose that G is solvable and U-semicritical with
respect to p. Then |π(G)| ≤4, the p-length of G is 1, and one of the following possibilities
occurs:
(1) G is U-critical (of order divisible by p);
(2) Assume Op(G) > 1. Then G = Op(G)M, where Op(G) is a minimal normal
subgroup of G, and M is a maximal p′-subgroup of G that is U-critical. If, in
addition, Op′(G) = 1, then M is A-critical, |G|p ≥p2 and |π(G)| = 3.
(3) Assume Op(G) = 1. Then |G|p = p and G = PM, where P is a Sylow p-subgroup
of G and M is a maximal p′-subgroup of G that is not supersolvable (not necessarily
U-critical).
In the final section, we will provide several examples to show that all the cases listed
in Theorem D actually occur.

3. Proofs of Theorems B and C
To determine which alternating groups are U-semicritical with respect to a given prime,
we will employ the well-known classification of transitive permutation groups of degree
prime. Combining Galois’s classification of solvable transitive groups of prime degree, a
result of Burnside [4], and the Classification of Finite Simple Groups (see [5] for example),
we summarize these results as follows.
Theorem 3.1. A transitive permutation group G of prime degree p is one of the following:
(1) In the solvable case, G is isomorphic to a subgroup of the general affine linear
group AGL(1, p) that contains the translation subgroup (of order p).
(2) In the nonsolvable case, G is isomorphic to:
(a) Ap or Sp where p ≥5;
(b) a group H such that PSL(d, q) ≤H ≤PΓL(d, q) where (qd −1)/(q −1) = p
with q a prime power and d ≥2.
(c) PSL(2, 11) or M11 when p = 11, or M23 when p = 23.
The above classification, together with several properties of certain simple groups, such
as PSL(n, q), is sufficient to demonstrate Theorem B.
Proof of Theorem B. First, we analyze A5. Since the maximal subgroups of A5 are D10,
A4 and S3, we clearly see that A5 is U-semicritical only with respect to 5. As the prime
5 satisfies the conditions given in the statement, we can assume for the rest of the proof
that n > 5. Also, if n is a composite number, then the set of primes dividing n! is exactly
the same as those dividing (n −1)!. As An−1 ≤An, we conclude that An cannot be U-
semicritical with respect to any prime that divides its order. Henceforth, we will assume
that n is prime, say p (larger than 5). In this case, for every prime divisor r ̸= p of the
order of Ap, the same argument shows that Ap is not U-semicritical with respect to r. For
that reason, we only need to study when Ap is U-semicritical with respect to p.
First, since M11 and M23 are subgroups of A11 and A23, respectively, we deduce that
both alternating groups cannot be U-semicritical with respect to 11 and 23, respectively.
Now, suppose that we can write p = (qd −1)/(q −1) for some prime power q and some
d ≥2.
It is known that PSL(d, q) acts in natural and transitive way on the set of
(qd −1)/(q −1) = p points contained in the projective plane PG(d −1, q). This means
that PSL(d, q) ≤Sp. Now, since this subgroup is nonabelian simple because p > 5, the
simplicity of Ap certainly gives PSL(d, q) ≤Ap. Furthermore, we claim that the equality
does not hold.
This is due to the fact that the only ismorphisms that exist between
both families of simple groups are: PSL(2, 4) ∼= PSL(2, 5) ∼= A5, PSL(2, 9) ∼= A7 and

This finishes the proof of the direct implication.

Remark 3.2. It seems very complex to determine which simple groups of Lie type are
U-semicritical with respect to a given prime. Even for the family PSL(2, q) with q a
prime power, we want to show that the problem exhibits considerable difficulty. However,
when q is prime, it is not hard to determine which primes such a group is U-semicritical
with respect to. This is due to the structure of the maximal subgroups of PSL(2, q). As
stated in Theorem 2.4, the maximal subgroups are the Borel subgroups (of order q(q −1)
or q(q −1)/2), which are supersolvable; certain dihedral groups, which are supersolvable
too, and possibly A4, S4 and A5 depending on the arithmetical properties of q. Thus, for
p ̸= 2, 3, 5 we have that PSL(2, q) is U-semicritical. Now, if q is not a prime, again by
Theorem 2.4, there exist maximal subgroups isomorphic to PSL(2, q0) and PGL(2, q0) for
an appropriated q0, so PSL(2, q) is not U-semicritical with respect to a given prime p ≥7
dividing the order of such subgroups when they are not solvable. More generally, if q is a
prime power but not a prime and n ≥3 one can give examples of groups PSL(n, q) that
may be U-semicritical or not with respect to a given prime. For example, PSL(2, 27) is
U-semicritical with respect to 7 but not with respect to 13; PSL(5, 2) is U-semicritical
with respect to 31 but not with respect to 7; or PSL(3, 8) is U-semicritical with respect
to 73 but not with respect to 7.
4. Solvable U-semicritical groups
For dealing with solvable U-semicritical groups, we will utilize standard properties con-
cerning solvable, supersolvable and minimal non-supersolvable groups, as well as the clas-
sification of minimal nonabelian groups (for instance, see [1, Theorem 1]. In accordance
with the latter classification, one easily deduces the following.
Lemma 4.1. A minimal nonabelian group that is not supersolvable has the following
structure: G = Vq ⋊Crs, where q and r are different prime numbers, s is a positive
integer, and Vq is an irreducible Crs-module over the field of q elements of dimension at
least 2, with the kernel being the maximal subgroup of Crs.
We will also make use of the next result to prove Theorem D.
Lemma 4.2. [8] Let G be a finite group. Suppose that A, B, C and D are supersolvable
subgroups of G. If any pairs of |G : A|, |G : B|, |G : C| and |G : D| are coprime, then G
is supersolvable.
Proof of Theorem D. We begin by showing that |π(G)| ≤4. Set π(G) = {p0, p1, . . . , pr}
with p0 = p. Since G is solvable, it possesses a Hall p′
i-subgroup, say Hi, for every prime
pi with i = 0, 1, . . . r. Suppose that |π(G)| > 4, so r > 3. Then, for every i = 1, . . . , r, we
have that Hi is a proper subgroup of G of order divisible by p, so by hypothesis, every Hi
is supersolvable. Thus, there are at least four subgroups H1, H2, H3, H4 whose indices in
G are powers of distinct primes. In particular, the indices are pairwise coprime. Applying

Lemma 4.1, we would get that G is supersolvable, contradicting our assumptions. This
proves |π(G)| ≤4.
Next, we prove that G satisfies the properties appearing in each of the cases of the
statement and that the p-length of G is 1. If G is U-critical (of order divisible by p), it
is clear that G has p-length 1 according to the structure of such groups [1, Theorem 10].
Henceforth, for the rest of the proof we will assume that G is not U-critical, that is, there
exists a maximal subgroup M of G that is not supersolvable. By hypothesis, M must be
a p′-group, and then by the solvability of G, it is clear that M is a Hall p′-subgroup of G.
Assume first that Op(G) ̸= 1 and we will prove (2).
The maximality of M gives
G = MOp(G), and obviously, G has p-length 1. Suppose that there exists L a minimal
normal subgroup of G properly contained in Op(G). Then LM < G is supersolvable by
hypothesis, contradicting the non-supersolvability of M. Accordingly, Op(G) must be
a minimal normal subgroup of G. Furthermore, if K is a maximal subgroup M, then
Op(G)K < G is supersolvable, so K is also supersolvable, and this proves that M is
U-critical. Thus, the first part of (2) is proved. Suppose, in addition, that Op′(G) = 1.
Then, for every maximal subgroup K of M, the subgroup KOp(G) is supersolvable by
hypothesis and, as a consequence, (KOp(G))′ = K′[K, Op(G)] is nilpotent. It follows that
K′, being a normal p-complement of (KOp(G))′, centralizes [K, Op(G)], so in particular,
[Op(G), K′, K′] = 1. By coprime action, we deduce that K′ ⊆CG(Op(G)). Now, since
Op′(G) = 1, it is known that CG(Op(G)) ⊆Op(G), which gives K′ = 1. Moreover, as
seen above, M is not supersolvable, in particular, is not abelian, so we conclude that M
is also A-critical. Furthermore, in view of [23, Theorem 1.1], we have |π(M)| = 2, and
hence |π(G)| = 3. Finally, observe that the action of M on Op(G) is faithful because
Op′(G) = 1, so M ≤Aut(Op(G)). We deduce that |Op(G)| ≥p2; otherwise, M would be
abelian, a contradiction. Therefore, the second part of (2) is proved.
Finally, we prove (3), so we assume Op(G) = 1, and by solvability Op′(G) > 1. Let
H = Op′,p(G) (possibly equal to G) and P a Sylow p-subgroup of G. The maximality of M
indicates that MH = G, and hence P ≤H. In particular, G has p-length 1. On the other
hand, note that Op′(G) ≤M, and we distinguish two possibilities. If Op′(G) = M, then
the normality of M clearly yields |P| = p, so we get all the conditions in (3). Henceforth,
we assume that Op′(G) < M. Consider NG(P), which is a proper subgroup of G, so it is
supersolvable by hypothesis. Take P0 to be a minimal normal subgroup of NG(P) with
P0 ≤P. Then, the supersolvability of NG(P) implies that |P0| = p. Also, the Frattini
argument gives G = HNG(P), and by Dedekind’s rule M = Op′(G)NM(P). Now, since
P0NM(P) ≤G, then P0NM(P)Op′(G) = P0M is also a subgroup of G. By maximality,
P0M = G, and this forces P0 = P. Thus, we again get the properties described in (3), so
the proof is finished.
□
