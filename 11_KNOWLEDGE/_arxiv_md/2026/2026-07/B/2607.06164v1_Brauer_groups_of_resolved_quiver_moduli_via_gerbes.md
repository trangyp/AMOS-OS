Abstract
We show that the Brauer group of any resolution of singularities of the moduli space of semistable quiver
representations is trivial. We do this by extending the quiver-curve dictionary, translating a proof of the
analogous result by Biswas–Hogadi–Holla for moduli of vector bundles on a curve to the setting of moduli
of quiver representations, giving an algebro-geometric proof. This gives a new proof of this triviality, first
proved by Le Bruyn–Schofield, building on algebraic (resp. cohomological) vanishing results due to Saltman
(resp. Colliot-Thélène–Sansuc). Reversing the logic, our approach gives a new algebro-geometric proof of these
vanishing results.

Introduction
Moduli spaces of (semi)stable quiver representations have been introduced in the 1990s [26]. Moduli spaces of
(semi)stable vector bundles on curves have been introduced in the 1960s [34, 35, 48, 49]. Whilst their origins are
seemingly very distinct, the former coming from representation theory and the latter from algebraic geometry,
they are both associated to categories of homological dimension 1. This leads to interesting parallels between the
two settings.
The starting point for this work is the following result [12, Theorem 1.1], for moduli spaces of vector bundles on
curves, where we work over an algebraically closed field k of characteristic 0.

Quiver-curve dictionary
The goal of this paper is not to prove a new result (because it was known already by
Le Bruyn–Schofield) but to expand the quiver-curve dictionary. Namely, we will take our approach for Theorem 1.1
from [12] for moduli spaces of vector bundles on curves, and explain how to adapt it to the setting of quiver
moduli. This gives a new instance of the quiver-curve dictionary, relating the properties (and their proofs) of
moduli of vector bundles on curves and moduli of quiver representations.
Other instances of the many parallels between these moduli spaces are given by similarities in
• their constructions as quotients using geometric invariant theory, their hyperkähler counterparts, and the
role of the Weil conjectures in the study of both, surveyed in [24];
• the role Lie algebras play, surveyed in [45];
• automorphisms and deformations of moduli spaces, comparing [33] for curves to [6, 7] for quivers;
• the universal object giving a fully faithful Fourier–Mukai functor, comparing [11, 17, 32] for curves to [3,
7] for quivers;
• the construction of intrinsic ample line bundles on the good moduli spaces from the stacky description
without resorting to geometric invariant theory, comparing [2] for curves to [8] for quivers;
• their birational properties, comparing [27] for curves to [46] for quivers;
• the positivity properties for the anticanonical line bundle, with moduli of vector bundles being Fano
varieties [15] and quiver moduli often being Fano varieties [22].
This article should thus mostly be seen in light of further extending the quiver-curve dictionary, and as an
elaboration of the special and interesting features for quiver moduli.
In Section 2 we recall the relevant details in the construction of quiver moduli, prove some results on how function
fields of quiver moduli compare to one another (Proposition 2.1), on the existence of stable representations
(Proposition 2.2), and recall the Reineke–Schröer description of the Brauer group of quiver moduli from [40],
which uses geometric objects called smooth models, see [16]. In Section 3 we discuss how one can translate the
approach from [12] to the setting at hand. In Section 4 we give some remarks on Theorem 1.1 and Theorem A,
we describe for an algebro-geometric audience what the role of generic matrices is in the earlier results, and we
comment on some loose ends in [12, 40].

At some point in the proof we will need to construct a 𝜃-stable representation with (indivisible) dimension

this is not automatic, as the moduli space can be empty, even for indivisible dimension vectors1.

Smooth models
On a moduli space, the (non-)existence of a universal bundle is an important property, as
it can be used to probe the geometry of the moduli space. In the setting of moduli of vector bundles on curves
this question is answered (in three different ways) in [4, 15, 38], and existence is shown to be equivalent to all
semistable bundles being stable. The analogous result for quiver moduli is shown in [40, Theorem 4.4], using the

4.1
Remarks on the statement
We are now in a position to make some remarks on Theorem 1.1 and Theorem A.
Remark 4.1. When gcd(𝑟, deg L) = 1 (resp. gcd(𝒅) = 1 and 𝜃is generic) these moduli spaces are smooth
projective varieties that are rational [27] (resp. [46]). This implies Theorem A and Theorem 1.1 by the birational
invariance of the Brauer group for smooth projective varieties [14, Proposition 6.2.7].
When gcd(𝑟, deg L) ≥2 (resp. gcd(𝒅) ≥5, as rationality is already known when gcd(𝒅) ∈{2, 3, 4} [18, 19]) these
moduli spaces are (usually) singular and their rationality is a major open question. Theorem A and Theorem 1.1
can thus be seen as the vanishing of an obstruction to (stable) rationality, which also motivated the results by
Saltman and Le Bruyn–Schofield.

4.3
The Brauer group of the semistable moduli space
In [12, Remark 3.1] it is claimed that the methods used in op. cit. show that the moduli space of semistable vector
bundles M𝐶(𝑟, L) has trivial Brauer group. The argument alluded to in [12, Remark 3.1] would also prove that the
moduli space of 𝜃-semistable representations M𝜃-sst(𝑄, 𝒅) has trivial Brauer group, by the methods in Section 3.
However, we want to point out that there is seemingly an issue with the argument sketched in op. cit. It
refers to a result that if an integral scheme 𝑋(with function field 𝐾) is locally factorial, then the natural
morphism Br(𝑋) →Br(𝐾) is injective. But this seems to require étale-locally factorial (or geometrically locally
factorial), cf. [14, Theorem 3.5.4] for the statement with this condition, and [14, §7.6 (3)] for a locally factorial yet
not geometrically locally factorial example where the injectivity does not hold. Thus, it is not clear to us whether
one can conclude that Br(M𝐶(𝑟, L)) = 0 (and likewise, it seems one cannot conclude Br(M𝜃-sst(𝑄, 𝒅)) = 0).
4.4
When stability coincides with semistability
It is conjectured4 in [40, Conjecture 4.3] that Theorem 2.6 holds without the condition of ample stability. It is
mentioned that it is not even known to hold for indivisible dimension vectors. There is, however, the following
easy proposition, which we will prove as a corollary to Theorem A.
Proposition 4.6. Let 𝑄be acyclic, and let 𝒅be a 𝜃-coprime dimension vector. Then 𝒅is indivisible, and we have
that Br(M𝜃-st(𝑄, 𝒅)) = 0, i.e., [40, Conjecture 4.3] holds in this case.
Proof. By 𝜃-coprimality we have that M𝜃-st(𝑄, 𝒅) = M𝜃-sst(𝑄, 𝒅) is a smooth and projective variety, and by [30,
Corollary 2] we have that it is stably birational to some quiver moduli space where ample stability does hold,
e.g., by taking some Kronecker moduli space with indivisible dimension vector [40, Proposition 6.2]. We apply
Theorem A to this moduli space, so by stable birational invariance of the Brauer group for smooth projective
varieties we then have Br(M𝜃-st(𝑄, 𝒅)) = Br(M𝜃-sst(𝑄, 𝒅)) = 0, which proves the statement.
□
