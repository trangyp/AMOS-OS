Bundling Complements*
Weijie Zhong
Abstract
I develop a duality-based multi-dimensional screening framework with a ge-
ometric characterization of combinatorial preferences. For a mechanism to be
optimal, the type distribution pins down required directions of binding feasi-
bility constraints, while the complementarity among bundles determines the
covered directions; optimality reduces to full coverage of required directions.
I apply the framework to a one-parameter family in which every bundle con-
taining a fixed core of items earns a complementarity premium. Two thresh-
olds organize the optimum: above a lower threshold the grand bundle must
be offered; above a higher threshold a core-peripheral menu — a bundled core
with optional add-ons that are not sold standalone — is optimal. The tight dis-
tributional condition for finiteness of the higher threshold is inclusivity, that
the menu exclude no near-top buyer.
Keywords: multi-dimensional screening, bundling, complementarity, optimal trans-
port, mechanism design, core-peripheral mechanisms.
JEL Classification: D42, D82, D86.

1 INTRODUCTION
Many products and services are sold with a core component paired with op-
tional add-ons unavailable standalone: AI assistants bundle premium reasoning
models with a base subscription, enterprise vendors license security and compli-
ance modules only atop their core platform, and cable operators have long sold
premium channels only as add-ons to a basic tier. In each, an add-on is worth
more paired with the core than alone — a premium model inside a workflow that
also runs faster base models for routine steps, a security module integrated with
the platform whose data it monitors. Such cross-good interactions generate combi-
natorial preferences: a bundle’s value is not the sum of its parts, and the interaction
— complementarity, in the case these examples share — drives the bundling deci-
sion.
This paper traces the comparative statics of the optimal screening mechanism
as the strength of complementarity among goods varies.
The model considers
a monopoly seller who sells multiple indivisible goods to a buyer with a multi-
dimensional private type. A valuation map determines the buyer type’s value for
each bundle of goods, which can exhibit complementarity, substitutability or ar-
bitrary combinatorial preferences.
The methodology extends the optimal-transport duality of Daskalakis et al.
(2017) and Kleiner and Manelli (2019) and surfaces a geometric structure of com-
binatorial preferences hidden in the dual cost. In the dual problem, the type distri-
bution and preference jointly determine a differential virtual value (positive where
providing buyer surplus increases revenue, negative where it decreases). The dif-
ferential virtual value pins down the required directions of the dual transport —
the directions of binding feasibility constraints under an optimal mechanism. The
allocation feasibility constraint attaches to each bundle a cone of covered directions
along which allocating the bundle maximizes the surplus differential. Optimal-
ity of a candidate menu reduces to a geometric coverage condition: every required
direction must lie inside the covered cone of the allocated bundle. Under linear val-
uation, the problem pulls back to the primitive type space and the two sides fully
separate — required directions are determined solely by the type distribution, and
the covered cones are determined solely by the valuation map.
This separation reframes the comparative-statics landscape. The additive-preferences

1.1 Literature review
Optimal mechanisms under additive multi-dimensional preferences. The struc-
tural foundations of multidimensional screening were laid by McAfee and McMil-
lan (1988), Armstrong (1996), and Rochet and Chone (1998), who established the
logic of exclusion, bunching, and convexity once the buyer has several dimensions
of private information. A central feature of the additive multi-good optimum, em-
phasized by Manelli and Vincent (2007) and traced in two-good detail by Pavlov
(2011), is that randomization (lotteries over bundles) is intrinsic rather than a tech-
nical artifact: deterministic posted prices are generically suboptimal. Manelli and
Vincent (2006) characterizes when deterministic bundle pricing nevertheless solves
the multi-good monopoly problem. Rochet (2024) surveys recent developments in
this literature.¹
Optimal-transport duality. A methodological strand within this literature char-
acterizes the additive multi-good optimum through a transport dual. Daskalakis
et al. (2017) and Kleiner and Manelli (2019) establish the duality and the saddle-
point characterization of the optimum against a dual cost 𝑐, applying the frame-
work to a range of canonical settings. In these foundational developments the cost
𝑐is taken as a primitive object, with the preference structure of the screening prob-
lem implicit in its definition. This paper builds on these foundations by decompos-
ing the cost: under linearity, 𝑐admits an explicit decomposition over its gradient
polytope, with each vertex (a bundle’s gradient) carrying its own cone of covered

Sufficient conditions for pure bundling. A separate strand identifies sufficient
conditions on the type distribution under which the additive optimum collapses
to pure bundling or to separate sales. McAfee et al. (1989) show that bundling
dominates separate sales when two non-complementary goods have stochastically
independent values. Giannakopoulos and Koutsoupias (2018) give exact two-good
sufficient conditions for pure bundling under uniform distributions. Menicucci
et al. (2015) establishes pure-bundling optimality for two additive, independently
distributed goods under non-negative virtual valuations. A parallel literature on
robust mechanism design, set in additive or nearly additive environments, charac-
terizes the optimum under various ambiguity sets: Carroll (2017) shows separate
sales are robust when only marginal distributions are known; Deb and Roesler
(2023) shows pure bundling is informationally robust when the prior is exchange-
able across items; Che and Zhong (2025) traces categorical bundling through a
hierarchy of moment-based ambiguity sets. Relatedly, Frick et al. (2026) show that
as the seller’s information about the buyer grows precise, pure bundling becomes
asymptotically optimal and strictly beats separate sales. Hart and Reny (2015) ob-
serve that revenue can fail to be monotone in the buyer’s value even in additive
multi-good settings. These papers locate the bundling pressure entirely in the
type distribution or in the seller’s information about it — preferences are addi-
tive, so the force comes from how the distribution tilts the marginal-value com-
parison toward the bundle. This paper introduces complementarity as a separate
preference primitive and justifies the practically prevalent core-peripheral menu
— which nests pure bundling as a special case — as the optimum under sufficient
complementarity.
Combinatorial preferences and bundling extensions. The closer comparators are
papers that allow non-additive bundle valuations. Yang (2025) develops a theory
of nested bundling in a one-dimensional type-space setting amenable to the My-
ersonian approach. Haghpanah and Hartline (2021) works in multi-dimensional
types with general non-additive bundle valuations and identifies a monotone-ratio
condition — via a path decomposition reducing the problem to a family of one-

4.4 From geometry to algebra: why core-peripheral?
Theorem 2 certifies the core-peripheral menu — supported on ℱ𝐶= {𝐵∶𝐶⊆𝐵}
— as optimal above 𝛼∗. Why this specific structure? The framework reveals an
asymmetry across bundles, organized by size: large bundles are hard to exclude from a
certifiable menu even when their cones are narrow; small bundles are easy to exclude even
when their cones are wide. The grand bundle [𝑁] is the extreme case: by Theorem 3,
once complementarity makes it a needed cover — the top-cell wedge — it cannot be
dropped, its protection structural rather than a matter of cone width. (Inclusivity,
Theorem 4, is a separate force: it requires every item’s top face to be served but
does not by itself pull in the grand bundle, since separate sales can leave every top
face served.) A singleton {𝑖}, in contrast, has a wide cone but is replaced for free
whenever some larger premium bundle covers item 𝑖: cone-wideness is geometric
slack the menu does not need.
Algebraic mirror: closure under supersets. The asymmetry has a direct algebraic
counterpart. The proof of Theorem 2 scales the gradients of the core-containing —
premium — bundles by 𝛼; within-premium cones are 𝛼-invariant (premium gra-
dients scale together) while between-premium cones widen. This invariance re-
quires the premium set to be closed under supersets — if 𝐵is premium, every 𝐵′ ⊇𝐵
must be too, or a non-premium superset breaks the cancellation. The natural such
family is ℱ𝐶, and every non-empty superset-closed family must contain [𝑁]: the
set-theoretic universality of the grand bundle is exactly its algebraic universality.

5.3 Three regimes in observed markets
The two thresholds traced in Sections 4.1 and 4.2 produce a comparative-statics
arc — separate sales below 𝛼∗, a mixed-bundling intermediate regime, core-peripheral
bundling above 𝛼∗— observable where the same goods are sold to segments dif-
fering in their integration premium, the complementarity premium 𝛼of (4).
AI providers maintain two parallel price lists for the same models. The con-
sumer segment — individuals using a chat interface for everyday tasks at a flat
monthly fee — receives a tiered, bundled mechanism: the ChatGPT Plus / Pro
ladder. The developer segment — engineers building applications, agents, and
back-end tools that call the models programmatically — receives an unbundled
per-token price list, with each model priced separately by usage.¹⁸ The two chan-
nels target observably distinct segments: a consumer’s value of having both models
inside one subscription comes from the unified conversational interface, the shared
memory, and the multi-model workflow the provider performs automatically, so
the integration premium is large; a developer’s routing logic, context management,
and model substitution live in the developer’s own code, so the integration pre-
mium is small.
Enterprise software shows the same segmentation at industry scale. Large tra-
ditional firms — banks, retailers, manufacturers, with limited internal engineering
capacity — typically license integrated SAP, Oracle, or Salesforce suites at the bun-
dled list price. Tech firms — Meta, Stripe, Netflix, with deep internal engineering
capacity — typically forgo the integrated suite and assemble a custom stack out
of best-of-breed components. Between the two extremes, mid-sized enterprises li-
cense a core platform together with selected optional modules: an integrated suite
for the workflows tightest with the platform’s data, separately priced add-ons for
the rest.
The three segments map naturally onto the three regimes of the comparative-
statics arc. Per-token API access and best-of-breed assembly sit below 𝛼∗, where
the integration premium is too small to force the grand bundle into the menu —
the bundle’s cone covers no required direction that some Ã  la carte option cannot
already cover. Mid-sized enterprise licensing sits in the intermediate (𝛼∗, 𝛼∗) band,
where the grand bundle covers some required directions but core-only cones miss

6 CONCLUSION
This paper develops a bundle-to-cone framework for optimal screening under
combinatorial preferences: each bundle carries a cone of covered directions, the
differential virtual value identifies the required directions, and optimality is certi-
fied by matching required to covered, cell by cell, with complementarity widening
the cones of core-containing bundles. Applied to a scalar complementarity pre-
mium 𝛼multiplying every bundle that contains a fixed core, the framework iden-
tifies two thresholds, each a cover-side event in 𝛼: a lower threshold 𝛼∗at which
the grand bundle’s cone first becomes a needed cover (Theorem 3), and an up-
per threshold 𝛼∗at which the cones of core-containing bundles jointly cover every
required direction (Theorem 2), certified by alignment regularity — the stochastic-
dominance condition that orders revenue-rewarding above revenue-absorbing types
in every alignment score. Inclusivity is the binding distributional condition for
finiteness of 𝛼∗(Theorem 4). The applications discharge these hypotheses from
primitives. For pure bundling the picture is sharpest: alignment regularity is sup-
plied by standard Myersonian regularity, and inclusivity characterizes optimal-
ity outright — up to a knife edge, the distribution is inclusive if and only if pure
bundling is optimal for all large 𝛼(Theorem 5). The two-tier family (Section 5.2)
and the iid Beta benchmark (Section 5.1) extend the picture to one add-on and to
higher dimensions.
The methodology is portable to richer slices of the combinatorial preference
space; Section 4.4 sketches the natural extensions — coexisting complementarity
systems and competing vertical hierarchies — as open questions on the algebraic
structure of premium bundles.
