Beyond travel mode: urban context shapes active mobility’s mental 
health effects over time 
 
Shujuan Chen1,2, *, Yue Li1,2, Ying Jin1 
 
1 Martin Centre for Architectural and Urban Studies, University of Cambridge, Cambridge, UK

* Correspondence to Shujuan Chen, The Martin Centre, Department of Architecture, University of Cambridge, 1

Abstract 
Active mobility is widely promoted for sustainable and healthier living, but whether it translates 
into equitable mental health benefits across individuals and places over time remains unknown. 
Using causal machine learning and causal deep learning in 264168 UK adults, we find substantial 
inequalities in individualized effects of active mobility on anxiety, depression, and common mental 
disorders. These inequalities widen over time and are strongly structured by urban context. For 
example, anxiety risk at follow-up ranges from a 40.6% reduction to a 10.1% increase across 
individuals, versus a 10.4% reduction to a 0.1% increase at baseline. Benefits are greatest in 
greener, safer, less polluted, and less deprived neighborhood environments, with 81.8% of 
individuals experiencing above-average benefits and mean anxiety risk reduced by 26.4%, versus 
10.4% of individuals and 7.4% reduction in the least supportive environments. Urban compact 
form further modifies these effects through nonlinear interactions with neighborhood 
environments, amplifying benefits only under supportive conditions. Despite these strong 
environmental gradients, genetic moderation is negligible. These findings suggest universal active 
mobility promotion could widen health inequalities if individual and contextual differences are not 
accounted for.

Introduction 
As cities expand and health challenges intensify, active mobility is increasingly recognized for its 
potential health co-benefits beyond decarbonization (1-9). Mental health is a particularly important 
domain for such benefits, given both the scale of the global burden, with one in seven individuals 
affected by a mental disorder and 71% not receiving treatment (10), and the potential for active 
mobility to serve as a routine behavioral intervention supporting psychological wellbeing on a 
daily basis (11, 12). Yet despite growing interest in promoting active mobility across sectors, 
evidence on its mental health effects remains unclear, particularly regarding who benefits and 
under what conditions. This lack of evidence on heterogeneity constrains the ability to design 
targeted, equitable interventions. 
Emerging evidence suggests that health responses to active mobility, including psychological 
outcomes, may not be uniform. At the individual level, the average effects of active mobility on 
cardiovascular disease have been found to vary by sex and body composition (7, 8), while effects 
on depressive severity were greater among younger adults and women (3), Associations with 
dementia risk appear strongly modified by genetic predisposition, such as Apolipoprotein E ε4 
carrier status (13). At the same time, the places in which active mobility occurs may matter. Some 
observational studies have reported lower psychological distress among active commuters, with 
stronger associations in greener and less deprived settings (5, 14, 15). Similarly, active commuting 
through natural environments has been linked to better health outcomes, with benefits moderated 
by environmental quality (16). In practice, planning frameworks worldwide further shape these 
contexts by promoting active mobility within compact urban development characterized by high 
density and accessibility (1, 2). Yet evidence on how compact urban form affect the health benefits 
of active mobility remained limited, as measures based only on population density do not capture 
compact urban form sufficiently (17). 
Several challenges in the existing work must be addressed to predict the psychological effects of 
active mobility with precision. First, prior studies have typically examined urban features in 
isolation, limiting their ability to characterize the full complexity of urban contexts to which active 
travelers are exposed, including both neighborhood environments and urban form (1). Second, 
most have relied on single mental-health indicators at single time points, providing only a partial 
view of psychological wellbeing across time and the continuum from symptoms to clinical 
diagnosis. Third, existing work has relied on estimating average associations from conventional 
risk models, rather than predicting individual causal effects that compare outcomes under active 
mobility with the counterfactual of motorized travel. With these limitations, it remains unknown 
whether the mental health benefits of active mobility are equitably distributed, or concentrated 
among specific populations and places. 
These gaps in evidence have direct policy consequences. If health co-benefits of active mobility 
are context-dependent, uniform promotion policies may fail to deliver them equitably and could 
widen health inequalities, especially as active mobility becomes more central to public health and

urban strategies. Given the limited feasibility of randomized experiments for estimating causal 
effects of such behavioral interventions, addressing these gaps requires analytical tools that go 
beyond average effects to estimate individual treatment effects and examine how they are 
structured by urban context. Although some machine learning (ML) or deep learning approaches 
for heterogeneous treatment effect estimation have been proposed in precision health, they have 
rarely been applied to urban mobility and health research (18). To date, no existing framework 
integrates individualized causal inference with the multiple dimensions of urban context needed 
to inform place-based health and urban policy. 
To address the gaps, we developed a causal framework incorporating stacked machine learning 
and deep survival modeling to quantify individualized and context-specific treatment effect of 
active mobility on mental health. Using prospective cohort data from 17977 UK Biobank 
neighborhoods over the period from 2006 to 2020, linked with environmental, electronic health 
records, and genetic data, we applied this framework to multiple mental health outcomes, including 
anxiety, depression, and common mental disorders (CMDs). We then investigated how these 
effects vary across two key dimensions of urban context: neighborhood environments and compact 
urban form. To characterize these dimensions, we constructed two corresponding metrics: a 
composite environmental supportiveness measure for transport health (THES), integrating 
physical, natural, and social conditions, and a functional urban compactness measure reflecting the 
intensity of urban activity and accessibility. We further examined how these two dimensions 
interact to shape mental health gains from active mobility promotion, identified thresholds and 
trade-offs in these effects, and assessed whether individual genetic susceptibility modifies them. 
These findings could provide evidence for targeted policies to advance health equity in densifying 
cities. Fig. 1 shows the data, models, and study design.

Results 
This study included 264168 (52.6%) of the UK Biobank participants, aged 38–70 years (mean age: 
52.6 years), from 17977 small-area neighborhoods with complete data on travel mode. At the 
baseline (2006–2010), 34747 participants screened positive for depression and 72762 participants 
screened positive for anxiety. The follow-up online mental health assessment in 2016 to 2017 was 
completed by 88915 participants, of whom 2434 screened positive for depression and 1993 
screened positive for anxiety. Over a median follow-up of 10.8 years from baseline to 2020, 14273 
participants received a first diagnosis of CMDs recorded in primary care or inpatient admissions. 
Participant characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table S4.

We developed a neighborhood environmental supportiveness score (THES) integrating green 
space, air pollution, deprivation, and crime into a single measure ranging from 5 to 50, with higher

densities of public transport, street movement, retail, and residential activity, ranging from 4 to 40

Across the UK, environmental supportiveness was generally higher in northern and rural areas 
and lower in major urban centers, while urban compactness showed the reverse pattern, 
concentrated in London and other large cities (Fig. 2a, b). Environmental supportiveness 
decreased with increasing urban compactness, reflecting higher crime, lower green space, higher 
air pollution, and greater deprivation in more compact areas (Fig. 2c, d).

Overall, active mobility was associated with highly unequal mental health benefits across 
individuals, and these inequalities were larger at follow-up than at baseline. On average, if 
individuals had taken active mobility instead of motorized travel, their risks of having mental 
disorders would have been 3.8% lower for depression and 3.3% lower for anxiety at baseline, 3.9% 
lower for depression and 15.6% lower for anxiety at follow-up, and 16% lower for 10-year risk of 
incident CMDs (Fig. 3).  
Specifically, for the online follow-up outcomes, ITEs for active mobility ranged from 0.839 to 
1.176 for depression (mean RR 0.961; p > 0.05), and from 0.594 to 1.101 for anxiety (mean RR 
0.844; p < 0.05). For mixed mode, ITEs ranged from 0.939 to 1.189 for depression (mean RR 
1.023; p > 0.05) and from 0.652 to 1.029 for anxiety (mean RR 0.853; p < 0.05). For clinically 
diagnosed CMDs from health records, ITEs ranged from 0.754 to 0.969 for active mobility (mean 
RR 0.838; p < 0.05), and from 0.867 to 1.056 for mixed mode (mean RR 0.936; p < 0.05; Fig.3).

RR 0.962; p < 0.05) and similar trends for anxiety (ITE range: 0.896 to 1.001; mean RR 0.967, p 
< 0.001). Mixed-mode ITEs ranged from 0.933 to 1.076 for depression (mean RR 0.996; p > 0.05) 
and showed similar patterns for anxiety.

ITEs, neighborhood environments, and urban compactness 
ITEs and neighborhood environments 
The dose-response relationship between environmental supportiveness and ITEs of active mobility 
for follow-up anxiety was monotonic and nonlinear (p < 0.0001 for nonlinearity), with benefits 
accelerating markedly in more supportive environments (Fig. 4a-e). Across categorical levels of 
environmental supportiveness, ITE distributions differed significantly (p < 0.0001), with the 
largest benefits concentrated in the most supportive environments (Fig. 4f-j). In the highest 
environmental supportiveness category (45–50), 81.7% of people showed enhanced benefits (RR 
below the mean RR) under active versus motorized travel, whereas in the lowest category, 10.4% 
participants showed enhanced effects.  
On average, active mobility in the most supportive environments corresponded to a markedly 
lower incident anxiety risk, with a 26.4% risk reduction (RR 0.736, 95% CI: 0.731–0.741). By 
contrast, in the least supportive environments the risk reduction was 7.4% (RR: 0.926, 95% 
CI:0.923–0.928). Overall, the mean risk reduction was 15.6%.  
Moreover, disparities in health gains of active mobility between more and less supportive 
environments widen at follow up, compared to baseline. Similar patterns were found for 
prospective depression (Fig. 4d, 4i), clinically diagnosed CMDs (Fig. 4e, 4j), and baseline 
depression and anxiety (Fig. 4a, b, f, g). Statistical test results were presented in Supplementary 
Table S5. Dose-response analyses using each component indicator (greenspace, air quality, 
deprivation, and crime) showed consistent trends, with more supportive environments associated 
with greater mental health benefits of active mobility (Supplementary Fig. S1–S3).

Joint modification of neighborhood environments and urban compactness

less supportive environments, benefits were generally weaker and declined monotonically as 
compactness increased. The interactions between environmental supportiveness and urban 
compactness were statistically significant (p < 0.01) and showed broadly consistent patterns across 
all health outcomes (Fig. 5; Supplementary Table S6). The underlying directed acyclic graph is

Spatial distributions of ITEs, neighborhood environments, and urban compactness 
The mental health benefits of active mobility showed substantial spatial inequalities across the UK, 
closely tracking neighborhood environmental supportiveness (Fig. 6). Areas with more supportive 
environments (higher THES) tended to have larger mental health gains from active mobility (lower 
ITE quartiles), whereas areas characterized by lower THES showed weaker benefits (higher ITE 
quartiles). This gradient was sharp: mean THES decreased from Q1 to Q4 across ITE quartiles 
(e.g., for anxiety, Q1: 37.7/50 vs Q4: 17.5/50) (Fig. 6, a1, b1, c1). This pattern was also reflected 
by settlement type: rural areas had the highest mean THES and the largest share of Q1, while major 
cities had lower THES and a higher proportion of higher ITE quartiles (Fig. 6, a2-3, b2-3, c2-3). 
These patterns were consistent across all outcomes (Fig. 5b, Fig. 5c for follow-up health outcomes; 
Supplement Fig. S5 for baseline health). 
These findings were complementary to the spatial gradient of ITEs and urban compactness (Fig. 
7). Lower ITE quartiles (greater benefits) from active mobility were more common in lower-
compactness areas, largely outside major cities. In contrast, higher ITE quartiles (weaker benefits) 
tended to cluster in highly compact major cities, most notably around London and other large 
conurbations. Accordingly, mean urban compactness increased across ITE quartiles from Q1 to 
Q4 (e.g., for anxiety, Q1: 14.1/40 vs Q4: 27.1/40) (Fig. 7, a2, b2, c2), and was highest in major 
cities (Fig. 7, a1, b1, c1). Similar patterns were observed across outcomes (Fig. 7b–7c for follow-
up outcomes; Supplement Fig. S6 for baseline outcomes).

ITEs and genetic susceptibility 
Finally, we evaluated whether individual genetic susceptibility to mental disorders, characterized 
using polygenic risk score (PRS) that summarizes the cumulative effect from common genetic 
variants associated with each disorder, modified the mental health effects of active mobility, and 
whether it interacted with environmental supportiveness. In Fig. 8, the composition of ITE 
quartiles varied markedly across THES categories, but showed little variation across PRS levels. 
Although differences in ITE quartile composition across PRS strata was statistically significant for 
some outcomes, the effect sizes were negligible (Cramer’s V 0.0103–0.0211, Supplementary Table 
S7ii). Consistently, PRS differed trivially between participants with high vs low ITEs (Cohen’s d 
-0.008 – 0.03; Supplementary Table S7i, Fig. S8). We found no evidence of joint modification by 
genetic risk and environmental supportiveness (p > 0.05, Supplementary Table S7iii). Similar 
results were observed for baseline health outcomes (Supplementary Fig. S7).

active mobility are strongly context-dependent and unevenly distributed, with neighborhood 
environments and urban form strongly shaping who and where benefit. Our findings suggest that 
universal promotion of active mobility without accounting for local context could widen health 
inequalities, and show the value of individualized causal approaches in health and urban research. 
This study represents one of the first efforts to characterize the full distribution of individual-level 
health benefits of active mobility, and to compare these effects across multiple follow-up horizons. 
Active mobility showed beneficial treatment effects that varied considerably across individuals, 
with on average stronger benefits at follow-up than at baseline, particularly for anxiety and 
clinically diagnosed CMDs. Average risk reductions were larger for anxiety at follow-up (15.6% 
lower risk) and for 10-year incident CMDs (16.2% lower risk). These increasing benefits may 
reflect the physical activity (PA) involved, which can support neurocognitive adaptation, self-
efficacy, and well-being that strengthen protective effects over time (21, 22). Risk reductions were 
larger for anxiety than depression, potentially because anxiety is more stress-reactive and 
behavior-responsive (23), consistent with evidence that PA-based interventions yield larger 
improvements in anxiety than in depression (24). Alternatively, genetic predisposition may 
constrain the responsiveness of depression to active mobility more than anxiety, as suggested by 
our finding that genetic moderation, though modest overall, was relatively more apparent for 
depression. Previous studies have also linked active mobility with better mental health, but most 
focused on average associations or a single time point (25, 26), rather than estimating treatment 
effects, particularly at the individual level. This individualized evidence moves beyond average 
population effects to identify who is most and least likely to benefit, providing a basis for targeted 
interventions.  
Turning to how these individual treatment effects were structured by urban contexts, we found that 
neighborhood environments strongly structure the mental health benefits of active mobility. Our 
composite measure of environmental supportiveness (THES) captures whether active mobility is 
undertaken in settings that are greener, cleaner, safer, and less deprived. This framing aligns with 
policy priorities on sustainable and healthy cities, equity, and safety. Mental health benefits were 
greater in more supportive environments with higher THES, consistent with evidence linking 
active commuting in natural environments to lower stress (3, 5). Spatially, enhanced effects were 
more common in rural areas where THES tended to be higher, whereas diminished effects clustered 
in major cities and other urban areas with lower THES.  
The magnitude of effect modification by environmental supportiveness was substantial. In the 
most supportive environments (THES 45–50), 81.8% of participants would experience enhanced 
benefits under active mobility, corresponding to a 26.4% lower risk of incident anxiety. In the least 
supportive environments, only 10.4% would experience enhanced effects, with a corresponding 
risk reduction of 7.4%. Environmental moderation was found weaker for EHR-based CMDs, likely 
because clinician-diagnosed outcomes are affected by case ascertainment, including health-
seeking behaviors, service access, and diagnostic practices (27-29). Despite the strong role of

environments, genetic susceptibility played a comparatively modest role in modifying mental 
health benefits of active mobility. These findings highlight the policy relevance of modifiable 
neighborhood environments in determining whether active mobility translates into mental health

Although compact-city strategies increase urban compactness to support accessibility and thus 
active mobility (1, 2), it remains unknown whether active mobility in compact urban form yields 
mental health co-benefits. Using our measure of functional urban compactness, we found that 
diminished health benefits clustered in major cities with higher urban compactness, such as Greater 
London, whereas enhanced benefits were more common in rural areas with lower compactness. 
Importantly, the associations between compactness and mental health effects of active mobility 
strongly depended on environmental supportiveness. Enhanced benefits were observed across 
compactness levels, but only in greener, cleaner, safer, and less deprived neighborhoods. In less 
supportive environments, benefits were consistently diminished and declined further as 
compactness increased. This may reflect higher exposure to traffic, noise, and safety-related 
stressors during active mobility in more compact settings, which can attenuate mental health gains 
(30, 31). Without buffering from supportive environmental quality, these stressors may outweigh 
the accessibility advantages of compactness (32-34). By contrast, greener, cleaner, and safer 
neighborhoods may mitigate these exposures, allowing accessibility advantages to translate into 
larger mental health gains (35, 36). 
Moreover, within supportive environments, the associations between compactness and mental 
health benefits were curvilinear, with weaker benefits at mid-range compactness and stronger 
benefits at both ends. This aligns with prior evidence of elevated depression risk in moderately 
dense compared to highly dense areas, although those results were based on building density (37). 
One possible explanation is that once compactness is sufficiently high and accomplished with 
supportive environments that offset stressors (e.g., noise, traffic, safety-related exposures), the 
accessibility advantages of compact urban form become more dominant, allowing health benefits 
of active mobility to accumulate. Such nonlinear variations in health effects across the 
compactness gradient, and its moderation by environmental quality, have rarely been described. 
Existing studies often relied on coarse urban-rural division and seldom tested its interactions with 
local environmental conditions, limiting their ability to detect curvilinear patterns or quantify 
trade-offs between compactness and environmental quality in shaping health co-benefits from 
active mobility (38, 39). 
This study has several strengths. First, we assessed mental health at different stages, from probable 
symptoms assessed via validated questionnaires to clinically diagnosed disorders identified 
through linked EHR. This provides insight relevant to population monitoring, case finding, and 
healthcare planning. Consistent findings across outcomes and data sources support the robustness 
of our results. Second, we characterized the complexity of wider neighborhood environments and 
compact urban form using composite measures that combine physical, natural, social, and built 
environmental domains, rather than isolated features. Third, we developed a causal framework

incorporating stacked ML and deep survival modeling to accommodate multimodal health 
outcomes, environmental, and genetic components, enabling estimation of heterogeneous effects 
with precision. By linking individual effects to place-based contexts, this framework enables 
context-sensitive estimation of treatment effects and supports more targeted evaluation of health 
gains from urban mobility.  
This work has several limitations. First, mental health measures based on self-reported 
questionnaires may introduce recall-related measurement bias. However, our analyses using 
clinically diagnosed outcomes from EHR showed broadly consistent patterns. Second, exposure 
misclassification related to residential location remains possible, but such error is likely non-
differential and would typically bias estimates toward the null (40). Finally, although propensity 
weighting improved baseline comparability between control and treatment groups, the non-
probability sampling nature of UKB participants warrants further validation through practical 
experiments to generalize our findings. 
Conclusions 
Using a causal framework with machine learning algorithms, we quantified individualized mental 
health effects of active mobility and uncovered how they were shaped by neighborhood 
environments, compact urban form, and genetic susceptibility. Our findings show that health co-
benefits of active mobility are strongly context-dependent and unequal across individuals and 
places, with neighborhood environments and compact urban form playing a far greater moderating 
role than genetic predisposition. These results suggest that strategies promoting active mobility 
should account for urban context to narrow rather than widen health inequalities. More broadly, 
these findings demonstrate the value of causal frameworks to inform individualized, place-
sensitive urban and public health strategies. 
Materials and Methods 
Study populations and area 
Our study population were selected from the UKB, a nationwide population-based dataset with 
prospective follow-up. This dataset recruited around 500000 adults aged 39-70 at baseline across 
the UK between 2006 and 2010, with multiple ongoing follow-up assessments and linked 
electronic health records (41). At baseline, all participants provided information on demographic, 
biological, lifestyle factors, as well as mental health assessed via relevant mental questionnaire 
and clinical diagnosis. In an online follow-up survey (2016–2017), 157366 participants completed 
additional mental health assessments. Ethical approval for UKB was granted by its Ethics 
Committee, and informed written consent was obtained from every participant. 
In this study, we included all working participants (employed or self-employed) who were not 
working remotely, and had available baseline data on commuting mode (N = 264158, 52.6%), 
distributed across 17977 small-area neighborhoods, corresponding to lower-layer super output

areas (LSOAs). First, we used this cohort (N = 264158) to assess whether participants who used 
active mobility at baseline had lower risk of prevalent anxiety or depression, compared to they 
would have had if they had used motorized commuting. Second, among individuals without 
records of mental disorders at baseline who also reported mental health status in the 2016–2017 
follow-up survey (N = 88915), we evaluated whether engaging in active mobility at baseline was 
related to a lower probability of incident anxiety or depression at follow-up, compared with if they 
had used motorized travel. Third, among individuals without records of baseline mental disorders 
and with linked medical records available (N = 240584), we assessed effects of active mobility on 
incident common mental disorders identified from linked electronic health records (EHR) during 
continuous follow-up from 2006 through 2020. 
Measurement of treatment 
Transport mode for commuting was the treatment variable in this study. At baseline, participants 
were asked about their commuting mode and were allowed to choose multiple modes, namely 
private motor vehicle, walking, cycling, and public transit. These responses were classified into 
three commuting categories in this study: “active” for walking or cycling, “motorized” for private 
motor vehicle and/or public transit exclusively, and “mixed” for all other combinations. Active 
mobility (also referred to interchangeably as “active transport” or “active travel”) or mixed-mode 
travel were the treated groups, with active mobility as the primary treatment of interest. Motorized 
travel served as the reference (control) group throughout. 
Measurement of health outcomes 
The health outcomes were symptom-based measures of probable depression and anxiety derived 
from mental health surveys at baseline and online follow-up, and broader common mental 
disorders (CMDs) based on clinical diagnoses from linked EHR.  
At baseline, participants reported the frequency of four symptoms, namely low mood, reduced 
interest/enthusiasm, tension/restlessness, and fatigue/lethargy, each rated from “not at all” to 
“nearly every day”, coded as 0 to 3. The first two questions correspond to the PHQ-2 depression 
screener and the remaining two correspond to the GAD-2 anxiety screener. A summed score ≥3 
across questions 1–2 indicated probable major depression, and a summed score ≥3 across 
questions 3–4 indicated probable generalized anxiety disorder (hereafter referred to as baseline 
depression and baseline anxiety) (42, 43). 
At the 2016–2017 online follow-up, anxiety and depressive symptoms were measured with 
validated GAD-7 and PHQ-9 questionnaires, respectively. GAD-7 comprises 7 questions assessing 
generalised anxiety symptoms during the preceding two weeks (e.g., restlessness, inability to 
control or excessive worry, difficulty relaxing, irritability, feeling nervous, and fear that something 
awful might happen), each scored from “not at all” to “nearly every day”, coded as 0 to 3. There 
are 9 questions in PHQ-9, which examines the frequency of depressive symptoms across the same 
timeframe (e.g., anhedonia, low mood, sleep, energy, appetite, concentration, psychomotor change,

and self-harm thoughts), using the same 0–3 scale. A total score ≥10 on the GAD-7 or PHQ-9 
indicated probable generalized anxiety disorder or major depression (44, 45), respectively 
(hereafter follow-up anxiety and follow-up depression). Details of questionnaires are provided in 
Supplementary Table S3. 
Using linked inpatient hospital admissions and primary care (GP) records, we defined CMDs as 
any diagnosis with ICD-10 codes F32–F33 and F40–F44, encompassing depressive, anxiety, 
stress-related, and related non-psychotic disorders. For EHR-based analyses, follow-up time was 
measured from baseline to the first recorded CMD diagnosis or censoring on 1 January 2020, 
whichever came first. 
For both prospective cohort analyses (online follow-up and clinical diagnosis from EHR), 
participants with any recorded mental disorder before baseline were excluded. 
Measurement of neighborhood environments 
Grounded in a socio-ecological framework, we conceptualized neighborhood environments as 
three interrelated layers, namely the natural, physical, and social environments, which can 
influence the psychological outcomes of active mobility (46-48). Drawing on this framework and 
prior evidence (32, 33, 49-53), we selected several key variables within each layer that capture the 
restorative, stress-buffering, and social-engagement potential of daily urban mobility. These 
variables included: percentage of green space, annual mean levels of fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅, 
µg/m³) and nitrogen dioxide (NO₂, µg/m³), neighborhood deprivation, and neighborhood safety. 
Greenspace percentage was measured within a 1-km network-based buffer centred on each 
individual’s residential location. Air pollutant concentrations were assigned to residential location 
using ESCAPE land use regression (LUR) models. Neighborhood deprivation was defined using 
the Townsend deprivation index, and neighborhood safety was defined using a crime score 
capturing social stability and safety, both at the small-area geographic scale (LSOA). Data sources 
are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. 
To synthesize these variables, we developed a neighborhood environmental supportiveness score 
capturing how well each participant’s surrounding neighborhood supports the realization of 
psychological benefits from active mobility. Each variable was transformed into standardized 
deciles (1–10) and aligned so that higher values indicate greater supportiveness (green space 
retained its natural ordering; PM₂.₅, NO₂, deprivation, and crime were reverse-coded). The five 
deciles were summed to generate an overall score between 5 (least supportive) and 50 (most 
supportive). We refer to this measure as transport-health environmental supportiveness (THES), 
hereafter environmental supportiveness. This framing aligns with the several United Nations SDGs, 
including SDG 3, 10, 11, and 16 for health promotion, sustainable urban development, inequality 
reduction, and safer neighborhoods.

Measurement of compact urban form 
Compact urban form was captured by functional urban compactness derived from the UKBUMP 
built environment database (54), measured within a 1-km network-network buffer surrounding 
each sample’s residential location. The composite measure incorporated four density metrics, 
namely public transport, street movement, retail, and residential, to capture the intensity and 
accessibility of urban activity. Each component was converted to standardized deciles and the four 
deciles were then combined into an overall score between 4 (lowest compactness) and 40 (highest 
compactness). Metric definitions and data sources are summarized in Supplementary Table S2 and 
described in previous literature (54). 
Measurement of genetic susceptibility 
To evaluate whether genetic susceptibility to mental disorders modifies treatment effects of active 
mobility, we estimated polygenic risk scores (PRS) for anxiety, depression, and CMDs. PRS were 
derived based on the genetic architecture from established genome-wide association (GWAS) 
studies (55, 56). Because CMDs reflect shared genetic liability across depression and anxiety 
phenotypes, we approximated genetic susceptibility to CMDs by combining the z-standardized 
depression and anxiety PRS (57). Specifically, we retained SNPs meeting imputation quality 
thresholds (INFO > 0.8) and minor allele frequency above 0.01, excluding mismatched, duplicated, 
and strand-ambiguous variants. PRS were derived for each UKB participant by applying the 
GWAS summary statistics to the UKB genotype database using PLINK software (v1.9) (58) and 
analyses were controlled for sex and the first five genomic principal components.  
Statistical analysis 
Overall, we estimated treatment effects for each health outcome by comparing potential risks under 
active mobility versus motorized travel at baseline for all participants, using models tailored to 
each outcome type and time scale. Specifically, we estimated (i) probability of prevalent 
depression or anxiety at baseline; (ii) probability of incident depression or anxiety at the 2016–
2017 online follow-up; and (iii) the 10-year risk of clinically diagnosed CMDs from linked EHR. 
The study design and conceptual framework of this study are shown in Fig. 1.  
Prediction models for treatment effects 
While treatment effects cannot be established with certainty from observational data, we assume 
that bias arising from nonrandom likelihood of engaging in active mobility can be addressed 
through controlling for baseline characteristics, thereby improving comparability between active 
and motorized travelers (59, 60). This identification assumption was discussed in Supplementary 
Method 2.   
We estimated average treatment effects (ATE) and individual treatment effects (ITEs) of active 
mobility on health outcomes using a causal framework that combines a propensity model and an 
outcome model. Specifically, we applied a propensity approach to derive each participant’s 
propensity score, defined as the likelihood of receiving the observed treatment, and constructed

Preprint version. This manuscript is under journal review. 
 
 
inverse probability weights to balance baseline characteristics between participants who engaged 
in active mobility and those who did not (61). This score was generated using a stacked machine 
learning (ML) method, which combined multiple base learners to provide the optimal ensemble 
prediction (62). Our stacked ensemble drew on multiple base learners, namely, random forests, 
extreme gradient boosting, regularized regression, regression splines, and a main term regression, 
to capture both linear and nonlinear relationships. For symptom-based outcomes at baseline and 
online follow-up, a separate stacked ML algorithm with the same set of base learners was used to 
predict outcome risk under each travel mode. For EHR-based CMD outcomes with varying follow-
up durations, we used a deep survival model similar to DeepSurv to estimate the 10-year risk of 
incident CMDs under active versus counterfactual motorized travel (63). Five-fold cross-
validation was used to fit these models. More model details are provided in Supplementary Method 
2, Fig. S1. 
Treatment effects (TEs) of active mobility were reported as relative risk (RR), derived for per 
individual as the ratio of predicted outcome risk under active mobility to the predicted risk under 
the counterfactual motorized travel. RR > 1 indicates that higher risks under active mobility than 
motorized travel (smaller health benefits), and RR < 1 indicates lower risks under active mobility 
(greater benefits). When RR of ITE is smaller than RR of ATE, individual health benefit are higher 
than the population average, we refer to it as an enhanced effect, otherwise, it is considered a 
diminished effect. 
ITEs, neighborhood environments, and urban compactness 
We characterized dose-response associations between ITEs of active mobility and environmental 
supportiveness by fitting restricted cubic spline regressions using five Harrell-recommended knot 
locations (5th, 27.5th, 50th, 72.5th, and 95th percentiles of THES). Differences in distribution of 
ITEs across THES levels were evaluated with Kruskal-Wallis test. Additionally, associations 
between each THES components and the primary outcomes from symptom-based follow-up were 
estimated. 
We then estimated the relationships between ITEs and urban compactness separately within “more 
supportive” and “less supportive” environments, dichotomized by the median THES. We evaluated 
whether environmental supportiveness may modify the association of urban compactness with 
ITEs by fitting restricted cubic spline models including terms for THES, compactness, and their 
interaction. Finally, we mapped the spatial distribution of ITEs of active mobility and 
corresponding THES and urban compactness, aggregated on local authority district level, to 
evaluate the spatial patterns.  
ITEs, neighborhood environments, and genetics 
To assess the modification effects of individual genetic predisposition to mental disorders on the 
mental-health benefits of active mobility, we conducted three complementary analyses. First, we 
compared PRS between participants with higher versus lower ITEs (median split) and quantified 
the effect sizes using Cohen’s d. Second, we examined whether the distribution of ITE quartiles

varied across PRS strata (categorized into nine equal-sized groups). Third, we assessed effect 
modification by genetic risk by fitting models with THES, PRS group, and interaction between 
them to determine whether the ITE–THES association differed between high- and low-PRS 
participants. Details of the statistical tests are provided in Supplementary Method 3. 
Covariates 
All analyses were adjusted for confounding variables in three categories, selected based on a 
review of prior studies (7, 26, 64). These included sociodemographic (i.e., age, sex, ethnicity, 
education, household income, occupation, neighborhood deprivation, area of residence), health-
related behaviors (i.e., body mass index, occupational physical activity, travel mode for leisure, 
leisure moderate physical activity, time spent walking for pleasure, smoking habit, frequency of 
alcohol intake), and baseline comorbidity status (i.e., angina, myocardial infarction, hypertension, 
stroke, diabetes, cancer, and other major conditions).  
Sensitivity analyses 
Several sensitivity and robustness analyses were conducted. First, to mitigate potential reverse 
causation, we re-estimated treatment effects on CMDs from EHR after excluding events occurring 
within the first two years after baseline. Second, we randomly permuted treatment assignment for 
follow-up anxiety and depression to verify that estimates centered around null under random 
assignment and were no longer statistically significant (p-value > 0.05). Third, we evaluated 
sensitivity by introducing a random covariate into follow-up mental health models and examining 
whether estimates changed. Fourth, we repeated the analyses in an 80% subsample for baseline 
anxiety and depression and compared the resulting treatment effects with the main results to assess 
the stability of the estimates. Finally, we assessed consistency of findings across all mental health 
measures derived from different sources and time points.
