Abstract
We study the fundamental problem of fairly dividing indivisible items among agents with
additive utilities. In our model, an item can be a good yielding non-negative utilities to some
agents and simultaneously a chore yielding negative utilities to others. We take the best-of-
both-worlds perspective and our goal is to construct a randomized allocation that is exactly
fair ex ante while also being supported on ex post approximately fair allocations. The fair-
ness notions examined in this paper are envy-freeness (EF) and its well-known relaxation envy-
freeness up to one item (EF1). Our main result is that ex-ante EF and ex-post EF1 can be achieved
simultaneously. To achieve this, we introduce a novel probabilistic Hall-type matrix decom-
position that intricately correlates the fractional assignments of goods and chores. We resolve
this decomposition problem by combining continuous minimax duality—via Sion’s minimax
theorem—with carefully designed biased flow networks.

Introduction
The fair division of valuable scarce resources or undesirable burden among agents is a common
problem that arises frequently in society, and has attracted extensive and ongoing interest in the
fields of mathematics, economics, operations research, and computer science [Brams and Taylor,
1996; Brandt et al., 2016; Moulin, 2003, 2019; Rothe, 2024]. The quintessential notion of fairness
is arguably envy-freeness (EF), which stipulates that each agent values their own allocated bundle
weakly more than any other bundle in the allocation [Foley, 1967]. In other words, each agent
does not envy any other agent.
In this paper, we are concerned with the fundamental problem of fairly dividing indivisible
items. Due to its potential to capture a wide range of real-world applications, indivisible-items al-
location has received significant attention in recent years, with focus mainly on allocating (purely)
goods [Amanatidis et al., 2023] or (purely) chores [Guo et al., 2023]. Note that goods (resp., chores)
are items yielding non-negative (resp., negative) utilities to the agents. In addition to theoretical
research, the developed algorithms have already been deployed in real life and made accessible to
the public through web applications; see, e.g., Course Match [Budish et al., 2017], Spliddit [Gold-
man and Procaccia, 2015] and Kajibuntan [Igarashi and Yokoyama, 2023].
We focus on a more general model that allows agents to report more expressive utilities over
the set of indivisible items to be divided. More specifically, in our model of mixed goods and
chores, an indivisible item can be a good to some agents and simultaneously a chore to other

agents. Use cases include household chore division between couples or tenants, the division of
assets and liabilities between multiple parties, etc. In our first example, an agent may want to
avoid cooking, while another agent may find cooking is desirable and relaxing. Similarly, in our
second example, while some stakeholders seek risky assets as they have the potential to yield high
returns, others may be risk-averse and would like to receive low-risk assets.
When items are indivisible, envy-freeness is too demanding to achieve, as envy-free allocations
may not always exist. One common way to circumvent this issue is to weaken envy-freeness. For
instance, envy-freeness up to one item (EF1) is a very well-known relaxation of envy-freeness, which
was first introduced in the goods setting [Budish, 2011; Lipton et al., 2004] and later generalized to
the mixed goods and chores setting [Aziz et al., 2022]. An allocation of indivisible items is said to
be EF1 if any envy an agent has towards another agent can be eliminated when we (hypothetically)
remove a chore from the envious agent’s bundle or a good from the envied agent’s bundle. With a
mix of goods and chores, an EF1 allocation always exists when agents have additive utilities [Aziz
et al., 2022; Bhaskar et al., 2021].
Another natural and common alternative to achieve fairness is to use randomization, with
which we can specify a probability distribution (i.e., lottery) over a set of deterministic allocations.
Randomization has been used in a variety of contexts such as assignment problem [Bogomolnaia
and Moulin, 2001], apportionment [Gölz et al., 2026; Grimmett, 2004], collective choice [Bogomol-
naia et al., 2005; Brandl et al., 2021], and resource allocation [Budish et al., 2013]. With the power
of randomization, it is easy to achieve ex ante envy-freeness, e.g., via the following procedure: we
choose a single agent uniformly at random and then allocate all of the items to the agent. It can be
seen that no agent envies another in expectation, although each realized deterministic allocation
introduces a large amount of envy ex post.
In the work of Freeman et al. [2020], they focused on the goods setting, and initiated the study
of constructing a randomized allocation (i.e., a probabilistic distribution over deterministic allo-
cations) that is exactly fair ex ante (before the randomness is realized) and approximately fair
ex post (after the randomness is realized). This “best of both worlds (BoBW)” approach has at-
tracted an active line of research. Among other results, Freeman et al. showed that for additive
utilities, ex-ante EF and ex-post EF1 are compatible. Put differently, there always exists a random-
ized allocation that is envy-free in expectation and can be decomposed into a set of deterministic
allocations, all of which satisfy EF1. This motivates a natural question in our setting:1
With a set of mixed goods and chores and agents having additive utilities, can we achieve
simultaneously ex-ante EF and ex-post EF1?
1.1
Our Result and Technical Overview
We settle the above question in the affirmative.
Theorem 1.1. Under the mixed goods and chores setting with additive valuations, ex-ante EF and ex-post
EF1 are compatible.
As we will describe shortly in Section 1.2, a majority of prior fair division research on best-
of-both-worlds fairness is concerned with only goods. Moreover, with appropriate and straight-
forward adaptation, the ex-ante EF and ex-post EF1 BoBW result for the goods setting continue
to hold for the chores settings [see, pp. 1685 of Aziz et al., 2024a]. However, showing ex-ante EF
is compatible with ex-post EF1 for mixed goods and chores becomes tricky and calls for novel
conceptual ideas and mathematical techniques, which may be of independent interest.

1.1.1
Technical Challenges
It is known that the Probabilistic Serial (PS) lottery and its variant, the recursive Probabilistic Serial
lottery, can achieve both ex-ante EF and ex-post EF1 when the items are goods only. In our setting
with mixed goods and chores, both PS-lottery and recursive PS-lottery can be extended to handle
the following two special cases:
1. Each item is valued non-negatively by at least one agent (we call such an item a subjective
good).
2. Each item is valued negatively by all agents (we call such an item an objective chore).
We will discuss these in detail in Section 2.1.
Since every item is either a subjective good or an objective chore, a naive approach to solving
the general case is to independently run known best-of-both-worlds mechanisms (such as the
Probabilistic Serial (PS) or recursive PS rule) for both the set of subjective goods and the set of
objective chores. However, this only yields ex-ante EF and ex-post EF2.2
To improve the ex-post guarantee to EF1, the lotteries for subjective goods and objective chores
must be intricately correlated. This is the main technical challenge for our problem. To overcome
this challenge, we introduce many mathematical techniques that have not been used in the fair di-
vision literature before. Notably, we derive a novel probabilistic Hall-type matrix decomposition
theorem.

Proof Sketch for Theorem 1.1
Our algorithmic framework accomplishes this in three parts: a bundling preprocessing phase,
followed by two distinct algorithmic approaches depending on the number of remaining objective
chores.
Bundling Preprocessing (Section 3).
As mentioned before, items are partitioned into subjective
goods and objective chores. In the first step, we group items carefully such that each group is
viewed as a single “meta-item”. At the end, we reduce the original instance to an instance with
three crucial structural properties:
1. each subjective good has a non-empty interest set of agents who value it non-negatively;
2. the interest sets are pairwise disjoint, meaning that each agent values at most one subjective
good non-negatively (this implies no more than n subjective goods); and
3. each subjective good is chore-maximal, meaning that the addition of any objective chore ren-
ders its overall value strictly negative for all agents.
We then divide our analysis based on the number of remaining objective chores.
The Hard Case: More than n Objective Chores (Section 4).
When the number of objective
chores strictly exceeds the number of agents n, we explicitly define a target fractional allocation
that is ex-ante EF. Subjective goods are divided equally among the agents in their respective in-
terest sets, while objective chores (padded with dummy items) are fractionally allocated using the
recursive PS rule.
The primary technical hurdle is decomposing this combined fractional allocation into a lottery
over integral allocations that strictly satisfy ex-post EF1. We introduce a synchronized rounding
scheme: an agent can be allocated a subjective good only if she is simultaneously assigned an
objective chore (rather than a dummy item) in the first round of the recursive PS decomposition,
or if no agent in their interest set receives a chore in that round. Due to the chore-maximality
property, the disutility of the assigned chore outweighs the value of the assigned subjective good
in every agent’s perspective. This makes the ex-post fairness analysis similar to the case of chores-
only instances, and it ensures ex-post EF1.
The remaining difficulty is to show that such a correlated decomposition exists while preserv-
ing the ex-ante marginal probability. For this, we impose a probabilistic Hall-type condition on
the support of the first-round objective chore matchings. We prove that if this condition holds,
then subjective goods can be successfully allocated to satisfy both ex-ante and ex-post constraints
via a max-flow min-cut argument. Proving the existence of a first-round chore decomposition
that satisfies this Hall-type condition is the most technical part of our paper, which is stated in
Section 1.1.2.
The Easy Case: At Most n Objective Chores (Section 5).
When there are at most n objective
chores, we apply a distinct randomized combinatorial algorithm.
We first execute a secondary preprocessing phase to ensure that the subjective goods are
good-minimal, which guarantees that any ex-post EF1 condition satisfied by removing a bundled-
subjective good is inherently satisfied by removing a subjective good from the original instance,
and the instance satisfies a small-goods-only property, i.e., the disutility of any objective chore
strictly outweighs the total utility of all subjective goods an agent values non-negatively. Next,
we design a three-step randomized lottery:

1. We uniformly at random sample a subset of agents (equal to the number of chores) and
assign exactly one objective chore to each.
2. We run a serial dictatorship among these selected agents to distribute any subjective goods
they value non-negatively.
3. Finally, the remaining agents (who received no chores) divide the leftover subjective goods
via a standard PS-lottery padded with dummy items.
Ex-post EF1 follows naturally from the small-goods-only and good-minimality property. Ex-
ante EF is established by employing a delicate coupling argument and pairing symmetric outcomes
of the lottery, such as swapping the roles or selection orders of two agents.

Related Work
The literature of fair division has been developed rapidly [see, e.g., the surveys of Amanatidis
et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Nguyen and Rothe, 2023; Suksompong, 2021, 2025].
We will present here the most relevant papers to our work. We first summarize the development
of fairly allocating mixed goods and chores, followed by the best-of-both-worlds fairness results.
Fair Division of Mixed Goods and Chores
Mixed Indivisible Goods and Chores. An EF1 allocation always exists [Aziz et al., 2022; Bhaskar
et al., 2021]. The problem becomes trickier if we ask for both EF1 and the economic efficiency
notion Pareto optimality (PO), and it remains open to date.3 By introducing other envy-freeness
relaxations, Barman et al. [2025] and Barman and Verma [2026] showed that their proposed envy-
freeness relaxations are compatible with PO.
When weakening EF1 to proportionality up to one item (PROP1), for additive utilities, a PROP1
and PO allocation can be computed in strongly polynomial time, even if agents have asymmetric
weights [Aziz et al., 2020].
The existence and computation of other fairness notions, such as envy-freeness up to any item
(EFX), maximin share (MMS) fairness and relaxations of equitability, as well as their compatibility
with PO, have also been studied [e.g., Hosseini and Sethia, 2025; Hosseini et al., 2023; Kulkarni
et al., 2021a,b; Livanos et al., 2022]. A more detailed account of these works can be found in the
overview of Liu et al. [2024, Section 4].
Mixed Divisible Goods and Chores (i.e., Mixed Manna). Bogomolnaia et al. [2017] initiated the
study of dividing a mix of (homogeneous) divisible goods and chores (i.e., mixed manna) and
focused on an economic concept called competitive equilibrium. They established the existence of
competitive equilibria. Follow-up research has then devoted to studying the computation of an
equilibrium [Chaudhury et al., 2023; Garg et al., 2023].
Mixed Indivisible Goods and Chores plus a Cake. Most recently, Aziz et al. [2026] studied a
more general model in which a heterogeneous divisible cake and a set of mixed indivisible goods
and chores are divided among agents who have additive utilities over the resources. They inves-
tigated an envy-freeness relaxation called envy-freeness for mixed resources (EFM), which combines
EF and EF1 in a natural way. Aziz et al. showed that an EFM allocation always exists in the
aforementioned setting.

Preliminaries
For any positive integer k, let [k] := {1, 2, . . . , k}. Denote by N = [n] the set of n agents and
M = [m] the set of m indivisible items. Each agent i ∈N has a valuation function vi : {0, 1}M →R,
where vi(∅) = 0. Throughout the paper, we assume each agent’s valuation function is additive,
that is, vi(S) = ∑h∈S vi({h}) for S ⊆M. For simplicity, we use vi(h) to denote vi({h}).
Under the setting with both goods and chores, we classify item h as an objective good if vi(h) ≥0
for all agents i ∈N, and an objective chore if vi(h) < 0 for all agents i ∈N. We classify item h as a

Allocation.
A fractional allocation is specified by a non-negative n × m matrix X = (Xih)i∈N,h∈M,
where Xih ∈[0, 1] denotes the fraction of item h being allocated to agent i, and ∑i∈N Xih = 1 for
each item h ∈M. Each row Xi = (Xih)h∈M represents the fractional allocation of agent i. Each
agent i’s utility for Xi is naturally extended as vi(Xi) = ∑h∈M Xih · vi(h).
An integral allocation is a fractional allocation X = (Xih)i∈N,h∈M with Xih ∈{0, 1} for all i ∈N
and h ∈M. For simplicity, we denote an integral allocation by A = (A1, . . . , An), where A is a
partition of M and Ai is the bundle assigned to agent i.
A randomized allocation, also called a lottery of integral allocations, is a probability distribution
over integral allocations {(pk, Ak)}k∈[K], where, for every k ∈[K], Ak is an integral allocation
implemented with probability pk ∈[0, 1], and ∑k∈[K] pk = 1. We say that randomized allocation
{(pk, Ak)}k∈[K] implements a fractional allocation X, or {(pk, Ak)}k∈[K] is a decomposition of X, if
for each agent i ∈N and each item h ∈M, Xih is the marginal probability that h is allocated to
agent i, that is, Xih = ∑k∈[K] pk · I[h ∈Ak

A randomized allocation is said to satisfy some ex-ante property if the fractional allocation it
implements satisfies this property, and is said to satisfy some ex-post property if every integral
allocation in its support satisfies this property.
Fairness Notion.
One of the most natural fairness notions is envy-freeness, which requires that
no agent envies any other agent.
Definition 2.1. A fractional allocation X satisfies envy-freeness (EF), if vi(Xi) ≥vi(Xj) for any pair
of agents i and j.
For integral allocations, envy-freeness cannot always be guaranteed. We therefore consider its
standard relaxation, which allows envy to be eliminated by the removal of one item.
Definition 2.2. An integral allocation A satisfies envy-freeness up to one item (EF1), if for any pair
of agents i and j, either of the following holds:
• i does not envy j: vi(Ai) ≥vi(Aj), or
• there exists h ∈Ai ∪Aj such that vi(Ai \ {h}) ≥vi(Aj \ {h}).
Alternatively, an integral allocation A is EF1 if, for any pair of agents i and j, either of the
following holds:
• vi(Ai) ≥vi(Aj \ {h}) for some h ∈Aj with vi(h) ≥0, or
• vi(Ai \ {h}) ≥vi(Aj) for some h ∈Ai with vi(h) < 0.

PS rule.
Below, we describe the probabilistic serial (PS) rule by Bogomolnaia and Moulin [2001]
in the context of divisible objective goods. Starting at time t = 0, each agent simultaneously “eats”
their favorite available item at a unit speed. If multiple agents prefer the same item, it is consumed
collectively at a rate equal to the number of agents eating it. Once an item is fully depleted, the
agents consuming it immediately move to their next most-preferred available item. This process
continues until all items are consumed, by which time t = m/n.
It is easy to see that the fractional allocation output by the PS rule is envy-free.

hi,t. Then, for any two agents i, j and any t1 < t2, we have vi(hi,t1) ≥vi(hj,t2) since agent i values
each item in Mi,t1 weakly higher than each item in Mj,t2 due to the property of PS (that items with
higher values are consumed first). Thus, agent i will not envy agent j if hj,1 were removed from
agent j’s bundle.
Recursive PS-lottery.
Prior to Aziz et al. [2024a], Freeman et al. [2020] introduces the recursive
PS-lottery, which also admits an interpretation as a simultaneously “eating” process at a unit
speed. In contrast to the PS-lottery, recursive PS proceeds for T discrete rounds (again, we as-
sume T ∈Z≥0). In each round, each agent will consume their most-preferred available item for
one unit of time, resulting in a partial fractional allocation. This partial fractional allocation will be
immediately decomposed into a lottery of partial integral allocations where each agent receives
exactly one item from the set of items she has (possibly partially) consumed in this round. When

2024a].

multiple lotteries exist, we may choose any of them. Under each partial integral allocation, the
process is then recursively executed for another unit of time among the unallocated items. The
process terminates after all items are allocated, i.e., after T rounds. In contrast to the PS-lottery
where we first complete the PS rule and then decompose the obtained fractional allocation, the re-
cursive PS-lottery alternately performs the eating (PS for one unit of time) and the decomposition
steps in every unit of time.
The recursive PS-lottery is also ex-ante EF and ex-post EF1. It is ex-ante EF since EF holds for
the partial fractional allocation in each time unit. By the nature of the recursive PS-lottery, for each
agent, items with higher values are consumed first. Therefore, the ex-post EF1 property holds for
the same reason as it is in PS-lottery.
For both PS-lottery and recursive PS-lottery, we have shown the following proposition (which
will be used later) for each realized integral allocation.
Proposition 2.3 (Aziz et al. [2024a], Freeman et al. [2020]). Fix an integral allocation in the PS-lottery
or the recursive PS-lottery. Let the item received by agent i in round t be hi,t. Then, for any two agents i, j
and any two rounds t1 < t2, we have vi(hi,t1) ≥vi(hj,t2).
Extensions beyond objective goods.
Both of the above rules can be extended to the setting with
only subjective goods or with only objective chores to get ex-ante EF and ex-post EF1 guarantee.
In an instance that only contains subjective goods where each item is non-negatively valued by at
least one agent, we can apply either rule after adding a sufficiently large number of dummy items,
each of which has value 0 to each agent, to M, so that each agent will not consume any item with
negative value. The ex-ante EF property holds for the same reason, and each integral allocation is
ex-post EF1 as envy from agent i to agent j will be eliminated by removing the item received in
the first round in the integral allocation by j.
In an instance that only contains objective chores, applying either rule results in an ex-ante EF
lottery for the same reason, and ex-post EF1 is satisfied as envy from agent i to j will be eliminated
by removing the item received in the last round by i.
For our setting with both subjective goods G and objective chores Z, each rule satisfies ex-ante
EF and ex-post EF2. Here, EF2 is a weaker notion than EF1, which allows envy to be eliminated
by the removal of up to two items. In particular, we can execute PS or recursive PS on G and Z
separately as mentioned above, and then combine the lotteries. Denote the fractional allocations
implemented on G and Z as XG and XZ respectively, then the combined fractional allocation is
naturally defined by setting Xih = XG

Discussion
In this paper, we have studied the problem of fairly allocating a mix of goods and chores among
agents who have additive utilities over the set of indivisible items. We show that there always
exists a randomized allocation (i.e., a lottery over deterministic allocation) that simultaneously
satisfies ex-ante EF and ex-post EF1.
All components of our construction can be implemented in polynomial time except for com-
puting the probabilistic Hall-type decomposition in Section 4.1. A direct implementation that ex-
plicitly enumerates the relevant matchings and constraints gives an exponential-time algorithm.
On the other hand, the decomposition’s minimax formulation and its polynomial-time network-
flow subproblem for each fixed weight vector suggest that an oracle-based or more compact im-
plementation may yield a polynomial-time algorithm. Establishing such an implementation is
beyond the scope of this work, whose focus is the compatibility of the ex-ante and ex-post fairness
guarantees.
An interesting direction is to explore further avenues for applying our techniques. Given the
generality of the probabilistic Hall-type decomposition, we believe our framework has additional
applications both within fair division and beyond. In particular, since the decomposition allows
us to choose an arbitrary set and bound the concentration of matchings over any of its subsets, it
has potential applications to a wide range of correlated matching problems.
Our work focuses exclusively on fairness. In future research, an immediate next step could be
understanding the interplay between fairness and economic efficiency and charting the boundary
between impossibilities and positive results when applying the BoBW approach. Another inter-
esting direction is to consider agents’ utility functions beyond additive utilities. Nevertheless, a
solid understanding for the goods- and chores-only settings would be the first step. Going beyond
additive utilities, it is not known whether ex-ante EF and ex-post EF1 are compatible or not even
for the goods-only or chores-only setting.
It would also be interesting to consider unequal entitlements of the agents and study the
weighted setting of mixed goods and chores. As discussed in Section 1.2, for goods- and chores-
only settings, weighted EF is compatible with a version of its relaxation. Even considering only
ex-post fairness in the weighted setting, it is intriguing if weighted EF1 (with proper adaptation
for mixed goods and chores) or its relaxation can always be satisfied [Suksompong, 2025, Open
problem 8].
