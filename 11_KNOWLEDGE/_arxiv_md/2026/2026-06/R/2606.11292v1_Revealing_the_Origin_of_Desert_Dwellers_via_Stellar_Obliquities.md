ABSTRACT
Observations suggest that the hot Neptune desert contains the remnants of destroyed gas giants.
Recent theoretical work has shown that gas giant destruction via Roche lobe overflow (RLO) can indeed
populate the desert with remnant planets, but only if mass transfer removes most of the planet’s orbital
angular momentum (“lossy” RLO). Motivated by the fact that stellar accretion naturally gives rise to
such lossy RLO, in this Letter we examine how planet-to-star mass and angular momentum transfer
manifests in the distribution of stellar obliquities. We find that RLO tilts host stars into spin/orbit
alignment (within a few ∼tens of degrees) regardless of initial conditions. Obliquity damping by RLO
can only be reversed by the presence of misaligned companion planets within ≲2 au. While tides and
mass transfer usually produce stellar spin up, host stars can also emerge from RLO slowly rotating
if systems begin strongly retrograde; retrograde RLO reconciles theory with the anomalously slow
rotation of the desert dweller host, LTT 9779. Predicted spin/orbit alignment may differentiate RLO
from alternative giant planet destruction mechanisms, in particular hot Jupiter disruption during high
eccentricity migration (which tends to produce broadly distributed stellar obliquities). We summarize
other population-level predictions that can further distinguish RLO from high eccentricity migration.
Our work suggests that follow-up obliquity measurements may reveal the formation pathways of desert
dwellers, and potentially open a window into gas giants’ exposed interiors.
1. INTRODUCTION
The Kepler spacecraft revealed that hot Jupiters
(≳8 R⊕, ≳100 M⊕) and super-Earths/sub-Neptunes
(≲4 R⊕, ≲10 M⊕) inside orbital periods ≲3 days are
wedged apart by a dearth of mid-sized planets (e.g.
Szab´o & Kiss 2011; Beaug´e & Nesvorn´y 2013).
The
census of planets surrounding this “sub-Jovian desert”
has recently been brought under new light by TESS
(Ricker et al. 2015, see e.g. Jenkins et al. 2020; Arm-
strong et al. 2020). In contrast to Kepler-era estimates
that the desert was virtually empty (hence the name
“desert”; e.g. Szab´o & Kiss 2011; Mazeh et al. 2016),
TESS has revealed a number of planets residing squarely
in this region previously thought uninhabitable (“desert
dwellers”; Vissapragada & Behmard 2025; Doyle et al.
2025); though sparsely populated, ultrahot mid-sized
planets do exist. The goal of this paper is to motivate
and guide observations that may elucidate the origin of
these unexpected desert dwellers.

The sub-Jovian desert records the migratory and mass
loss histories of the hottest exoplanets. Opening inward
from ∼3 days toward smaller masses/radii, the desert’s

(2022), though see Kurokawa & Nakamoto (2014) for a
differing perspective). In-situ formation of hot Jupiters
could also help carve the desert’s upper boundary, with
tidal orbital decay setting the innermost edge of the hot
Jupiter population (Bailey & Batygin 2018).

In order for hot Jupiter RLO to successfully reproduce
desert dwellers, Hallatt & Millholland (2026a) found
that mass transfer must be “lossy”: most of the or-
bital angular momentum carried by material leaving the
planet cannot be returned to the orbit.
This “lossy”
RLO contrasts with classical mass transfer theory, in
which most of the donor’s orbital angular momentum
is returned via torque from an accretion disk (e.g. Ver-
bunt 1982; Verbunt & Rappaport 1988).
As we will
show, lossy RLO naturally arises due to stellar mag-
netospheric accretion of angular momentum, preventing
any back-reaction torque on the planet. In this Letter
we clarify how lossy RLO driven by stellar accretion of
angular momentum and mass can be tested observation-
ally and differentiated from other theories. We argue
that lossy RLO makes a clean prediction for the distri-
bution of stellar obliquities (the angle between stellar
spin and orbital angular momenta; Albrecht et al. 2022)
in the sub-Jovian desert. Specifically, we find that an-
gular momentum/mass transfer robustly tilts host stars
into alignment with planetary orbits (within a few ∼tens
of degrees).
Predicted spin/orbit alignment can differentiate RLO
from other mechanisms populating the desert. The chief
alternative mechanism that may conceivably populate
the desert is tidal disruption during high eccentricity mi-
gration (HEM), whereby dynamical tides excited in the
planet at periapse drive catastrophic mass loss (Guil-
lochon et al. 2011; Liu et al. 2013, though see Wel-
don et al. 2025 for further discussion of how violent
this process may be), leaving behind a remnant core
due to its higher density. If hot Jupiters are destroyed
during HEM, we may expect their vestiges to inherit
broadly-distributed obliquities produced during HEM
(e.g. via von Zeipel/Lidov/Kozai oscillations; Fabrycky
& Tremaine 2007; Storch et al. 2014). The obliquity pre-
dictions reported in this work therefore serve as strong
motivation for follow-up observations of desert dwellers
to test the Hallatt & Millholland (2026a) theory.
Our goal of providing clean obliquity predictions to
test the origin of desert dwellers is akin to ongoing ef-
forts focused on planets beyond the desert (in the so-
called “ridge”; e.g. Bourrier et al. 2023; Attia et al. 2023;
Louden & Millholland 2024; Yee et al. 2025). To the
best of our knowledge, there are currently no published
obliquity (projected or true) measurements for planets
in the sub-Jovian desert sample of Hallatt & Millholland
(2026a). It is our hope that this paper provides moti-
vation to extend the census of close-in planet obliquities
into the desert.
This Letter is organized as follows. Section 2 estab-
lishes that stellar spin and hot Jupiter orbital angular
momenta are comparable, indicating that angular mo-
mentum/mass transfer during RLO can strongly influ-
ence the rate and direction of stellar spin (and therefore

3, before outlining how stellar obliquities are tilted into

rameters to help guide observations. Sections 6 and 7
summarize and tabulate the ways that observations can
confirm the Hallatt & Millholland (2026a) picture. We
close in Section 8 with a brief recap of our results.

Another avenue to test the Hallatt & Millholland

convective envelopes and weaker magnetic braking than

hot stars with hot Jupiters.
Mirroring this trend of

frequent above the Kraft break; the sub-Jovian desert
should remain empty.
This expectation of an empty
desert above the Kraft break contrasts with the ex-
pectation from hot Jupiter destruction during HEM,
where tidal disruption should occur indiscriminately
above/below the break.
The presence of nearby companion planets provides
yet another line of distinction. HEM is known to dis-

the HEM picture.

7. DISCUSSION
7.1. Alternative Formation Channels
Our paper has focused on how desert dweller for-
mation via lossy RLO can be distinguished from
HEM. One remaining possibility however is that forma-
tion/migration processes alternative to RLO/HEM are
responsible for populating the sub-Jovian desert. If al-
ternative formation routes are at play, spin/orbit align-
ment may not be a unique signature of RLO. Two al-
ternative formation routes that also produce spin/orbit
alignment are coplanar HEM (CHEM; Li et al. 2014;
Petrovich 2015) and disk migration (in disks aligned
with stellar spin; e.g. Goldreich & Tremaine 1979, 1980;
Lin & Papaloizou 1979). We address each of these al-
ternative pathways in turn.
While CHEM can indeed shrink periapse distances be-
low the tidal disruption limit, Petrovich (2015) found
that such destruction is rare and only occurred as a re-
sult of extreme initial conditions instead of secular evolu-
tion; disruption occurred in ∼0.1% of cases in their pop-
ulation synthesis, compared to the ∼3.1% of cases that
formed a hot Jupiter. Moreover, Petrovich (2015) find
CHEM produces hot Jupiters at a rate ∼5% per outer
cold Jupiter. Given that the fraction of disruptions per
hot Jupiter formed is ∼(0.1/3.1)∼0.03, the number of
disruptions per star is: ∼0.03×0.05 hot Jupiters/cold
Jupiter ×0.15 cold Jupiters/star (e.g. Cumming et al.
2008; Fulton et al. 2021) ∼2×10−4 disruptions/star,
i.e.
0.023% of stars should host a desert dweller if
they all form via CHEM. This rarity of destruction con-
trasts with the measured planet occurrence in the desert
of ∼10% that of hot Jupiters (occurrence ∼0.1%/star;
Cui et al. 2026). Nevertheless, the possibility of copla-
nar HEM can be ascertained for a given desert dweller
system by the presence of eccentric (e≳0.2), massive
(≳1 MJ) and distant (≳5 au) planet companions, as
predicted by Petrovich (2015).
While disk migration may deposit planets in the
desert’s orbital period range (e.g. Lee & Chiang 2017;
Hallatt & Lee 2020, assuming the star has shrunk
to ≲7R⊙, freeing up orbital periods ≲2 days), it is
unclear that disk migration is consistent with desert
dwellers’ puzzling features, namely, that desert dwellers
orbit stars with the same metal-rich distribution as hot
Jupiters (Vissapragada & Behmard 2025) and boast ex-
ceptionally high densities compared to planets beyond
the desert (Doyle et al. 2025).
As photoevaporation
alone cannot strip the atmosphere of a mid-sized desert
dweller (see e.g.
Figure 4 of Owen & Lai 2018, and
Ionov et al. 2018), disk migration is unlikely to allow
for enough mass loss to reproduce desert dwellers (un-
less their low gas fractions arise due to late-time core
formation; e.g. Lee 2019). We therefore consider con-
tamination of aligned desert dwellers via disk migration
a remote possibility.
Another alternative for aligned desert dwellers is that
they arise via mergers of coplanar smaller planets (e.g.
Liu et al. 2015; Chachan et al. 2025). Since hot small
planet occurrence correlates weakly with stellar metal-
licity (Petigura et al. 2018), such a “bottom-up” picture
may run counter to desert dwellers’ preference for metal-
rich stars (Vissapragada & Behmard 2025).
Whether
small planet multiplicity correlates with stellar metal-
licity remains poorly understood; if so, collisions could
indeed be consistent with desert dwellers’ preference for
metal-rich stars. Future work is needed to estimate how
small planet multiplicity scales with stellar metallicity.

8. CONCLUSION
Recent observational (Vissapragada & Behmard 2025)
and theoretical (Hallatt & Millholland 2026a) work sug-
gests that the sub-Jovian desert is populated with the
remnants of destroyed hot Jupiters. Here, we build on
the Hallatt & Millholland (2026a) picture by which hot
Jupiters’ stripped remains backfill the desert via “lossy”
Roche lobe overflow. Motivated by the fact that “lossy”
RLO naturally arises due to magnetospheric accretion of
angular momentum by the host star, our goal is to deter-
mine how mass/angular momentum transfer manifests
in the stellar obliquity distribution. Our results guide
observations that may test and differentiate the Hallatt
& Millholland (2026a) theory from others (chiefly, tidal
disruption during HEM). Our main conclusions are as
follows:
• Lossy RLO tilts host stars into spin/orbit align-
ment. Regardless of initial conditions and the de-
tails of angular momentum transport, post-mass
transfer obliquities cluster within ≲60◦.
• Spin/orbit alignment differentiates lossy RLO
from tidal disruption during HEM, since post-
HEM realignment is inefficient. Contrary to HEM,
RLO also predicts an empty sub-Jovian desert
around stars above the Kraft break, that desert
dwellers are ∼Gyrs old, and that they may harbor
nearby companions.
• Spin/orbit alignment produced by RLO can be
reversed after RLO by companion planets. Such
companions must lie within ≲2 au, and should be
misaligned with the desert dweller.
• Stars can emerge from RLO slowly rotating, but
only if the system begins strongly retrograde.
Slowly rotating host stars relieve tension between
top-down formation and the prototypical desert
dweller, LTT 9779 b.
• Surveys of nearby stars for ablating planets are
more likely to find evaporating hot Neptunes
than hot Jupiters actively undergoing catastrophic
RLO; nevertheless, such hot Jupiters should exist

A. IS “LOSSY” ROCHE LOBE OVERFLOW ADIABATIC?
The obliquity predictions made in this work rely on Hallatt & Millholland (2026a)’s “lossy” RLO mechanism to
destroy hot Jupiters and populate the sub-Jovian desert. To calculate the outcome of RLO, Hallatt & Millholland
(2026a) employed a streamlined methodology to couple planets’ structural/thermal/mass loss/orbital evolution (as
outlined in Hallatt & Millholland 2026b). These coupled structure/mass loss/orbital calculations are simplified in
the sense that the planet is assumed to remain in hydrostatic and thermal equilibrium at all times; this equilibrium
assumption enables one to follow planetary evolution during RLO using a single ordinary differential equation in planet
entropy (see Equation A1 below) rather than the coupled partial differential equations of planetary structure/evolution
(i.e. the stellar structure/evolution equations). The purpose of this Appendix is to confirm that this simplified approach
to following RLO – and therefore predicting stellar obliquities – is accurate, by benchmarking the result against full-
fledged hydrodynamics simulations of the planet structure.
We begin by recapping the method used in this paper and Hallatt & Millholland (2026a) to compute RLO evolution.
Following standard methodology for planets passively cooling (e.g. Hubbard 1977; Arras & Bildsten 2006), Hallatt
& Millholland (2026a) evolved the entropy in their planets’ innermost convective zones under radiative cooling alone
(“following the adiabats”). Using a pre-computed grid of planet structures in hydrostatic and thermal equilibrium,
Hallatt & Millholland (2026a) evolved the following equation for innermost convective zone entropy S (e.g. Marleau
& Cumming 2014):

Figure 8 compares RLO evolution using the two different approaches. The evolution is generally in strong agreement
between methods. As displayed in Figure 9, MESA produces complete atmospheric evacuation in the ∼Gyr following
RLO, whereas the Hallatt & Millholland (2026a) calculations maintain a thin ∼1% by mass atmosphere (see e.g. their
Figure 5). This late-stage atmospheric mass loss occurs as RLO gives way to photoevaporation (implemented in MESA
following Hallatt & Millholland 2026a). We have traced this difference in final mass to the transition from a convective
to a radiative structure as the atmosphere becomes extremely thin (≲10−1 M⊕). As a radiative window begins to open
at depth, the internal temperature gradient flattens (or becomes weakly inverted) while the entropy in the interior rises
and luminosities become negative; the envelope expands under PdV work, producing an inwardly-directed radiative
flux. The planet remains susceptible to photoevaporation until the remnant atmosphere is fully evacuated.
Another difference between calculation approaches is reflected in entropy evolution during mass loss (see zoomed
inset in Figure 8). The MESA run shows that the entropy in the convective zone (measured at the model’s bottom cell,
corresponding to the base of the innermost convective zone) is not fixed during mass transfer, but rather mirrors the
mass loss rate (growing to a peak before declining). The evolution in entropy can be qualitatively understood from
the ratio of escaping gas’ specific gravitational potential (−GMp/Rp) to internal (denoted u∼c2
s, with cs the sound
speed) energy. If material is only marginally bound (|GMp/Rp|≳u; i.e. Rp∼RBondi=GMp/2c2
s) mass loss does not
remove very much negative energy from the planet; losing mass while maintaining a very negative total energy requires
entropy decrease. Likewise, losing strongly bound material (|GMp/Rp|≫u) removes significant amounts of negative
energy so that the total planet energy will become very much less negative; in the case of RLO, mass loss is so intense
that entropy grows. The planets in Figure 8 satisfy |GMp/Rp|≫u initially (Rp≪RBondi), so that entropy grows upon
RLO. After peak mass loss, entropy declines as the planet transitions into the |GMp/Rp|≳u regime. Entropy then
grows again once the atmosphere becomes radiative.
