One Hex reduction to rule them all:
Quoridor, Maze Attack, Pinko Pallino and Blockade
are PSPACE-complete

Abstract
Quoridor is a popular award-winning board game whose computational complexity, listed
among the open problems of the Demaine–Hearn survey [DH09], remained open for nearly
two decades. It was settled only recently [DRvdV26], via a reduction from the formula game
Gpos tailored to Quoridor. We give a shorter and more general proof: a single reduction
from Reisch’s planar graph-Hex [Rei81], in which wall placement encodes the path-connection
structure of Hex. The same construction settles three closely related games—Maze Attack
and Pinko Pallino with no change, and Blockade with only minor adaptations—showing
that all four are PSPACE-complete, the latter three for the first time. More generally, our
reduction shows that any race-and-wall game is PSPACE-complete.

Introduction
The systematic study of the computational complexity of games has been one of the most
fruitful meeting points between recreational mathematics and theoretical computer science. Ever
since Schaefer showed that a handful of innocent-looking two-player games are as hard as any
problem solvable in polynomial space [Sch78], a long line of work has placed classical board
games at the top of the polynomial hierarchy: generalized Hex and the Shannon switching
game [ET76, Rei81], Gomoku [Rei80], Go [LS80], Amazons [FKUB05], and connection games in
general [BJS16] are all PSPACE-complete on boards of arbitrary size. Games whose play is not
bounded in advance can be even harder: generalized chess and checkers are EXPTIME-complete
[FL81, Rob84]. This boundary is captured cleanly by Hearn and Demaine’s constraint-logic
framework [HD09], in which bounded two-player games are PSPACE-complete while unbounded
ones are EXPTIME-complete. The race-and-wall games we study fall on the bounded side:
once a wall is placed, it cannot be moved or removed, and hence the number of distinct wall
configurations is finite. This is the key reason why the corresponding decision problems lie in
PSPACE. The survey of Demaine and Hearn [DH09] collects many of these results and, tellingly,
also records the games whose complexity was still open—Quoridor among them.
Quoridor is among the most popular abstract strategy games of the last three decades.
Designed by Mirko Marchesi and published by Gigamic in 1997, it won the Mensa Select award
that same year and has since become a widely sold, award-winning title, with a particular

Quoridor does not stand alone but belongs to a small family of two-player, perfect-
information race-and-wall games, in which players move pawns across a grid while dropping walls
to obstruct the opponent. The oldest member is Blockade, marketed in Europe as Cul-de-sac,
created by Philip Slater and published by Lakeside in 1975 [Sla75]. Much of its mechanics
resurface in Pinko Pallino and, later, in Quoridor itself, both attributed to Marchesi; Maze
Attack is a more recent jump-less variant. The four games thus share a common ancestry and
a common core mechanic, but differ in movement rules, wall types, and the move/place turn
structure—differences that, as we show, do not affect their worst-case complexity.
Related work.
The complexity of Quoridor was settled only very recently. Concurrently
with and independently of the present work, Drop, Rin, and van der Velde [DRvdV26]—whose
preprint appeared shortly before ours—proved Quoridor PSPACE-complete by a reduction from
the formula game Gpos. Their argument, however, relies on Quoridor-specific pawn-interaction
primitives, such as railroading, which depend on the jump rule and on purely orthogonal movement.
For this reason, it does not directly transfer to the related games considered here. Our approach
is instead closer in spirit to the connection-game tradition, since it reduces from Reisch’s planar
graph-Hex [Rei81] and uses only wall gadgets, making the construction robust under the rule
variations of Maze Attack, Pinko Pallino, and Blockade. More broadly, the complexity of
concrete board games has continued to attract attention, with recent results settling Arimaa
[RS24] and Hive [AR26]. Particularly close in spirit is the escape game Nemesis of Bergé, Dailly,
and Gerard [BDG26], in which a fugitive tries to reach an exit while an adversary deletes one
edge per round, and which is again shown to be PSPACE-complete. Such pursuit-and-evasion
games on graphs are conceptually adjacent to the race-and-wall games studied here, where one
player races to a target side while the other erects walls to seal off every route.
Our contribution.
We give a single reduction from planar graph-Hex that settles four games
at once. The construction never depends on the specific mechanics of any one game—jumps,
diagonal moves, double pawns, or directional walls—so it applies verbatim to Quoridor, Maze
Attack, and Pinko Pallino, and extends to Blockade with only the minor adaptations its
second pawn and directional walls require. This gives a new, shorter, and arguably more elegant
proof for Quoridor and the first proof for the other three.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the four games, their shared
rules, and the parametric generalization on which the decision problems are based; Section 3
recalls the planar graph-Hex problem we reduce from; Section 4 presents the gadgets; and
Section 5 gives the reduction itself.
2
The games and their generalization
2.1
The four games
The common framework.
The four games we study are two-player, perfect-information,
deterministic race-and-wall games: each player controls one or more pawns that start on one
side of a rectangular grid, and the goal is to be the first to bring a pawn to the opposite side.
The players move in alternating turns. Besides moving pawns, a player may drop walls on the
grid lines to obstruct the opponent: a wall has costant length (two for these four games), blocks
pawn movement across it, and may neither cross nor overlap another wall. In all four games,
once a wall has been placed it is permanent: it can never be removed nor moved for the rest of
the game. This monotonicity is fundamental to our reduction, since it turns every wall placement
into an irreversible commitment, mirroring the irreversible colouring of a vertex in Hex.

A second legality constraint is likewise shared by all four games: a wall may be placed only if
it does not remove the last remaining path of any pawn to its target side. Walls can therefore
lengthen a route arbitrarily but can never seal it off completely. However, we will consider this
rule not to be assumed in general in race-and-wall games. The original games are played on fixed
boards (Quoridor on 9 × 9, Pinko Pallino on 11 × 11, Blockade on 11 × 14); for the complexity
analysis these sizes are generalized to arbitrary n, as detailed in the next subsection.
Axes of variation.
Within this common framework, the four games differ only along a few
independent axes:
• Pawns per player: one (Quoridor, Maze Attack, Pinko Pallino) or two (Blockade).
• Pawn movement: a single orthogonal step (Quoridor, Maze Attack); a single orthogonal
or diagonal step (Pinko Pallino); or two orthogonal squares or one diagonal square
(Blockade). See Figure 2.
• Turn structure: either move a pawn or place a wall (Quoridor, Maze Attack, Pinko
Pallino), versus move a pawn and then place a wall when one is available (Blockade).
• Pawn confrontation: when two pawns meet, Quoridor allows a jump over the opponent,
with a lateral deviation if the landing square is blocked; Maze Attack forbids jumps, so
the pawns block one another; Blockade again allows jumps. We were unable to find the
confrontation rule of Pinko Pallino in the available sources; as we will see, however, our
reduction works for any choice of confrontation rule. See Figure 1.
• Wall types: a universal wall type that can be placed either horizontally or vertically
(Quoridor, Maze Attack, Pinko Pallino), versus two distinct wall types, one restricted
to vertical and one to horizontal placement (Blockade).

2.2
Generalization and the decision problem
For complexity purposes each game is generalized from its fixed commercial board to an arbitrary
m × n board, with the number of walls available to each player linearly bounded in n + m. The
four games share the same notion of position and differ only in a few rule switches, so we define a
single decision problem and let the game itself be a parameter, rather than repeating four almost
identical encodings.
Race-and-wall position (common to all four games).
A race-and-wall position consists
of an m × n board possibly already containing walls, together with
• the positions of the pawns, p per player (since Blockade requires two pawns per player);
• the number of walls each player still has available, possibly split into vertical and horizontal
ones (since Blockade has two kinds of walls);
• the player to move, White or Black.
The wall mechanics—length-two walls, no crossing or overlapping, permanence, and the non-
blocking constraint—are common to all games and are as described above. A position is valid if the
placed walls are pairwise non-crossing and every pawn can still reach its target side. A valid posi-
tion is a valid configuration of Game ∈{Quoridor, Maze Attack, Pinko Pallino, Blockade}
if it has the number of pawns and the wall types prescribed by Game.
Definition 2.1. For Game ∈{Quoridor, Maze Attack, Pinko Pallino, Blockade} we
consider
Win(Game) = {B : B is a valid configuration of Game and White has a winning strategy} .
3
Generalized Hex on planar graphs
The source problem for our reduction is not the ordinary n×n game of Hex, but the generalization
of Hex to planar graphs introduced by Reisch on his way to proving that Hex is PSPACE-complete
[Rei81].
4


## Page 5


We recall it here in Reisch’s own terms, because two features of his formulation—the pla-
narity of the underlying graph and the degree bound on the uncolored vertices—are exactly what
makes our embedding into a race-and-wall board possible.
3.1
Hex-graphs and the game graph-Hex
Following Reisch, the game is played on a planar graph equipped with two distinguished terminals
that lie on the outer face.
Definition 3.1 (Hex-graph and outside pair). Let H = (V, E) be a finite undirected planar
graph and let s, t ∈V be two distinct vertices. We say that s and t form an outside pair if the
graph (V, E ∪{st}) obtained by adding the edge st is still planar; equivalently, H admits a planar
embedding in which both s and t are accessible from the outer face. A planar graph together
with a selected outside pair (s, t) is called a Hex-graph.
The terminals s and t are never played on: they merely mark the two endpoints that White must
connect.
Definition 3.2 (graph-Hex). graph-Hex is played on a Hex-graph H = (V, E) with outside pair
(s, t). White and Black alternately place a stone of their colour on a vertex of V \ {s, t}, one
per turn; a vertex may be played at most once, and a stone is never moved or removed. White
moves first and wins if at some point the white vertices contain a path from s to t; otherwise
Black wins.
A position (or play situation) of graph-Hex is a Hex-graph in which some vertices already carry
a stone. We encode it by a partial colouring χ: V (H) \ {s, t} →{W, B, ⊥}, where ⊥marks the
open (still-uncoloured) vertices; the terminals s, t are never coloured and are not counted among
the open vertices.
3.2
The source language
We reduce from the exact language that Reisch proves PSPACE-complete [Rei81]. Beyond
requiring White to have a winning strategy, it imposes a degree restriction on the open vertices.
Definition 3.3. G(graph-Hex)is the set of graph-Hex positions ⟨H, s, t, χ⟩such that
1. White has a winning strategy;
2. every open vertex has degree at most 3.
This is exactly Reisch’s restriction: since the terminals are not open vertices, condition (2)
bounds the degree of every open (uncoloured) vertex, which is the property our reduction relies
on.
Theorem 3.4 (Reisch [Rei81]). G(graph-Hex) is PSPACE-complete: deciding whether White
has a winning strategy in a graph-Hex position is PSPACE-complete even under the restriction
that every open vertex other than the two terminals has degree at most three.
Lemma 3.5 (Determinacy of graph-Hex). graph-Hex is a finite, two-player, perfect-information
game without draws such that every play halts after at most |V (H)| moves. Consequently, from
any position exactly one of the two players has a winning strategy.
Proof. At most one stone is ever placed on each vertex of V (H) \ {s, t} and stones are never
removed, so every play halts after at most |V (H)| moves. By the winning condition the outcome
of a halted play is decided—White wins exactly when an s–t path of white vertices exists, and
Black wins in every other case—so no play ends in a draw. A finite, perfect-information game
without draws is determined: by backward induction on its finite game tree, we get that White
has a winning strategy precisely if and only if Black has none.

4
Embedding graph-Hex into the board
We translate a graph-Hex instance into a board configuration using only walls. This is done in
two stages. First, we fix a geometric layout of the graph H on the board; then, we replace each
vertex and each edge of this layout by a local gadget made of walls. Since the construction uses
only wall placements, the same translation is valid for all the game variants considered here.
Throughout, the figures display the exact wall geometry of each gadget, while the text explains
why the gadget behaves as required.
The layout.
The layout is provided by a standard planar grid embedding.
Fact 4.1 (Orthogonal grid embedding [TT89]). Every n-vertex planar graph of maximum degree
at most four admits an orthogonal grid drawing of area O(n2), computable in polynomial time:
each vertex is placed on a distinct grid point and each edge is drawn as a path of axis-parallel
grid segments, so that distinct edges meet only at shared endpoints and bend only at grid points.
Lemma 4.2 (Degree reduction). Every graph-Hex position ⟨H, s, t, χ⟩can be transformed, in
time polynomial in |V (H)|, into an equivalent graph-Hex position in which no vertex is coloured
black, every vertex has degree at most three, and the two terminals lie on the outer face.
Proof. Each of the three transformations below preserves the winner, hence yields an equivalent
position.
Black vertices. A vertex coloured black is permanently unusable by White, since a black
stone is never removed; deleting it together with its incident edges therefore leaves the winner
unchanged, and we may assume no vertex is coloured black.
Terminals. If a terminal, say s, has degree greater than three, add a new vertex s′ joined to
s by a single edge, recolour s white, and let s′ be the new terminal (and likewise for t). As s′
is adjacent only to the now-white s, a white path from s′ to t′ exists if and only if the original
position has a white path from s to t, so the winner is unchanged. Since s and t form an outside
pair, s′ and t′ can be placed on the outer face, where they have degree one.
High-degree vertices. After these steps every vertex of degree greater than three is coloured
white. We replace each such white vertex by a tree of degree-three white junctions; as a connected
set of white vertices behaves like a single vertex, this preserves the winner. A vertex of degree d
yields O(d) junctions, and since H is planar the degrees sum to O(|V (H)|), so the transformation
is polynomial and adds only linearly many vertices.
By Lemma 4.2 we may assume that H has no black vertex, has maximum degree three, and
has its two terminals on the outer face. We fix an orthogonal drawing of H as in Fact 4.1, whose
bounding rectangle measures w × h grid units, with w, h = O(|V (H)|).
We realize the drawing on the board by replacing each grid edge with an edge gadget (a
corridor of walls), each grid vertex with a vertex gadget, and by bending corridors wherever the
drawn edge bends. The rest of this subsection describes the gadgets.
4.1
Edge/corridor gadgets
An edge gadget is a corridor one cell wide, flanked on both sides by short rungs: length-two
wall segments protruding into the cells adjacent to the channel. We distinguish edge gadgets by
the position of these rungs. If the rungs on the two sides face each other at the same height,
we call that portion of the corridor aligned; if they alternate between the two sides, we call it
staggered. Both types appear because the two ports of a vertex gadget may demand different
local configurations.
Since some connections require an aligned corridor and others a staggered one, Figure 3
shows a small edge-switch gadget that converts one into the other; it is inserted on any corridor

Lemma 4.3 (Corridors are non-blockable). For each of the configurations shown in Figures 3
and 4, there is no legal way to obstruct the gadget and prevent a pawn from passing through it.
Proof. To stop a pawn in a one-cell-wide channel, a player must lay a wall across the channel, i.e.
on a grid line separating two consecutive channel cells. Such a wall has length two and therefore
extends one further unit into a cell adjacent to the channel. By construction the rungs occupy,
on every grid line that crosses the channel, exactly that adjacent segment on at least one side
(on both sides in the aligned case, on the alternating side in the staggered case, and likewise
around a bend and through the switch). Any cross-wall would thus overlap or cross an existing
wall, which is illegal. Hence no legal wall blocks the channel, and the pawn can always pass.

Open vertex gadget
Every still-uncoloured vertex has degree at most three, and its gadget is shown in Figure 5. It
consists of a small central chamber into which the (at most three) incident corridors open. In
the empty gadget the chamber is open enough that the incident corridors are not yet mutually

secure a passage, and Black two walls to seal it off. This is exactly what lets the gadget mimic
the colouring of v. Vertices of degree two are built in the same way, with one of the three ports
already walled off; a degree-one vertex never needs to be traversed and may be treated as a dead

Lemma 4.4 (White colours a vertex). By placing the single white wall shown in Figure 5, White
turns the chamber into a passage that cannot afterwards be obstructed by Black. Equivalently,
placing this wall saves the vertex, and White can use it on a route to t.
Proof. After the white wall is placed, the chamber together with its boundary walls forms a
channel one cell wide whose flanking grid lines are already occupied by walls, exactly as in a
corridor. By the argument of Lemma 4.3, no further wall can be laid across it, so the connection
through the vertex is permanent.
Lemma 4.5 (Black colours a vertex). If both black walls are placed as in Figure 6, no pair of
incident edge gadgets can be connected through the chamber. Moreover, after Black places the
first of these two walls, the vertex is already virtually blocked: White cannot prevent Black from
later placing the second wall.
Proof. With both walls in place, the chamber is partitioned so that the openings of distinct
corridors lie in regions separated by walls. Hence no path can connect two incident edge gadgets
through the chamber; see Figure 6. It remains to justify the commitment claim. Once the first
black wall has been placed, it is irreversible by wall permanence. The second wall remains a legal
placement for Black regardless of White’s reply (its legality with respect to the non-blocking
constraint is ensured by the safe corridor; see Lemma 5.2). Indeed, any white wall that would
pre-empt that position would either cross the first black wall, which is illegal, or close the chamber,
which only helps Black. Thus White cannot prevent Black from completing the seal.

5
The reduction
It remains to turn the embedding of Lemma 4.6 into a full configuration by choosing the safe
corridors, the pawn positions and the wall budgets. We describe a single configuration that
serves Quoridor, Maze Attack, and Pinko Pallino at once: it is built so as to remain valid
whether or not diagonal moves are allowed. The configuration for Blockade is then obtained
from this one by adding only the parts needed for the second pawn of each player. We now carry
out the construction for the first three games.
Given a graph-Hex position on a graph H, the corresponding board position is obtained
from the embedding of H of Lemma 4.6. The embedded graph occupies a rectangle of size
width(H) × height(H), positioned so that the terminal t lies at distance 2 from White’s target
side and is joined to it by a single pair of walls forming an unbreakable corridor. The two pawns
are then placed in two distinct connected components of the board.
White component.
We orient the board so that moving left goes toward Black’s target side
and moving right toward White’s target side, while up and down denote the two perpendicular
directions. Starting from s, we construct an unblockable safe corridor. The corridor first goes
straight to the left (toward Black’s target side) for 2 · |V (H)| cells; call the endpoint of this
segment Q. From Q, the corridor goes downward and then continues to the right for another
2·|V (H)| cells, reaching a point A directly below s. From A, the corridor continues as a snake-like

path placed exactly below the embedding: it moves to the right for width(H) cells, then goes
down and moves to the left for another width(H) cells, then goes down again and moves to the
right, and so on. This back-and-forth pattern is repeated 2 · height(H) times, each horizontal
segment having length width(H) and lying directly below the embedding. Two consecutive
horizontal corridors lie at distance 2 from each other and are therefore joined by a vertical
segment of length exactly 4 (the value used for the vertical segments in Remark 5.1). After
the last such segment, the corridor continues toward Black’s target side for another 2 · |V (H)|
cells, reaching point B. It then turns downward and back toward White’s target side for at least
another 2 · |V (H)| + width(H) cells, remaining unblockable throughout.
Black component.
The black component is obtained by copying the snake-like path from A to
B and placing this copy on the left, at distance 2·|V (H)| from Black’s target side. Let A′ and B′
denote the points corresponding to A and B in this copied path. Unlike in White’s component,
the corridor from B′ continues directly toward Black’s target side for another 2 · |V (H)| cells,
again remaining unblockable throughout.
Pawns, walls, and turn order.
The white pawn is placed at Q and the black pawn at A′,
and White has |V (H)| walls to place while Black has 2 · |V (H)| walls. The player to move in
the board position is the same as in the graph-Hex position. The number and placement of the
pawns and the division of the wall budget are the only game-dependent parts of the construction;
they are adjusted for Blockade below.
Because the two pawns lie in separate components, we have full control over the distance
between them: increasing it as a function of width(H) and height(H) enlarges the board by
any polynomial amount without touching the gadgets or the logic of the reduction. Hence in
variants where the number of walls depends on the board size, the budget can be scaled to any
polynomial in width(H) and height(H), the linear case included.

Lemma 5.2 (Wall placements are legal). In the constructed position each pawn always retains
the safe corridor of its own component as a route to its target side. Consequently no placement
of a wall inside a vertex or edge gadget ever removes a pawn’s last route, so every such placement
satisfies the non-blocking constraint and is legal.
Proof. The safe corridors of the two components are unblockable (Lemma 4.3) and are disjoint
from every gadget wall; hence, whatever walls are placed inside the gadgets, each pawn keeps a

that would remove a pawn’s last route, so it never forbids a gadget wall.

Lemma 5.3 (The confrontation rule is irrelevant). In any play of the constructed position, no
confrontation between the two pawns can ever occur. Consequently, the outcome does not depend
on the confrontation rule.
Proof. By construction, the two pawns are placed in two distinct connected components of the
board. Therefore, neither pawn can ever reach the component containing the other pawn, and in
particular the two pawns can never interact directly. Hence no confrontation rule can ever be
triggered, and the outcome of the game is independent of such rules.
Theorem 5.4. Given a G(graph-Hex)position, consider the board position produced by the
reduction. White has a winning strategy in graph-Hex if and only if White has a winning strategy
in Quoridor; by Lemma 5.3 the same equivalence holds for Maze Attack.
Proof. Assume first that White has a winning strategy in graph-Hex. Then White can follow the
corresponding strategy inside the Quoridor embedding by placing one wall for each vertex that
the graph-Hex strategy colors white. Thus, regardless of Black’s strategy, White can secure an s–t
path that cannot later be destroyed within at most |V (H)| moves. If Black follows the graph-Hex
simulation, this is exactly White’s winning strategy in graph-Hex. If Black deviates from the
simulation—in particular if Black races along its corridor instead of colouring a vertex—then
Black effectively skips moves in the simulated graph-Hex game; since an extra tempo never hurts
the player who receives it in graph-Hex, White’s winning strategy remains winning, and the path
is secured no later. During these turns, Black may instead move along its corridor. The black
pawn starts at A′, from which reaching its target side requires at least d2 + 2 · |V (H)| moves (the
path from A′ to B′, followed by the finale straight extension of length 2 · |V (H)|), regardless of

costs at most |V (H)| wall placements, while moving the white pawn from its start Q through s
to t and on to White’s target side costs at most 2|V (H)| + width(H) × height(H) + 2 moves,

has a winning strategy in graph-Hex. Consider the following Quoridor strategy for Black.
First, Black simulates its winning graph-Hex strategy by placing one wall in each vertex gadget
corresponding to a vertex that Black colors in graph-Hex, thereby partially blocking that vertex.
This phase lasts for at most |V (H)| turns. Then Black spends at most another |V (H)| turns
placing the second wall in each partially blocked vertex gadget, so that every vertex colored
by Black in the simulation becomes completely closed. After this, Black moves along the safe
corridor toward Black’s target side. We claim that White’s winning strategy cannot rely on the
safe corridor. Indeed, even if White spent all of the first 2 · |V (H)| turns moving along that
corridor while Black completes the simulated blocking phase, Black would still be closer to its
own target side. Hence the safe corridor cannot provide White with a winning route. Against
the strategy described above, after at most 2 · |V (H)| turns Black has completely closed all
vertex gadgets corresponding to vertices that Black can force in the graph-Hex simulation. Since
White has no winning strategy in graph-Hex, this eliminates every possible s–t path through the
embedded graph. During these same 2 · |V (H)| turns, White can at most reach the entrance
of the embedded graph near s, even if diagonal movements are allowed. Once White reaches
that point, however, no s–t path remains available inside the embedding. White also cannot
exploit vertices that were only partially blocked, because Black’s second phase closes them before
White reaches s. Moreover, once a vertex has been partially blocked, White cannot prevent Black
from placing the second wall and closing it completely, even if White still has walls available.
Therefore, if White loses the simulated graph-Hex game, the only surviving route is the safe
corridor, and that route is losing for White by construction. This contradicts the assumption
that White has a winning strategy in Quoridor. Hence any winning strategy for White in the
Quoridor instance must pass through a secured s–t path in the embedded graph, which yields
a winning strategy for White in graph-Hex.
Corollary 5.5. Win(Quoridor) and Win(Maze Attack) are PSPACE-hard.
Proof. The reduction maps every G(graph-Hex)position ⟨H, s, t, χ⟩to a valid configuration in
polynomial time (Lemma 4.6), and by Theorem 5.4 it preserves the winner for both Quoridor
and Maze Attack.
Corollary 5.6. Win(Pinko Pallino) is PSPACE-hard.
Proof. Pinko Pallino differs from Quoridor only in admitting diagonal moves. The reduction
is unchanged: the proof of Theorem 5.4 never relies on jumps, and by Remark 5.1 (the bound
on d2) diagonal movement gives neither player a faster route. Hence G(graph-Hex) reduces to
Win(Pinko Pallino) as well, which is therefore PSPACE-hard.

Corollary 5.7. Win(Blockade) is PSPACE-hard.
Proof. We adapt the construction of Theorem 5.4.
Here each player controls two pawns. We place each player’s second pawn in a private copy
of that player’s labyrinth; for White this copy omits the graph embedding, so the second white
pawn has only the long snake corridor available and can never reach White’s target side before
the pawn that traverses the embedding, while the second black pawn merely runs a copy of
Black’s snake. Consequently only one pawn per player is relevant, and the analysis reduces to
the one-pawn case. We use the same corridor construction as for Win(Quoridor) but double
the length of the relevant corridors; in particular, the corridor leading White to the entrance s
now has length 4 · |V (H)|. Since a Blockade turn moves a pawn and, when possible, places
a wall, and a move covers at most two cells (the bound on d3 in Remark 5.1), these 2 · |V (H)|
turns suffice for the players both to advance along the corridor and to carry out the simulated
colouring before the white pawn reaches s. Finally, since Blockade distinguishes vertical and
horizontal walls, each player is given only 2 · |V (H)| walls of horizontal type, which are the ones
used to colour vertices. With these adjustments the proof proceeds as in the previous cases.
Lemma 5.8 (Membership). For each of the four games Game, the language Win(Game) is in
PSPACE.
Proof. A position is describable in polynomial space and its legal moves are computable in
polynomial time, so it suffices to bound the length of the plays we must examine. Walls are a
finite, monotone resource: once placed, a wall is never removed, so along any play the number of
wall placements is at most the total wall budget, which is linear in n.
We claim that if White has a winning strategy, then White has one in which no configuration
ever repeats. Suppose not, and let a winning strategy of White revisit some configuration with
White to move. From the second occurrence onward Black could repeat the replies it used after
the first occurrence, returning the play to that configuration again and again; the play would
then cycle forever and White would never reach its target side, contradicting that the strategy
wins. Hence we may restrict attention to White strategies that repeat no configuration.
Configurations sharing a fixed set of walls are only polynomially many—they are determined
by the pawn placements and the player to move—and the wall set changes at most linearly
many times along a play, so a non-repeating play has polynomial length. Consequently, whether
White can force a win can be decided by evaluating a game tree of plays of polynomial length
whose positions and legal moves are polynomial-time computable, which lies in PSPACE [HD09].
The argument uses only the wall mechanics shared by the four games, so it applies to each of
them.
Theorem 5.9. For each of the four games Game, Win(Game) is PSPACE-complete.
Proof. PSPACE-hardness is established by the corollaries above—Theorem 5.4 for Quoridor
and Maze Attack, and the corresponding corollaries for Pinko Pallino and Blockade—and
membership in PSPACE by Lemma 5.8.
Remark 5.10 (The race-and-wall family). The reduction settles much more than the four games
of Table 1. Its only working ingredients are the race to the opposite side and costant-length walls
that cannot cross or overlap and, once placed, never move: corridor non-blockability is purely
geometric (Lemma 4.3), the two pawns sit in separate components and never meet (Lemma 5.3),
permanence yields both the irreversible commitment (Lemma 4.5) and the polynomial bound
on play length (Lemma 5.8), and the non-blocking constraint does not affect the validity of any
move in the constructed instances. Essentially, every race-and-wall game is PSPACE-complete:
whatever the pawn-movement rule (orthogonal or diagonal, any fixed number of cells per move),
whatever the confrontation rule, whatever the number of pawns, with universal or directional
walls, and whether or not a player may seal a pawn off from its target side completely.
The four commercial titles are merely the instances that happen to have a name.
