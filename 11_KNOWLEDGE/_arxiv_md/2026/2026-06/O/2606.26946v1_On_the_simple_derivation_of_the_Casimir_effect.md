The Casimir effect in its simplest form describes the attraction of two parallel con-
ducting plates at close distance due to the vacuum fluctuation of the electromagnetic
field. Its derivation can be found in many introductory works on quantum optics.
Here we return to the original paper by Casimir and find subtle nuances in his deriva-
tion that are worth discussing to give a complete picture of a mathematically sound
derivation of the effect.
In his seminal paper [1], Casimir showed that when one puts two large metal plates of
size L × L at a small distance a from each other, a negative force of

Casimir’s argument is presented in condensed form, one step is sometimes compressed in
a way that renders the derivation inconsistent, which can make it appear mysterious to
students approaching the topic. We should emphasize that this is independent of the dis-
cussions on different subtle aspects and more advanced derivations of the Casimir effect in
the contemporary literature [6, 8–13]. In this article we present this condensed argument
and then reproduce Casimir’s original argument [1] for a comparison.

Let us pause to comment on a slightly different, but also correct argument that can be
found in the literature to arrive at Eq. (14), for example in Ref. [13]. Notice that the energy
in the right compartment, E(L −a), is simply its volume L2 × (L −a) multiplied by the
energy density of the ‘free vacuum’, because the boundary conditions can be ignored for
this large volume. One can interpret that, due to the presence of the plate at distance a, a
volume of L2 × a of the free vacuum (originally of size L3) has been replaced by a modified
vacuum. It is then argued that if one considers only the left compartment (as in Figure 1A),
from Eleft(a) one should subtract the energy of the free vacuum originally contained in the
volume L2 × a of the left compartment. This argument also gives directly Eq. (14), which
should not be confused with the one we mentioned at the beginning of this note. While this
argument is consistent, we find Casimir’s original one more intuitive.
Coming back to the Casimir interaction energy U(a) in Eq. (14), it contains the difference
of two diverging quantities and hence it is still mathematically indefinite. To further proceed,
the interaction energy U(a) must be regularised. For completeness, we reproduce the original
regularisation procedure [1] here, which we also find very instructive. Casimir argued that
in realistic scenarios, electromagnetic waves of very short wavelengths would not be reflected
by the metal plates. Therefore, in computing the zero-point energy, modes with wavevectors
exceeding a certain cutoff Λ should be excluded. This would directly imply that E(a) already
in the definition in Eq. (4) is a sum over a finite number of modes, and is thus finite. As
a result, the Casimir interaction energy in Eq. (14) is well-defined as the difference of two
finite numbers. The result, of course, would depend on the cutoff Λ. However, if one assumes
that Λ is sufficiently large in comparison to the relevant scale of the problem (1/a), one can
formally consider the limit Λ →∞, which happens to be well-defined. In that way, the
cutoff-dependency is removed from the result.
