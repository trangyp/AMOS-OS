ABSTRACT
The Type II supernova (SN II) SN 2022acko was the first to be spectroscopically observed by the James Webb
Space Telescope (JWST). Here, we analyze SN 2022acko ’s second and third JWST spectra obtained at +259 and
+368 d. We identify strong features associated with hydrogen along with Intermediate-Mass and Iron-Group
Elements (IM/IGEs). The medium-resolution mode of JWST/MIRI uniquely enables the isolation of emission
features, allowing us to determine the structure of SN 2022acko, directly coupling the spectroscopic features
and the explosion mechanism. We find that IMEs display peak velocities of ∼300 km s−1, significantly larger
than the ∼100 km s−1 measured for H, He, and IGEs. We suggest a bipolar outflow best explains this ejecta
distribution, although Rayleigh-Taylor instabilities may also contribute. Additionally, we find a bulk velocity
offset of ∼97.4+86.3
−42.3 km s−1 in the ejecta which we associate with the natal kick of a neutron star. CO emission
is also detected while no SiO or dust signatures are observed. We fit the CO first-overtone and fundamental
bands with MOFAT and find a clumped distribution is required with a CO mass increasing from 1.55×10−4 𝑀⊙
at +259 to 2.47 × 10−4 𝑀⊙at +368 d. This CO mass is approximately an order of magnitude lower than that of
SN 2024ggi. As the first JWST nebular-phase study of a low-mass SN II, this work shows that such events form

1. INTRODUCTION
Type II supernovae (SNe II) are the explosions of H-rich
massive stars with zero age main sequence masses above
∼8 𝑀⊙. Owing to their short progenitor lifetimes, they are
expected to play an important role in rapidly enriching their
host galaxies with heavy elements and are often invoked as
a significant source of dust at early cosmic times (Dwek &
Scalo 1980; Todini & Ferrara 2001; Schneider et al. 2004;
Maiolino et al. 2004; Dwek 2006; Gall et al. 2011). A key
transition in this evolution is the cooling of the ejecta from an
atom-dominated plasma into the molecule formation regime.
Late-time infrared spectroscopy is uniquely suited to explor-
ing this transition because it contains several of the principal
diagnostics of the cooling inner ejecta, and therefore provides
direct constraints on the onset and strength of molecular emis-
sion, as well as on the cooling, mixing, and structure of the
ejecta (Liu & Dalgarno 1995; Wooden et al. 1993a; Cherch-
neff & Lilly 2008; Sarangi & Cherchneff 2013; Medler et al.
2025a; Mera et al. 2026a).
Among the first molecules expected to form in SNe II are
carbon monoxide (CO) and silicon monoxide (SiO). These
species trace the temperature, density, and chemical strati-
fication of the ejecta, and because they can provide direct
constraints on the degree of mixing between the H-rich enve-
lope and the carbon-oxygen rich core (Liu et al. 1992; Wooden
et al. 1993a; Sarangi & Cherchneff 2013). The principal diag-
nostics are the CO first overtone near 2.3 𝜇m, the CO funda-
mental band near 4.4 𝜇m, and the SiO fundamental band near
8.1 𝜇m (Snow & Rideal 1929; Singh 1975; Wooden et al.
1993a). However, historically these features have not been
observed in a uniform way. Limited ground based studies
have previously detected the CO first overtone in a limited
number of events (Catchpole et al. 1987; Spyromilio et al.
1988; Spyromilio & Leibundgut 1996; Gerardy et al. 2000;
Kotak et al. 2005; Pozzo et al. 2007; Meikle et al. 2011; Rho
et al. 2018; Davis et al. 2019), while Spitzer provided only
partial coverage of the CO fundamental band, although it en-
abled the first promising detections of SiO in SNe II (Kotak
et al. 2006, 2009; Szalai et al. 2011; Szalai & Vinkó 2013).
As a result, the emergence of molecular emission and its con-
nection to the broader ejecta spectrum have remained only
weakly constrained.
∗CCAPP Fellow
The advent of JWST has changed this picture.
With
sensitive spectroscopy spanning the Near- and Mid-Infrared
(NIR/MIR), JWST can follow nearby SNe II from the plateau
phase into the nebular regime and directly connect the early
H dominated spectra to the subsequent evolution of the inner
ejecta. Plateau phase observations of SN 2022acko (Shah-
bandeh et al. 2024), SN 2023ixf (DerKacy et al. 2026), and
SN 2024ggi (Baron et al. 2025; Mera et al. 2026a) showed
infrared spectra dominated by H emission. At later times,
however, JWST reveals a different picture. In SN 2023ixf,
strong CO emission together with an emerging infrared excess
shows that JWST can trace both molecular and dust emission
(Medler et al. 2025a). In SN 2024ggi, modeling of the CO
bands shows that the molecular emission can be used to con-
strain the multidimensional structure of the ejecta (Mera et al.
2026a), demonstrating that late time infrared spectroscopy
provides direct constraints on the chemistry, geometry, and
cooling of SNe II.
SN 2022acko is particularly important in this context. As
the first SN II spectroscopically observed with JWST (Shah-
bandeh et al. 2024), SN 2022acko provides the first oppor-
tunity to establish a high resolution infrared baseline for a
H-rich core collapse event with JWST. SN 2022acko was dis-
covered on UT 2022 December 6.2 by DLT40 in NGC 1300
and was rapidly classified as a young SN II (Tartaglia et al.
2018; Lundquist et al. 2022; Li et al. 2022).
Throughout
this work we adopt the PHANGS distance to NGC 1300 of
18.99 ± 2.85 Mpc (Anand et al. 2021). Pre explosion imag-
ing suggests a red supergiant progenitor with an initial mass
between ∼7.7 and 9 𝑀⊙and little evidence for substantial
pre-existing circumstellar dust (Van Dyk et al. 2023). Optical
and ultraviolet observations further showed that SN 2022acko
is a low luminosity SN IIP, making it especially interesting for
testing how molecule formation proceeds in a lower-energy
H-rich explosion (Bostroem et al. 2023).
The first JWST spectrum of SN 2022acko. Obtained at ∼
+50 d after explosion, this observation revealed numerous H I
features together with lines associated with CNO processed
material and heavier species, but no clear signatures of CO
or SiO (Shahbandeh et al. 2024; DerKacy et al. 2026). These
data therefore established that SN 2022acko was molecule
poor at early times and provided a critical baseline for later
observations.
In this paper, we present NIR/MIR nebular
phase spectral observations of SN 2022acko obtained at +259
and +368 d past explosion. These observations probe the SN
as it evolves through the nebular phase, when the NIR and

2. OBSERVATIONS
Infrared observations of SN 2022acko were obtained at
two epochs between ∼290 and 400 d after explosion, during
the nebular phase. The JWST observations were acquired
through program GO-2122 (PI: Ashall) with MIRI/MRS and
NIRSpec, while the ground based NIR spectra were obtained
with NIRES on Keck II as part of the Hawaii Infrared Su-
pernova Study (HISS; Medler et al. 2025b). Program GO-
2122 was designed to obtain a time series of infrared spectra
of a SN II spanning the plateau phase through the onset of
molecule formation. The first epoch, obtained at +50 d with
NIRSpec and MIRI/MRS, was initially presented in Shah-
bandeh et al. (2024) and a re-reduction presented in DerKacy
et al. (2026) which is the version used in the analysis in this
work.
The reduction procedures for the individual spectra are de-
scribed in the following subsections. Throughout this work,
all JWST data were reduced using Calibration Pipeline ver-
sion 1.18.0 together with Calibration Reference Data System
(CRDS) version 12.1.5 using jwst_1364.pmap (Bushouse
et al. 2025). A log of the observations is provided in Ta-
bles A.1 and A.2.
2.1. MIRI Medium Resolution Spectroscopy
Medium-resolution spectroscopic data were obtained with
MIRI at two epochs: +259 d and +368 d post-explosion.
Observations were performed using the Short, Medium, and
Long wavelength gratings to provide continuous spectral cov-
erage from 5–25 𝜇m. A 4-point dither pattern was employed
at each epoch.
The data were reduced using the publicly
available MIRI/MRS reduction notebook1, in order to pro-
duce one continuous data cube comprising all channels and
sub-bands.
The background flux in the MIRI/MRS data is poorly char-
acterized and varies both spatially across the field of view and
as a function of wavelength (see e.g., Shahbandeh et al. 2024;
DerKacy et al. 2024; Ashall et al. 2024). To remove this back-
ground, comprising instrumental and telescope noise as well
as complex emission from the host galaxy, we employ the
custom-built background subtraction pipeline AstroBkgIn-
terp2 (Nickson & Engesser 2025).
In brief, the pipeline
estimates the two-dimensional background beneath the SN
at each wavelength using a combination of interpolation and
polynomial surface fitting. The code requires aperture extrac-
tion radius and an outer annulus width, which we set to 10
and 3 pixels, respectively. We tested larger aperture sizes for
the extraction radius and found that it produced more noise in
the final extracted spectrum without changing the line fluxes.
The flux of the SN is then extracted from the full data cube
the Extract1dStep in the JWST pipeline.
We note that the data redward of ∼20 𝜇m are dominated by
noise and instrumental background. This is in part because
SN 2022acko was a low luminosity SN II, but is enhanced
by known issues with the degrading count rate throughput
of Ch3 and Ch4 3. Regardless, the SN is visible in some
slices of the data cube at these longest wavelengths, and while
the continuum is not accurate in these regions, tentative line
IDs are reliable.

3. SPECTRAL ENERGY DISTRIBUTION EVOLUTION
In Figure 1 we show the ground-based NIR and JWST NIR-
Spec and MIRI/MRS spectra of SN 2022acko and compare
the SED from +368 d to the SED obtained at +50 d from Shah-
bandeh et al. (2024); DerKacy et al. (2026). Between the two
epochs there is a clear decline in the SED of SN 2022acko as
the SN cools and fades. Unlike other SNe II observed at MIR
wavelengths, the IR continuum of SN 2022acko shows no
signs of an infrared excess associated with dust (Kotak et al.
2009; Szalai et al. 2011; Shahbandeh et al. 2023; Medler et al.
2025a). Figure 1 highlights the main differences between the
initial epoch of SN 2022acko and the latest, with the strong
H emission features and the [Ni II] 6.636 𝜇m being observed
in both epochs. However the two SEDs differ significantly at
other wavelengths showing the emergence of features associ-
ated with strong Intermediate-Mass and Iron-Group Elements
(IM/IGEs) lines, along with CO emission features, which are
present at the later phase. We discuss the lines identified in
the section below.
4. SPECTRAL EMISSION FEATURES
The spectra of SN 2022acko were obtained several hundred
days after explosion when the ejecta had become optically
thin, it was primarily powered by the radioactive decay of 56Co
and the emission was dominated by forbidden line transitions.
At these epochs they display prominent emission features
from H, He, IMEs, IGEs, and CO molecules.
In this work line identifications were guided by previous
studies of late-time SN II spectra (Jerkstrand et al. 2012;
Davis et al. 2019; Shahbandeh et al. 2022, 2024; Medler
et al. 2025a). The spectra separated by wavelength region are
shown in Figure 2, and the identified spectral lines are listed
in Table 2.
4.1. Hydrogen
H emission remains prominent throughout the late time
NIR and MIR spectra of SN 2022acko. Across the ground
based and JWST observations, we identify lines from the
Paschen, Brackett, Pfund, and Humphreys series.
In the
NIR, the strongest Paschen features include Pa𝛼1.875 𝜇m,
Pa𝛽1.282 𝜇m, and Pa𝛾1.094 𝜇m, while the Brackett
series is represented by Br𝛼4.051 𝜇m, Br𝛽2.625 𝜇m,
Br𝛾2.166 𝜇m, Br𝛿1.944 𝜇m, and Br𝜂1.817 𝜇m.
The
JWST spectra also reveal a rich set of Pfund lines, includ-
ing Pf𝛼7.466, Pf𝛽4.654, Pf𝛾3.741, Pf𝛿3.297, Pf𝜀3.039,
Pf𝜁2.873, and Pf𝜂2.758 𝜇m. At longer wavelengths, the
MIRI/MRS data further show Humphreys emission, includ-
ing Hu𝛼12.372 𝜇m, Hu𝛽7.503 𝜇m, Hu𝛾5.908 𝜇m, and
Hu𝛿5.129 𝜇m, together with additional higher order H tran-
sitions such as H9→7 11.309 𝜇m and H14→10 18.615 𝜇m.
4.2. Helium
Two emission features located at ∼1 𝜇m and ∼2 𝜇m
are identified in the SED of SN 2022acko corresponding to
the emissions from the strong He I 1.083 𝜇m and 2.059 𝜇m
lines. Additionally, there is a weaker emission feature located
around 1.7 𝜇m which may be associated with He I 1.700 𝜇m
line, however this line is much weaker than the 1 and 2 𝜇m
lines and is only identifiable in the later epochs.
4.3. Intermediate-Mass Elements
Several intermediate mass elements are detected in the late
time spectra of SN 2022acko. In the NIR, we identify emis-
sion from C, O, Mg, Si, and Ca, including [C I] 1.133 𝜇m,
[O I] 1.129 and 1.130 𝜇m, [Mg I] 1.488 and 1.503 𝜇m,
[Si I] 1.607 𝜇m, and [Ca II] 1.184 𝜇m. These transitions trace
chemically processed material in the inner ejecta and indicate
that the spectra are probing relatively cool, low ionization
regions. At longer wavelengths, the MIR spectra reveal ad-
ditional ionized species, most notably [Ar II] 6.985 𝜇m and
[Ar II] 12.813 𝜇m. The presence of both neutral and singly
ionized species points to a structured ejecta with distinct emit-
ting zones and varying ionization and excitation states.
4.4. Iron-Group Elements
Emission from IGEs is also prominent throughout the late
time spectra of SN 2022acko, showing that the observations
probe deep, chemically processed regions of the ejecta. Emis-
sion from Fe and Co is seen in several ionization states,
including [Fe II] 1.644 𝜇m, [Fe III] 2.905 and 3.229 𝜇m,
[Fe I] 15.561 and 16.596 𝜇m, [Fe II] 17.936 and 18.267 𝜇m,
[Co II] 1.636 and 10.521 𝜇m, [Co I] 12.255 𝜇m, and
[Co III] 13.813 𝜇m. An additional feature near ∼4.3 𝜇m may
also be associated with a weak Fe transition, although this re-
mains uncertain. Ni is likewise present, with [Ni I] 3.120 and

5. SPECTRAL COMPARISON
A comparison of SN 2022acko’s NIR and MIR spectra with
observations of other SNe II allow us to better understand how
this low-luminosity object differs from more typical SNe II.
This comparison allows us to assess differences in the char-
acteristics of CO signatures and the underlying continuum,
which may indicate the presence of warm dust within the
system.
5.1. NIR Comparison
Historically, the lack of spectral coverage between 2.3–
5.2 𝜇m has limited comprehensive studies of simultaneous
observations of the CO first overtone and fundamental bands
in SNe. Until SN 2022acko, SN 1987A was the only SN II
with published observations fully covering both CO regions

While each SN possesses a unique infrared continuum and

secondary peak near ∼2.47 𝜇m. Notably, a small notch on the
blue side of the CO overtone can be observed in the later spec-

from the mixing of 56Ni in the ejecta. This feature is absent in
SN 2022acko, as well as SN 2023ixf and SN 2024ggi, imply-

normal SNe II (Spyromilio et al. 1988). The CO fundamental
band also shows distinct differences. The initial peak of the
CO fundamental is coincident with the emission feature from
the Pf𝛽4.654 𝜇m line. In SN 2022acko the Pf𝛽line is sig-
nificantly stronger than in either SN 1987A, SN 2023ixf, and
SN 2024ggi. The differences in the observed Pf𝛽emission
strength relative to the underlying CO feature may reflect the
diversity in the H / CO mass ratio in the different SNe result-
ing in the varying visibility of the H line. Additionally, the
Hu𝛿5.129 𝜇m is more pronounced in SN 1987A, possibly
reflecting differences in CO mass that modulate the visibility
of the H line. After the initial hump in the CO fundamental
a smooth decline before a secondary bump is observed. The
deeper drop observed between the initial peak of the CO fun-
damental and individual rovibrational lines in SN 2022acko
compared to SN 1987A and SN 2023ixf may suggest a lower
CO temperature in SN 2022acko, as discussed in Section 7.

6. SPECTRAL VELOCITIES
Studying the properties of specific elements within the SED
of SN 2022acko provides valuable insight into the distribution
of material in the ejecta and the physics of the line-forming
regions. This is effectively accomplished by analyzing the
emission features of elements such as H, He, and Fe, which
are strong features even in low-luminosity SNe II (Reguitti
et al. 2021).
6.1. He, IME and IGEs

To model the molecular features of SN 2022acko we em-
ployed the MOlecular Fitting Analysis Tool (MOFAT: Mera
et al. 2026b), perviously used to model the CO emission
in SN 2024ggi (Mera et al. 2026a).
MOFAT is a seven-
parameter iterative framework that fits observed molecular-
band fluxes to identify optimal parameter set solutions. MO-
FAT determines the total amount of CO mass (𝑀𝐶𝑂) required
to reproduce the CO emission features by taking into account
the temperature (T1) at the inner edge of the CO shell defined
at a specific velocity (v1), which has a velocity width of Δv,
and n is the slope of the CO density distribution. Additionally,
when clumps within the ejecta are taken into account MOFAT
allows the relative size of the clumps with respect to the radius
𝑟𝑅, the density enhancement factor within the clump with re-
spect to its environment f𝑐, and the flattening parameter of the
spheroid shape of the clump, 𝜖, to be set. It assumes a spheri-
cally symmetric large-scale geometry with clumpy (spheroid)
substructures and solves the corresponding radiative transfer
equations. Molecular opacities are computed using HYDRA
(Hoeflich 1988; Sharp & Hoeflich 1990; Gerardy et al. 2002;
Rho et al. 2018), where MOFAT applies non-LTE correc-
tions. However, rather than solving time-dependent molec-
ular formation, MOFAT uses observations to constrain key
parameters and infer the temperature structure and physical
conditions at a given epoch.

We consider three representative temperature structures for
Model S. The three models are shown in Figure 10 and are
labeled as Models Sa, Sb, and Sc. Model Sa corresponds to a
temperature profile without reheating of the outer molecular
ejecta; in this case, the optical depth of the fundamental band
is extremely large, which suppresses its volumetric emission.
As a result, the flux from both modes originates from approx-

profile. In contrast to Model Sa, the second model, Model
Sb, assumes significant reheating of the outer ejecta by the
photon field, which reduces the opacity of the fundamental
band in these regions (see Figure 18 in Rho et al. 2021). This
reduction lowers the absolute flux contribution from the outer
layers, since the emergent intensity scales as 1 −exp(−𝜏) and
thus drops with decreasing 𝜏. Consequently, an intermediate
temperature structure between Model Sa and Sb is required to
reconcile these effects. This is achieved in Model Sc, which
provides a balance between the two extremes and successfully
reproduces the observed absolute flux levels near 5.2 𝜇m.

outer regions absorb a larger fraction of the radiation field,
leading to a partial reheating of the outer envelope, resulting
in a temperature structure that deviates from the non-clumped
case.
8. EJECTA DISTRIBUTION AND INSTABILITY
The emission-line profiles of H, He, IMEs, and IGEs re-
veal that SN 2022acko cannot be fully described by a simple
spherically symmetric SN II explosion. Given the difference
between the velocity offsets of the stellar burning products and
the nuclear burning products, we suggest an asymmetric ex-
plosion. The origin of this asymmetry is likely from a bipolar
wind that is launched from an accretion disk formed around
the compact central object from in-falling material (Hoeflich
et al. 2001; Höflich et al. 2004). This bipolar wind expands
outwards along the axis of rotation of the compact object,
displacing stellar burning products along its path (Hoeflich
et al. 2001), resulting in a structure where the IGEs are pre-
dominantly along the line of sight and the IMEs are located
perpendicular in the ejecta. As a bipolar wind is predicted
to fail prior to it breaking through the stellar surface (Höflich
et al. 2004), the outer H and He envelopes are left largely un-
affected by the motion of the inner layers of SN 2022acko’s
progenitor. This origin for the asymmetry agree with the ve-
locity distribution of H and He observed in the NIR and MIR
observations of SN 2022acko.
Further asymmetry could be produced by Rayleigh-Taylor
instabilities, which are expected to extend out to the carbon-
oxygen layer and He envelope interface (Kifonidis et al. 2000,
2003).
In such a scenario, the bulk of the nuclear burn-
ing products are either expelled along the line of sight with
plumes of IGE moving into the outer regions throughout the
star resulting in Rayleigh-Taylor instabilities at the interface
between different density gradients.
The enhancement of
the asymmetry brought about by the Rayleigh-Taylor insta-
bilities can explain the clumping we require for modeling
the CO emission features observed in the JWST spectra of
SN 2022acko.
A similar distribution of material was ob-
served in SN 1987A (Wang et al. 2001), as well as in several
other SNe via polarimetric observations (Wang et al. 1996;
Maund et al. 2010).
An alternative method to create the observed asymmetry in
IME and IGEs is a choked jet that fails to pierce the He en-
velope resulting in the mixing of the IME regions (Khokhlov
et al. 1999; Couch et al. 2009b; Suzuki & Maeda 2022),
however the choked jet mechanism is not favored as current
models require a neutrino driven explosions (Burrows et al.
2024).
Based on the velocity observations and mechanisms dis-
cussed above, we suggest that the structure of SN 2022acko
shown in Figure 11 may best explain the distribution of the
IGE-rich inner ejecta, the IME-rich outer ejecta and the H/He
outer envelope we observe from the NIR and MIR emission
lines. We do not constrain the properties of the S/Si layer as
there are not enough isolated lines to determine the FWHM
and offset velocity for elements within this layer.
Alongside the different velocity distributions of the IMEs
and IGEs, which can be explained by chemical asymmetries
produced by a bipolar wind or outflow, we also observe a sys-
tematic offset of the IME and IGE emission-line peaks from
zero velocity, as shown in Figure 6. The median velocity off-

9. CONCLUSIONS
SN 2022acko was the first SN II to be spectroscopically
observed with JWST, and remains the only SN II observed
with JWST MIRI/MRS during both the plateau and nebular
phases.
The initial analysis of this event placed an upper
limit of < 10−8 𝑀⊙on the CO mass and found no evidence
for SiO emission (Shahbandeh et al. 2024). In this work,
we extend the study of SN 2022acko to later phases using
JWST spectra obtained at +259 and +368 d after explosion.
These observations provide a unique view of the transition
from the plateau to the nebular phase in a low-luminosity
SN II, allowing us to follow the emergence of IMEs, IGEs,
and molecular emission in the infrared.
The spectra of SN 2022acko show clear evolution from
the initial JWST epoch. At later phases, the infrared spectra
are dominated by strong H emission together with forbidden
lines from ionized Ar, Ne, Fe, Co, and Ni. The emission
profiles show that most of the emitting material is confined
to velocities below ∼2000 km s−1.
The exception is H,
which has a characteristic velocity of 2400 ± 100 km s−1,
although this is still ∼1000–2000 km s−1 slower than in
the initial JWST observation. Compared to SN 2023ixf and
SN 2024ggi, SN 2022acko is substantially narrower in H,
with a FWHM approximately one third that of SN 2023ixf
and one half that of SN 2024ggi. This difference is consistent
with SN 2022acko being a lower-energy explosion from a
lower-mass progenitor.
We modeled both the CO overtone and fundamental emis-
sion in SN 2022acko at +259 and +368 d using the Molecular
Fitting and Analysis Tool, MOFAT. Spherically symmetric
models reproduce the global properties of the molecular emis-
sion, but clumpy models are required at later times to capture
the detailed line profiles. At +368 d, the inclusion of clump-
ing improves the fit by ∼10% and implies a characteristic
clump size of𝑟𝑅∼0.17 and a highly prolate morphology with
𝜖∼11. The inferred CO mass increases from 1.55×10−4 𝑀⊙
at +259 d to 2.47 × 10−4 𝑀⊙at +368 d, demonstrating on-
going molecule formation in the ejecta.
The CO-emitting
region is centrally concentrated, with 𝑣1 ∼300 km s−1 and
Δ𝑣∼1000 km s−1, consistent with expectations for a low-
mass progenitor of ∼8 𝑀⊙.
These results show that CO
emission is a sensitive probe of the thermal evolution, geom-
etry, and small-scale structure of SN ejecta, and that the extent
of the molecule-forming region may provide a diagnostic of
progenitor mass.
Despite the clear emergence of CO emission, SN 2022acko
shows little to no continuum emission associated with warm
dust. This makes SN 2022acko distinct from SN 2023ixf,
where warm dust emission was detected at comparable phases
(Medler et al. 2025a). The lack of pre-existing dust is con-
sistent with HST constraints on the progenitor environment
(Van Dyk et al. 2023), while the weak dust continuum and
lack of SiO emission suggest that freshly formed dust is also
limited at these epochs. Thus, SN 2022acko shows that low-
luminosity SNe II can form molecules, but may not efficiently
convert these molecules into dust on the same timescales as
more massive SNe II. Any associated dust formation may
therefore occur only on longer timescales, if at all.
The line profiles also reveal evidence for large-scale asym-
metry in the ejecta of SN 2022acko. The IGE features, to-
gether with the H and He envelopes, peak close to rest wave-
length but share a systematic offset of ∼+100 km s−1, while
IMEs such as Ar and Ne are offset by ∼+300 km s−1. This
difference suggests that the IME-rich layers are distributed
differently from both the inner IGE-rich material and the outer
H/He envelope, pointing to an asymmetric chemical structure
in the ejecta of SN 2022acko. We interpret this chemical
asymmetry as evidence that the stellar-burning products were
displaced or mixed during the explosion, potentially by fall-
back accretion onto the newly formed compact remnant and
the associated bipolar wind or outflow. Rayleigh-Taylor insta-
bilities at the boundaries between compositional layers may
have further enhanced this mixing and asymmetry in the IME-
rich ejecta.
In addition to this chemical asymmetry, the emission-line
peaks show a common residual velocity offset of ∼100 km s−1
after accounting for the local host-galaxy velocity field. This
bulk offset is distinct from the larger IME displacement and
provides evidence for the ejecta-side momentum signature
of the natal kick imparted to the newly formed neutron star.
In this picture, asymmetric mass ejection gives the compact
remnant a recoil velocity in the opposite direction through
momentum conservation.
The magnitude of the offset is
comparable to the natal velocities of slow-moving NSs in

Thus, the chemically distinct IME dis-
tribution, the fallback-powered bipolar outflow, and the bulk
velocity offset are linked observational signatures of the same
asymmetric explosion.
Taken together, these observations show that SN 2022acko
provides a rare view of the chemical and structural evolution
of a low-mass SN II. The high spectral resolution and broad
infrared coverage of JWST MIRI/MRS make it possible to iso-
late IME and IGE lines that are inaccessible from the optical
alone, while the detection of CO emission reveals the onset
of molecule formation in the inner ejecta. At the same time,
the weak dust continuum and lack of SiO emission suggest
that low-mass SNe II may contribute less efficiently to early
dust production, or may form dust on longer timescales than
more massive events. Future late-time JWST observations of
low-luminosity SNe II will be essential for determining how
common this behavior is, how their ejecta are structured, and
how these numerous explosions contribute to the chemical
enrichment and dust budgets of galaxies.
