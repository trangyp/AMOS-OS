ABSTRACT
Context. Extended Lyα emission is ubiquitously observed around quasars. This emission traces the cool gas within the circumgalactic
medium and provides key insights into the complex interplay between halo gas dynamics and active galactic nuclei (AGN) feedback.
However, the connection to the cold molecular gas of the host galaxies remains largely unexplored.
Aims. We aim to characterize the molecular gas reservoirs in quasars at cosmic noon and investigate how they are linked to extended
Lyα emission.
Methods. We present ALMA observations of the CO(4-3) transition in 37 quasars at z ∼3 from the QSO MUSEUM survey, which
have been previously mapped in Lyα with VLT/MUSE. We derive molecular gas masses and gas fractions, explore correlations with
Lyα nebula and quasar properties and search for CO-emitting companions in the fields.
Results. Of 37 quasars, 21 are detected in CO(4-3), with gas masses Mgas ≈(3 −40) × 109 M⊙. Quasars with the most massive
molecular gas reservoirs are associated with the centrally dimmest Lyα nebulae, while those hosting the centrally brightest Lyα
nebulae are generally not detected in CO. This suggests that gas and dust in the hosts regulate Lyα escape and consequently affect
the emission from halo gas. We find evidence that quasars with lower Eddington ratios harbor more massive gas reservoirs, whereas
strongly accreting quasars (λEdd ⪆0.9) likely deplete their gas, e.g. through powerful quasar-driven outflows. Despite their higher
molecular gas masses within the sample, the low-Eddington quasars with CO detections exhibit low gas fractions with a median of
Mgas/M∗∼0.10, below those typically found for inactive star-forming galaxies. Six quasars are marginally resolved in CO, with
effective radii that can be as large as ∼8 kpc. In addition, we detect 14 companion galaxies at high fidelity, indicating an overall
overdense environment in the quasar fields with a derived quasar-galaxy cross-correlation length of 9.81+2.22
−2.05 h−1cMpc.

1. Introduction
The growth and activity of supermassive black holes (SMBHs)
are tightly coupled to the evolution of their host galaxies (Fabian
2012; Kormendy & Ho 2013). Periods of rapid accretion onto
SMBHs power active galactic nuclei (AGN), whose radiative
and mechanical feedback can regulate star formation and drive
large-scale outflows (e.g. Di Matteo et al. 2005; Fabian 2012;
Weinberger et al. 2017; Zinger et al. 2020).
Quasars, the most luminous class of AGN, reside in mas-
sive dark matter (DM) halos of ∼1012.5 M⊙(White et al. 2012;
Timlin et al. 2018; Farina et al. 2019; de Beer et al. 2023; Costa
2024), placing them in overdense regions of the Universe.
Galaxy interactions and mergers, along with the enhanced gas
infall in these overdense regions, fuel both black hole accretion
and star formation in the quasar hosts (Kauffmann & Haehnelt

2000), resulting in host galaxies that are typically more
massive and actively star-forming than their contemporaries
(Molina et al. 2023). At redshifts > 2 and for halo masses char-
acteristic of quasar hosts, a substantial fraction of the gas supply
is expected to occur through accretion of cool gas (’cold-mode’)
from the intergalactic medium (IGM) (Dekel & Birnboim 2006).
This process naturally gives rise to a multiphase circumgalactic
medium (CGM), in which cool, dense gas streams coexist with
hotter, more diffuse halo gas.
Studying the CGM of quasars is crucial to understanding
the cycle of feedback and gas recycling. One particularly impor-
tant emission line for the study of the CGM is Lyα, tracing the
cool (∼104 K) halo gas. With the advent of sensitive integral-
field unit (IFU) spectrographs such as the Multi-Unit Spec-
troscopic Explorer (MUSE; Bacon et al. 2010) and the Keck
Cosmic Web Imager (KCWI; Morrissey et al. 2012), extended
Lyα emission can be detected down to unprecedentedly low
surface brightness levels. These observations have established

that quasars and AGN at z > 2 are ubiquitously surrounded
by extended Lyα nebulae, prompting large dedicated surveys
(e.g. Borisova et al. 2016; Farina et al. 2019; Cai et al. 2019;
Arrigoni Battaia et al. 2019; O’Sullivan et al. 2020; Fossati et al.
2021; Mackenzie et al. 2021; González Lobos et al. 2026). The
nebulae typically extend to scales of ∼100, kpc, and in excep-
tional cases reach several hundred kpc, forming the so-called
Enormous Lyα Nebulae (ELANe; e.g. Cantalupo et al. 2014;
Hennawi et al. 2015; Cai et al. 2017; Arrigoni Battaia et al.
2018).
AGN activity is thought to play a key role in shap-
ing the observed Lyα nebulae, primarily through recombina-
tion after quasar photoionization (e.g. Heckman et al. 1991;
Cantalupo et al. 2005; Costa et al. 2022; González Lobos et al.
2026) and resonant scattering of photons from the broad-line
region (BLR) (e.g. Cantalupo et al. 2014; Costa et al. 2022;
González Lobos et al. 2026). Collisional excitation through
gravitational cooling (e.g. Haiman et al. 2000; Dijkstra et al.
2006; Dijkstra & Loeb 2009; Faucher-Giguère et al. 2010) is
likely subdominant in the presence of a bright AGN (Costa et al.
2022; González Lobos et al. 2023). Simulations further suggest
that AGN feedback, particularly quasar-driven outflows, plays
a key role in facilitating Lyα photon escape and producing the
bright, large-scale nebulae commonly observed around quasars
(Costa et al. 2022).
The Lyα emitting gas is likely composed of cool and com-
pact gas clumps of densities similar to those in the interstel-
lar medium (ISM; nH > 1 cm−3) as invoked from photoion-
ization scenarios (Cantalupo et al. 2014; Hennawi et al. 2015;
Arrigoni Battaia et al. 2015; Borisova et al. 2016). A density dis-
tribution function with a tail at these high densities is indeed
needed in cosmological simulations to be able to reproduce
the extended Lyα emission around quasars (Costa et al. 2022;
Obreja et al. 2024). Given these conditions, the cool halo gas
could shield and harbor a cold, dense molecular phase (espe-
cially while it approaches the host galaxy), motivating efforts
to establish a connection between the two gas phases and how
they relate to AGN feedback processes (Drake et al. 2022). A pi-
lot study by Muñoz-Elgueta et al. (2022), targeting 9 quasars in
CO(7-6), CO(6-5) and [C i](2-1), with the Atacama Pathfinder
Experiment (APEX) telescope, revealed that the quasars with
the most massive molecular gas reservoirs are embedded in the
dimmest, least extended Lyα nebulae. These findings suggest
that obscuration in the host galaxy could play a role in suppress-
ing the reprocessed Lyα emission.
Beyond the host galaxies, extended molecular gas reservoirs
of quasars out to CGM scales have also been detected, albeit
in only rare cases (e.g. Emonts et al. 2019; Cicone et al. 2021;
Li et al. 2021; Jones et al. 2023; Scholtz et al. 2023; Li et al.
2023). Molecular gas in the CGM could arise from close
satellites, dense accreting gas or gas expelled from the host
galaxy by feedback processes, tidal interactions, or ram-pressure
stripping. In this context, ”cloud-crushing” simulations (e.g.
McCourt et al. 2018; Gronke & Oh 2018, 2020; Kanjilal et al.
2021) predict that cold gas can survive and even grow within
a hot halo under favorable conditions. However, latest obser-
vations suggest the presence of molecular gas only out to dis-
tances of about 10 −15 kpc from the quasar (e.g. Scholtz et al.
2023). Such scales might still correspond to ISM extents, when
considering predictions from cosmological simulations (e.g.
Obreja et al. 2024).
Cold molecular gas is typically traced through the rotational
(J) transitions of CO (e.g. Bolatto et al. 2013). With the de-
velopment of advanced interferometric submillimeter facilities,
such as ALMA (Atacama Large Millimeter/Submillimeter Ar-
ray; Wootten & Thompson 2009), quasar host galaxies can now
be studied in detail at high redshift, revealing molecular gas
masses in the range 109–1011 M⊙(e.g. Carilli & Walter 2013;
Feruglio et al. 2018; Hill et al. 2019; Pensabene et al. 2020;
Bischetti et al. 2021; Decarli et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023).
At the same time, strong AGN activity, and the consequent
radiation and outflows, can heat, dissociate and expel molecular
gas. Indeed, high-redshift (z > 2) AGN host galaxies often show
depleted gas reservoirs in the form of low gas fractions (gas mass
to stellar mass ratio) compared to inactive (non-AGN) galax-
ies (e.g. Brusa et al. 2018; Bischetti et al. 2021; Circosta et al.
2021; Bertola et al. 2024; Molyneux et al. 2025). On the other
hand, quasars in the local Universe are often found to be gas-
rich and star-forming (e.g. Rosario et al. 2018; Saintonge et al.
2012; Jarvis et al. 2020; Koss et al. 2021) with no clear evidence
of AGN activity significantly impacting the ISM of their hosts.
This contrast highlights the complex interplay between molecu-
lar gas and AGN feedback and hints at an evolution of the impact
of feedback across cosmic time.
Beyond the host galaxies, an important complementary
perspective is provided by the larger-scale environments
that quasars inhabit. High-redshift quasars are found to
be strongly clustered (e.g. Shen et al. 2007, 2010). Over-
densities surrounding quasars have been demonstrated in
the literature across a range of tracers, including rest-
frame UV-selected galaxies and dust-obscured submillime-
ter galaxies (e.g. Silva et al. 2015; Wylezalek et al. 2016;

systematic searches for such systems in the submillimeter.
In this work, we present a sample of 37 quasars at z ∼
3 observed with ALMA to study their molecular gas reser-
voirs via the CO(J=4–3) rotational transition. The quasars
are part of the QSO MUSEUM (Quasar Snapshot Obser-
vations with MUse: Search for Extended Ultraviolet Mis-
sion) survey (Arrigoni Battaia et al. 2019; Herwig et al. 2024;
González Lobos et al. 2026). This survey targets a population of
120 z ∼3 quasars with VLT/MUSE to study their extended Lyα
emission and search for ELANe. It has so far revealed that (i)
the extended Lyα emission is likely powered by a combination
of photoionization, resonant scattering and shocks, (ii) the aver-
age kinematics are consistent with gravitational motions within
quasar-hosting dark matter halos, however the velocity disper-
sion in the inner 40 kpc increases with higher quasar bolomet-
ric luminosities, (iii) similarly to the velocity dispersion, also the
Lyα surface brightness varies with quasar bolometric luminosity,
hinting at instantaneous AGN feedback effects on CGM scales
and, (iv) the surface brightness level of Lyα emission evolves
from z ∼2 to z ∼3. Following the work of Muñoz-Elgueta et al.
(2022), we aim to investigate the connection between the cool
halo gas traced by Lyα and the cold molecular gas, using a sta-
tistically more representative sample. In particular, we aim to
constrain the molecular gas properties in a large sample of z ∼3
quasars and establish a connection between the molecular phase,
the large-scale halo gas and AGN feedback processes. Addition-

2. Observations and data reduction
2.1. Sample selection
Our sample of quasars is selected from the QSO MUSEUM sur-
vey (Arrigoni Battaia et al. 2019; González Lobos et al. 2026).
The QSO MUSEUM quasars were selected from the Sloan
Digital Sky Survey (SDSS, DR14 Pâris et al. 2018) and the
13th edition of the Véron-Cetty & Véron (2010) catalogue of
quasars and AGN. The sample is divided into a bright subsample
(Arrigoni Battaia et al. 2019; González Lobos et al. 2026) with
61 quasars of absolute i-band magnitude normalized at z = 2 in
a range −29.67 ≤Mi(z = 2) ≤−27.03 and a fainter subsam-
ple (González Lobos et al. 2026) with 59 quasars of magnitudes
−27 < Mi(z = 2) < −24.
We selected 37 quasars from the bright subsample (with high
bolometric luminosity and nebular surface brightness) to be fol-
lowed up with ALMA to explore a connection between their Lyα
and CO emission (see Figure 1 for the location of the selected
quasars in the Lbol vs MBH plot). The targets are chosen such
that they cover a wide range of Lyα nebula properties and sur-
face brightnesses.
2.2. ALMA observations
The observations of the 37 quasars were carried out in ALMA
Cycle 10 under the program ID 2023.1.00963.S (PI: Fabrizio
Arrigoni Battaia) between October 3 2023 (UTC) and April 22
2024. The data were taken in Band 3 (84 to 116 GHz) with the
compact 12-m array configuration (maximum of 50 antennas
with baselines ranging from 15 m up to a maximum of 952 m
across our observations), as well as the 7-m Atacama Com-
pact Array (ACA) configuration. It provides a primary beam
of Half Power Beam Width (HPBW) of approximately 53′′
at the observed frequency of the sample, comparable to the
60′′ × 60′′ MUSE field of view. The program targets the CO(4-
3) (νrest=461.041 GHz) line emission. The total on-source ex-
posure times vary between about 30 and 90 minutes for the 12-
m array and were chosen to be sensitive to a molecular mass
as low as 5.5 × 109 M⊙, on average. The target properties and
observing conditions are summarized in Table 1. The ACA ob-
servations were automatically carried out to capture the largest
angular scales given in the proposal, but their depth is insuffi-
cient for our analysis and therefore not included here. The spec-
tral setup comprises four spectral windows with a bandwidth of
1.875 GHz (∆v ∼5100 km s−1 at the median redshift of the sam-
ple of z ∼3.2), one containing the CO(4-3) line and the others
the dust continuum.
The data were reduced using standard procedures with the
Common Astronomy Software Applications (CASA) package
version 6.5.4.9 (The CASA Team et al. 2022). The calibration
of the data was performed using the ALMA calibration pipeline
2023.1.0.124 (Hunter et al. 2023), together with the calibration
scripts that were supplied with the archival data by the ALMA
observatory. The pipeline calibration was used to create the Mea-
surement Set (MS) for each observing run, which were then
combined into a single MS for each source when multiple runs
were available.
Using the task uvcontsub we fit and subtract the contin-
uum from the measurement set in the uv domain using the three
spectral windows unaffected by CO(4-3) emission. The datacube
containing CO(4-3) is created from the task tclean with briggs
weighting and robustness parameter 2 and an automated clean-
ing threshold with noisethreshold=3.5, lownoisethreshold=1.5,
sidelobethreshold=2.0 and minbeamfrac=0.3. Robustness pa-
rameters of 2 (which maps to natural weighting) are used for
high-redshift quasars to maximize the signal-to-noise ratio (see
e.g. Decarli et al. 2018). We clean down to five times the sensi-
tivity in the cube, but verified that reducing the threshold to three
or two times does not noticeably change the final datacubes. The
channel width was specified to be 50 km/s for all cubes (with a
native velocity resolution of ∼2.7 km/s). Across the full sample,
the synthesized beam major axis has a median value of 1.6′′. The
observing conditions are summarized in Table 1.
We create continuum images by producing a multi-frequency
synthesis (MFS) images from all line-free channels (i.e. the three
spectral windows which do not contain the CO(4-3) emission
line) via the CASA task tclean using briggs weighting with the
robustness parameter set to 2. For each target, we additionally
create MFS images of the CO emission over the line-emitting
channels only using tclean, with the deconvolution performed
to a threshold of three times the rms sensitivity. These images
serve as continuum-subtracted moment 0 maps, which we use
in particular to examine the spatial extent of the emission re-
gion. Primary beam correction is applied to all datacubes and
MFS images. The depth of each observation is summarized in
Table 1, with a median value of ∼370 µJy in line RMS per
∆v = 50 km s−1 and ∼12 µJy beam−1 in continuum rms.

3. Results
3.1. ALMA CO(4-3) line detections from quasars
To robustly identify sources with CO(4–3) emission in the data
cubes, we use the code Lineseeker (González-López et al.
2017, 2019), which searches for emission-line candidates in the
ALMA cubes and provides signal-to-noise (S/N) estimates for
each detection. The code is run with its default settings, using the
recommended MinSN and MaxSigmas. Sources are considered
detections if their S/N exceeds that of any detection in the nega-
tive data. We additionally inspect the spectra at the quasar posi-
tions for potential line detections not identified by Lineseeker.
Using this criterion, CO(4-3) is robustly detected (with fidelity
∼100%) in 20 out of the 37 quasars. We additionally find very
narrow line emission at the quasar position and close to the peak
Lyα redshift of ID3. As continuum subtraction is more challeng-
ing in this source due to strong synchrotron emission, we verify
that the line is also present in the unsubtracted cube, where it is
clearly visible on top of the continuum. Although of low fidelity,
its spatial and redshift agreement with the quasar leads us to clas-
sify it as a tentative detection. Including this source as a detection
does not affect our results, as its molecular gas mass is very low
and comparable to the upper limits of the non-detections (see,
e.g., Figure 1).
For the detections, we extract spectra at the location of the
peak emission as given from the Lineseeker code with aper-
ture sizes of two or three times the beam size. The choice of
aperture size is based on a curve-of-growth analysis. We mea-
sure the integrated flux within increasing aperture sizes on the
MFS image of the CO emission and determine the beam size at
which the cumulative flux begins to plateau. This approach en-
sures that we capture the total flux associated with each detection
while minimizing contamination from noise or nearby sources.
Throughout this work, velocities in the CO(4-3) spectra are
defined relative to the centroid of the CO(4-3) emission (deter-
mined from a first-moment analysis), such that 0 kms−1 corre-
sponds to the systemic redshift of the host galaxy. To determine
the velocity range for flux integration, we fit a Gaussian profile to
each extracted spectrum. The number of Gaussian components
(one, two, or three) is selected using the Akaike Information Cri-
terion (AIC; Liddle 2007). We then integrate the flux over the
velocity interval defined by the line center ± one FWHM.
As several CO profiles require multiple Gaussian compo-
nents, we measure CO line widths using a second-moment anal-
ysis, scaled to match the FWHM of a Gaussian, and derive
CO redshifts from the first moment of the spectra. The line
widths range between ∼70 to 640 km s−1 with a median of
360 km s−1. Figure 3 shows the distribution of line widths com-
pared with other quasar studies and high-redshift radio galax-
ies (HzRGs). For direct comparison with our sample, the quasar
samples are restricted to CO(4-3) line-width measurements,
while the HzRG sample includes multiple CO transitions and
is shown for broader context. In the literature, typical molecu-
lar line widths of high-redshift quasars are found to be ∼150 −
1000 km s−1 (e.g. Carilli & Walter 2013; Venemans et al. 2017;
Bischetti et al. 2021; Li et al. 2023; Molyneux et al. 2025). We
find that the detected quasars have line widths similar to pre-
viously observed unobscured objects, but lower values than hot
dust-obscured systems (Hot DOGs; Sun et al. 2024) and HzRGs
(De Breuck et al. 2003, 2005; Ivison et al. 2012; Emonts et al.
2023). The difference in CO line widths may arise from a combi-
nation of geometric effects and host-galaxy conditions. HzRGs
are expected to be observed close to edge-on and are associated
with dynamically disturbed environments, while dust-obscured
quasars may exhibit broader CO profiles due to a more turbulent
ISM within the hosts (e.g. Martin et al. 2024).
Interestingly, two quasars with unusually narrow CO line
widths in our sample, ID3 and ID49, are both classified as
blazars (more specifically "flat-spectrum radio quasars, with an
optical spectrum showing broad emission lines and dominant
blazar characteristics") in Massaro et al. (2009). CO has been
only sporadically detected in blazars. Fumagalli et al. (2012)
searched for CO emission in three z ∼0.1 blazars, detecting it in
only one case, and inferred that blazars typically host relatively
low molecular gas reservoirs (of ∼109 M⊙) compared to quasars.
Additionally, the inferred CO(1-0) and CO(2-1) line widths are
narrow at ∆v = 121 and 110 km s−1, similar to the line widths we
obtain. The narrow CO line widths in blazars resemble those of
early-type galaxies, where the molecular gas—when present—is

3.5. CO vs QSO properties
We examine potential correlations of quasar properties, such as
bolometric luminosity, black hole mass, and Eddington ratio,
and the characteristics of their CO reservoirs. Figure 5 shows
the relation between Lbol and MBH for quasars from the QSO
MUSEUM survey, including both CO(4–3) detections and non-
detections. Within the sample, we do not identify significant
trends between molecular gas masses and either bolometric lu-
minosities or black hole masses. However, we do find evidence
that the CO-detected quasars, with more massive inferred molec-
ular gas reservoirs, have lower Eddington ratios compared to the
non-detected quasars. This is shown in Figure 6, which presents
Mgas as a function of MBH, with quasars color-coded by Edding-
ton ratio, together with the cumulative Eddington-ratio distribu-
tions of the CO detections and non-detections. In particular, we
do not find CO-detections for the quasars in our sample with
Eddington ratios larger than ∼0.9. The median Eddington ra-
tio of the CO detections is 0.40+0.24
−0.18, compared to 0.87+0.52
−0.21 for
the non-detections. A Mann-Whitney U test comparing the Ed-
dington ratios of detections and non-detections yields a p-value
of ∼3 × 10−5, indicating that there is a significant difference
between the Eddington ratios of detections and non-detections.
We note that the CO-detected quasars generally show higher
black hole masses. This could however be attributed to a selec-
tion bias, as the quasars with smaller Eddington ratios in the
ALMA-observed sample generally tend to have higher black
hole masses (see Figure 5). All these findings need to be con-
firmed given the substantial uncertainties in black hole masses (∼
0.5 dex), bolometric luminosities (∼0.3 dex) and hence Edding-
ton ratios. Indeed, within these error bars, all the quasars could
be considered to have roughly comparable black hole masses.
Furthermore, we note that differences in gas excitation could af-
fect the detectability of the CO(4-3) line in our sample. In sys-
tems with highly excited molecular gas, a larger fraction of the
CO emission may exist in higher J transitions, suppressing the
CO(4-3) flux expected from the standard assumed CO SLED
even at fixed molecular gas mass.
We investigate potential correlations between CO line widths
and quasar properties, but no significant trends are apparent. This
suggests that the kinematics of the molecular gas traced by CO
are not directly linked to the global properties of the central
AGN.

We next examine how the molecular gas reservoirs of the quasar
hosts relate to the Lyα emission properties of their surround-

function of the central Lyα surface brightness (SB, within the in-

profiles obtained with MUSE by González Lobos et al. (2026).
The noisy normalization region of 1 arcsec2 at the quasar loca-

traction, is masked to exclude the unresolved nuclear emission
when computing these surface brightness profiles.
We find that quasars with lower central Lyα SB tend to ex-
hibit higher CO detection rates and larger molecular gas masses
compared to quasars with higher Lyα SB. We primarily focus on
the central Lyα SB, as it probes gas near the quasar host galaxy
and is therefore more directly linked to the molecular gas reser-
voirs, while emission at larger radii has marked differences in
morphologies and extent between different targets. However, a
similar trend is observed when considering the Lyα SB averaged
over the full extent of each nebulae (see lower panel of Figure 7
and also Figure 9).

We find velocity shifts between the nebular Lyα peak red-

and blueshifted (∼−2000 to −12 km s−1) relative to CO. Such
offsets can arise from resonant scattering of Lyα photons through
bulk gas motions, where outflowing gas preferentially produces
redshifted emission and inflowing gas blueshifted emission (e.g.
Verhamme et al. 2006; Dijkstra 2014). The mean shift corre-

quasars in our sample the halo-gas is inflow-dominated. As the
CO redshifts provide a precise measure of the host galaxy sys-

Lyα nebulae in detail. This will be addressed in an upcoming
paper.

4. Discussion
In this section we discuss our results, starting with the connec-
tion between the large-scale Lyα emission and the molecular
gas content of the host galaxies. We find that quasars with dim-
mer Lyα nebulae tend to exhibit higher molecular gas masses,
and we explore several possible scenarios that could explain this
trend. We place our findings into the broader context of previ-
ous observational studies and simulations. We then discuss the
quasars’ gas fractions in terms of effects of AGN activity. Lastly,
we assess any evidence of our quasars residing in overdense en-
vironments and investigate effects of the quasar environments on
potential companion galaxies.
4.1. Molecular gas reservoirs and Lyα nebulae
As described in Section 3.6, we find a relation between the de-
rived molecular gas masses and the central surface brightness
of the surrounding Lyα nebulae, with brighter Lyα emission as-
sociated with quasars with comparatively lower molecular gas
masses. This trend is supported by the Kendall rank correla-
tion test (Table 3). We primarily focus on the central Lyα sur-
face brightness, as it probes gas in the immediate vicinity of the
quasar host galaxy and therefore provides a more direct probe of
the connection between the Lyα emission and the molecular gas
reservoirs.
As Lyα is a resonant line, its radiative transfer is highly
sensitive to the kinematics and distribution of the surrounding
gas. The absorption and re-emission of Lyα photons by hydro-
gen atoms occurs on very short timescales (typically ∼10−9 s,
e.g. Osterbrock & Ferland 2006; Dijkstra 2014) causing them
to undergo numerous scatterings before escaping dense envi-
ronments such as the ISM and cool clouds in the CGM (e.g.
Osterbrock & Ferland 2006; Dijkstra 2017). The origin of the
bright, extended Lyα nebulae observed around quasars, still re-
mains under discussion, but is thought to involve multiple con-
tributing mechanisms. González Lobos et al. (2026) find that
the Lyα nebulae around quasars in the QSO MUSEUM sur-
vey are likely powered by a combination of quasar photoion-
ization and subsequent recombination in the CGM; resonant
scattering of photons from the quasar broad-line region (BLR)
within the CGM; and collisional excitation in shocks. These
powering mechanisms are also supported by simulations (e.g.
Gronke & Bird 2017; Costa et al. 2022).
The observed connection between molecular gas masses and
Lyα SB should therefore be interpreted within the broader con-
text of Lyα radiative transfer and the mechanisms powering ex-
tended nebulae. On the one hand, quasars with more substantial
molecular gas reservoirs also tend to have a higher dust con-
tent (typical gas-to-dust ratios are measured to be ∼70 −100
(Wang et al. 2016; Davies et al. 2017; Venemans et al. 2017;
Bischetti et al. 2021)). A dusty host medium can absorb Lyα
photons originating from the BLR, preventing them from reach-
ing the CGM. Moreover, dust can obscure the quasar itself, re-
ducing the flux of ionizing photons that excite hydrogen in the
halos and ultimately produce the large-scale Lyα emission. In
this framework, the observed anti-correlation between molecu-
lar gas masses and Lyα SB may be driven, at least in part, by
increased dust attenuation in more gas-rich systems. This is con-
sistent with recent findings by Hall & Fu (2026), who report a
negative correlation between the integrated Lyα luminosity and
the FIR dust emission in a sample of four quasars hosted by
dusty starbursts. These systems also exhibit fainter and shallower
Lyα surface brightness profiles compared to quasars with lower
levels of dust obscuration of similar bolometric luminosity. Fur-
thermore, González Lobos et al. (2023) find that the ratio of the
nebula to quasar Lyα luminosity decreases with increasing dust
mass.
At the same time, we find tentative evidence that the CO de-
tection rate depends on quasar properties. In particular, quasars
hosting more massive black holes and exhibiting lower Edding-
ton ratios are more likely to be detected in CO and to har-
bor more massive molecular gas reservoirs (Section 3.5). On
the other hand, the high-Eddington ratio quasars in our sample
are comparatively gas-poor, while exhibiting brighter Lyα neb-
ulae. This could indicate that strong radiative feedback in the
high-Eddington systems reduces the gas and dust content, al-
lowing for a higher escape fraction of ionizing and Lyα photons.
However, we note that our sample is biased as the quasars with
higher Eddington ratios also tend to host lower-mass black holes
(see Figure 5). This raises the question of whether the observed
trend could simply reflect underlying scaling relations, given that
molecular gas content, stellar mass, and black hole mass are in-

these outflows lead to low-column density channels in hydrogen

Taken together with our observations, this suggests that quasars
with higher Eddington ratios may drive stronger outflows, which
can both reduce the molecular gas content of the hosts and
enhance the reprocessed Lyα emission.
To further investigate the link between gas depletion and
AGN feedback, we examine the relation between molecular gas
fraction and Eddington ratio. For the CO-detected quasars we
do not find a significant correlation. This holds even when con-
sidering only spatially resolved sources (which have more accu-
rate size measurements) or including additional resolved quasars
from Bischetti et al. (2021) and Molyneux et al. (2025). The ob-
served connection with accretion is thus primarily reflected in
the total molecular gas content rather than in the relative gas
fraction. However, the CO non-detections are expected to have
systematically lower gas fractions (see Figure 8). Including these
sources would therefore likely reinforce the same trend observed
between molecular gas content and Eddington ratio. Addition-
ally, our analysis covers only a limited dynamic range in Ed-
dington ratios, and the gas fractions themselves are subject to
considerable uncertainty due to the assumptions involved in their
derivation (see also Section 4.2).
Despite the possible connection between quasar-driven out-
flows, gas depletion and Lyα surface brightness, we do not
detect signatures of molecular outflows in our sample. Cold,
molecular outflows have been detected in CO on kpc scales
(e.g., Feruglio et al. 2010; Fiore et al. 2017; Brusa et al. 2018;
Herrera-Camus et al. 2019; Chartas et al. 2020; Vayner et al.
2021; Speranza et al. 2024). However, their detection gener-
ally requires high SNR and spatial resolution. Observational
studies of outflows in multiphase tracers have shown that the
cold, molecular phase likely constitutes the bulk of the out-
flow mass budget (e.g. Feruglio et al. 2015; Tombesi et al. 2015;
Rupke et al. 2017; Fluetsch et al. 2021; Speranza et al. 2024).
On the other hand, energy injection from AGN or stellar feed-
back could heat the cold molecular gas, producing a warmer
phase that could dominate the outflow (Davies et al. 2024;
Dan et al. 2025; Barfety et al. 2025). Moreover, simulations by
Costa et al. (2018a,b), which model outflows driven by AGN ra-
diation pressure on dust, show that warm, dense gas (T ∼105 K,
nH > 1 cm−3 ) carries a large amount of outflow mass on kpc
scales, even when not explicitly tracking molecular gas. Ad-
ditional quasar ionization could make this phase observable in
emission lines such as [O iii] (Costa et al. 2022). Even if the
molecular gas does not directly participate in the outflows, it
may still be impacted indirectly, for instance, through heating
or through photodissociation driven by quasar radiation.
In addition to affecting the surface brightness, quasar-driven
outflows also lead to more extended nebulae in the simulations
by Costa et al. (2022). This occurs because low-column-density
channels reduce scattering in the central few kpc, allowing Lyα
photons to remain in resonance with halo gas and scatter out
to larger radii. However, the same simulations also show that
more luminous and longer-lived quasars can reduce the overall
hydrogen optical depth, resulting in less efficient Lyα scatter-
ing and more compact nebulae. Additional factors, such as the
orientation and line-of-sight toward the quasar, can further influ-
ence the observed nebular extent. Muñoz-Elgueta et al. (2022)
reported a tentative trend between molecular gas mass and Lyα
emission area, with quasars hosting more massive molecular gas
reservoirs displaying less extended nebulae. Given the limita-
tions of the APEX data (see Section 3.8), we revisit this rela-
tion using our dataset by examining the relation between molec-
ular gas mass and Lyα emission area (measured within the 2 σ
isophotes). We do not find a significant correlation, even when
considering variations in Eddington ratio or bolometric luminos-
ity (see also Table 3). This lack of correlation may reflect the
discussed degeneracies and requires further investigation.
Another aspect that could affect the brightness and extent
of the Lyα nebulae is the orientation of the systems. Systems
with host galaxies that are viewed edge-on are more likely to
exhibit nebulae with an asymmetric geometry and lower surface
brightness (Muñoz-Elgueta et al. 2022; Costa et al. 2022). For a
disk galaxy, the Lyα flux escapes primarily along the rotation
axis, as it encounters lower column densities along this direc-
tion. However, the observed trend with molecular gas is unlikely
to be driven by orientation alone, as it would require that all
quasars with larger molecular gas reservoirs happen to be viewed
more edge-on by chance. Moreover, the correlation with Edding-
ton ratio supports the idea that intrinsic quasar properties, rather

An alternative explanation for the observed connection be-
tween Lyα surface brightness and molecular gas reservoirs in-
volves the halo mass. Quasars with more massive molecular gas
reservoirs may preferentially reside in more massive dark mat-
ter halos, with deeper potential wells to retain baryonic matter.
This could be relevant for our sample, since the CO detections
tend to have higher black-hole masses, albeit with substantial
uncertainties, which may statistically point to higher-mass ha-
los. In turn, a higher halo mass would indicate a system more
dominated by hot accretion. Indeed, according to simulations by
Dekel & Birnboim (2006), whether gas from the IGM shock-
heats or gets accreted in cold form depends sensitively on the
halo mass. In lower mass halos, a larger fraction of the ac-
creted gas remains in a cold phase, thus boosting the reservoir
of cool CGM gas available to emit Lyα, ultimately resulting in
brighter nebulae. Such a halo-mass dependence would compli-
cate a direct interpretation of the observed trend solely in terms
of quasar-driven outflows.
An equally important question is whether molecular gas
could form within the cool halo gas traced by the Lyα
nebulae, provided it reaches sufficiently high densities (e.g.
Decarli et al. 2021; Muñoz-Elgueta et al. 2022). Cloud-crushing
simulations by e.g., McCourt et al. (2018), Gronke & Oh (2018)
or Kanjilal et al. (2021) show that cold gas can be entrained
in a hot halo environment. However, extended CO emission
on circumgalactic scales has only been detected in a handful
of systems (Emonts et al. 2019; Cicone et al. 2021; Jones et al.
2023; Li et al. 2023), whereas several other studies of quasars
do not find evidence for molecular gas beyond the central galaxy
(Riechers et al. 2006; Decarli et al. 2021). In our sample, the ma-
jority of CO emission remains unresolved, indicating that it is
concentrated within the inner ∼4 kpc. Six targets are resolved in
the 12 m data according to the criterion of Decarli et al. (2018),
revealing larger spatial extents with radii up to ∼8.4 kpc. These
sizes are comparable with other reports of extended molecular
gas around z ∼2 −3 quasars (e.g. Scholtz et al. 2023) and con-
sistent with predicted extents of the ISM in quasar host galax-
ies at similar redshifts from cosmological simulations of up to
r ∼20 kpc (e.g. Obreja et al. 2024). This suggests that the de-
tected emission may still trace the outer regions of the ISM rather
than the CGM. We caution, however, that size estimates from
imfit are subject to significant uncertainties, in particular con-
sidering the low spatial resolution and SNR of our data and we
therefore do not interpret this as evidence for extended molecular
gas reservoirs.
In summary, the observed relation between Lyα surface
brightness and molecular gas mass likely reflects a combina-
tion of radiative transfer effects, dust attenuation, and underly-
ing connections with quasar properties. Quasar-driven outflows
may enhance the escape of Lyα photons while at the same time
reducing the molecular gas content, but alternative explanations
related to halo mass and host galaxy orientation cannot be ex-
cluded.
4.2. Low gas fractions in quasar host galaxies
We find that most quasars in our sample exhibit relatively low
gas fractions, with a median of ⟨fgas⟩= 0.10. The CO non-
detections (which we generally find to exhibit high Eddington
ratios and high Lyα surface brightness, see previous section)
are expected to exhibit even lower gas fractions (see Figure 8).
We note, however, that the estimates of gas fractions are highly
uncertain due to the assumptions involved, such as the adopted
value of the αCO, the inclination of the individual galaxies and
the assumed effective radius. Nevertheless, the inferred gas frac-
tions of the quasars in our sample are systematically lower com-
pared with inactive galaxies on the star formation main sequence

relation for MS galaxies with similar stellar mass ∼1011 M⊙and

found in high-redshift quasar studies, such as the hyperluminous

2021), the luminous quasars (z ∼2) in Molyneux et al. (2025)
or X-ray selected lower luminosity AGN from Circosta et al.

mon feature during certain phases of quasar evolution.
Consistent with this picture, dust-obscured quasars have
shown evidence for strong gas depletion (e.g. Brusa et al. 2015;
Perna et al. 2018; Sun et al. 2024). These systems are often hy-
pothesized to represent a transitional stage in the evolutionary
sequence from a starburst galaxy to an unobscured quasar (e.g.
Hopkins et al. 2008; Banerji et al. 2012; Temple et al. 2019;
Calistro Rivera et al. 2021). This transition, commonly referred
to as the “blow-out phase” is thought to be driven by AGN (and
supernova) feedback, which disperses the residual gas through
powerful outflows and eventually gives rise to unobscured, blue
quasars with depleted gas reservoirs. This interpretation aligns
with models in which AGN activity and in particular ener-
getic outflows (such as driven by radiation pressure Costa et al.
2018a,b) can significantly reduce the molecular gas content in
quasars, shaping their star formation and evolutionary path.
In summary, we find that the quasars in our sample show
strong evidence for significant gas depletion, consistent with re-
sults from previous AGN studies. This further supports a sce-
nario in which AGN feedback plays a key role in shaping the
gas reservoirs in the host galaxies of these systems.
4.3. Companion galaxies in quasar fields
As mentioned in Section 3.2, we detect 14 potential companion
galaxies within the spectral windows of the quasars’ CO emis-
sion that seem to be physically associated with the quasar. This
allows us to assess whether we find that the quasars in our sample
reside in overdense environments and quantify the quasar-galaxy
clustering. Previous works on LAEs in the environments of z ∼3
quasars with extended Lyα nebulae have reported strong over-
densities (Fossati et al. 2021). In addition, clustering analyses in-
dicate that such environments correspond to dark matter haloes
with masses of order 1012.5 M⊙(Shen et al. 2007; Timlin et al.
2018; Fossati et al. 2021).
To assess the likelihood that the detected lines arise from
lower-redshift interlopers, we compute the probability that any
of the detected CO line emitters correspond to lower-redshift
galaxies emitting in CO(1-0) at z ∼0.04, CO(2-1) at z ∼1.1,
or CO(3-2) at z ∼2.1. We use the CO luminosity functions for
these transitions from Saintonge et al. (2017) and Decarli et al.
(2019) at the corresponding redshifts. To account for the de-
creasing sensitivity with increasing radius, we model the primary
beam response as a 2D Gaussian with a FWHM of ∼53′′, cor-
responding to the HPBW of the primary beam for our observa-
tions, following García-Vergara et al. (2022). For each CO tran-
sition, the limiting luminosity at the phase center is determined
from the line RMS of our sample (see Table 1), assuming a repre-
sentative line width of 300 km s−1 and requiring a 5σ detection.
We then scale the limiting luminosity with radius according to
the primary-beam response, yielding Llim(R). At each radial in-
tegration step, Llim(R) is used to compute the number density
of CO emitters brighter than this limit. The radius-dependent
number densities are then integrated over the effective cylindri-
cal companion-search volume of each field, defined by a pro-
jected radius of ∼38′′ from the phase center and a line-of-sight
redshift window corresponding to ±2000 km s−1. The probability
that each detected line corresponds to a lower-redshift CO(1-0),
CO(2-1), or CO(3-2) contaminant is computed to be ∼0.2%,
∼7%, and ∼3%, respectively. To further assess possible lower-
redshift contamination, we inspect the MUSE broad-band im-
ages for continuum counterparts at the positions of the CO line
candidates. Strong continuum emission in the MUSE spectral
range would likely indicate a lower-redshift galaxy, whereas
sources at z ∼3 are expected to be very faint or undetected in
the continuum. In the case of ID43, we identify faint continuum
emission that is spatially coinciding with the CO companion, but
no spectral features which could be indicative of a lower redshift
interloper 1. Applying the same checks for all line candidates (in
both the continuum images and the MUSE spectra) yields no ev-
idence for lower-redshift contamination. Using synthetic SDSS
r-band images created from the MUSE datacubes, we estimate a
3σ continuum depth of mr ∼26 AB for a ∼1” aperture, indicat-
ing that our companions must be fainter in continuum than this
limit. This is in agreement with templates of z ∼3 submillimeter
galaxies (e.g., da Cunha et al. 2015). Our quasar-galaxy sample
is thus determined to consist of 14 potential companion galaxies
detected in CO(4-3).
For the clustering analysis, we further restrict companion
galaxies to lie within a redshift window of ±1000 km s−1 (cor-
responding to ∆z ∼0.03), while all galaxies considered already
have a fidelity of ≥80%, consistent with the CO(4–3) cluster-
ing study at z ∼4 by García-Vergara et al. (2022). The redshift
is measured relative to the CO(4-3) redshift of the associated
quasar or, if the quasar is undetected, to the peak Lyα redshift of
the nebula. As the companions of ID8, ID45, ID46 and ID49 fall
outside this redshift window, we exclude them from the follow-
ing analysis, leaving a total of 10 companion galaxy candidates.
We first compare the number of companion galaxies detected
in the 37 quasar fields to the number expected in blank fields
within the primary beam of our observations. The latter is es-
timated from the CO(4-3) luminosity function of Decarli et al.
(2019), using the same sensitivity model as described above. At
the center of our ALMA cubes, the representative limiting lu-

diameter of each field, and a redshift interval of ∆z ∼0.03. The
expected number of SMGs across 37 blank fields is thus esti-

5. Conclusions
We present CO(4–3) observations of a sample of 37 quasars at
z ∼3. As part of the QSO MUSEUM survey, these quasars are
known to host bright, extended Lyα nebulae previously observed
with VLT/MUSE. With our new ALMA data, we investigate the
molecular gas content of the quasar host galaxies and explore
their connections to the surrounding cool Lyα-emitting halo gas
and effects of AGN activity. The main results of our work can be
summarized as follows:
– Out of the 37 targeted quasars, 21 are detected in CO(4-3),
corresponding to a detection rate of about 57%. We find CO
line widths of ∼70 −640 km s−1 and derive molecular gas
masses of the detected quasars of Mgas = (3 −40) × 109 M⊙.
For the quasars undetected in CO, we derive 3 σ upper limits
on the molecular gas masses, with a median value of ∼3.5 ×
109 M⊙.
– We explore correlations between Lyα properties and molec-
ular gas properties and find that the quasars with the dimmest
Lyα nebulae are associated with the most massive molecular
gas reservoirs. In particular quasars that host nebulae with
central Lyα SB corrected for cosmological dimming above
∼1.3×10−14 erg s−1 cm−2 arcsec−2 are mostly not detected in
CO. This indicates that host galaxy properties play a pivotal
role in shaping the characteristics of the surrounding Lyα
nebulae. In particular, strong obscuration in the quasar hosts
by gas and dust could reduce both the escape fraction of Lyα
photons from the BLR and the ionizing photon flux, thereby
reducing the halo emission.
– We find that in our sample, quasars with slightly higher black
hole masses and lower Eddington ratios (below ∼0.8) ex-
hibit more massive molecular gas reservoirs. By contrast,
quasars with higher Eddington ratios remain mostly unde-
tected in CO. This trend can be interpreted as evidence that
strongly accreting quasars drive more powerful outflows,
which deplete the gas and dust content of their host galax-
ies and thereby enhance the reprocessed Lyα emission. This
interpretation is consistent with simulations by Costa et al.
(2022) which have shown that quasar-driven outflows can
produce brighter, more extended Lyα nebulae.
– By using a dynamical mass estimate, we find that the de-
tected quasars in our sample exhibit on average low gas frac-
tions (median of Mgas/M∗∼0.10) compared to inactive, star-
forming galaxies at similar stellar masses and redshifts. This
is consistent with findings in the literature and supports the
idea that AGN activity may heat and deplete molecular gas
reservoirs. We also investigate a potential trend with Edding-
ton ratio but find no significant correlation.
– Six out of the detected quasars are spatially resolved in CO
and we derive host galaxy sizes with effective radii of up to ∼
8 kpc. For the remaining unresolved sources, the beam-major
axis radius is larger than 4 kpc, indicating that most quasars
in our sample likely have CO-emitting regions < 4 kpc.
– We detect 14 potential companion galaxies in the 37
fields, implying an overall overdensity with respect to
blank fields. A clustering analysis for the detected CO line
emitters yields a quasar-galaxy cross-correlation length of
9.81+2.22
−2.05 h−1cMpc, compared to 8.37+2.42
−2.04 h−1cMpc for z ∼4
CO(4-3) emitters in García-Vergara et al. (2022). However,
on an individual basis, only 30% of the fields show evidence
of companion galaxies in the submillimeter.
Our results highlight the importance of using multiple gas
tracers across different physical scales to gain a comprehen-
sive understanding of the processes shaping both the quasar host
galaxies and their surrounding circumgalactic medium. By com-
bining interferometric observations with ALMA to determine
host galaxy properties, such as systemic redshifts, molecular gas
content and geometry, with MUSE observations of the extended
Lyα emission, we will be able to constrain Lyα radiative transfer
effects in future studies and better understand the processes pow-
ering these large-scale gas reservoirs. Moreover, expanding the
analysis to the fainter QSO MUSEUM targets, characterized by
lower black hole masses and Eddington ratios, may provide fur-
ther confirmation of our trends. Additionally, ionized gas tracers
such as [O iii] or Hα could provide valuable information on the
presence of outflows and help test our interpretation.
