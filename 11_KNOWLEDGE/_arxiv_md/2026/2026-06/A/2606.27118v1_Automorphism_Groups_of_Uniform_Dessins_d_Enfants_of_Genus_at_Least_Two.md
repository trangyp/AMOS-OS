Abstract. For a smooth algebraic curve defined over a number field, one can associate
a bipartite graph called a dessin d’enfant.
We study the regularity and automorphism groups of dessins with uniform passports.
In a previous paper, we proved that every passport of the form [n, bq, n] of genus at
least 2 admits a dessin with trivial automorphism group. Here we prove the analogous
result for passports of the form [bq, bq, n].
We also construct examples of uniform passports of genus at least 2 for which every
dessin with that passport has nontrivial automorphism group, and others for which every
dessin with that passport has trivial automorphism group.
Finally, we give an alternative proof of the [n, bq, n] case using counting arguments
based on centralizers of permutations.

1. Introduction
1.1. Bely˘ı’s Theorem and Dessins d’Enfants
Theorem 1.1 (Bely˘ı’s Theorem). [Bel79][Bel02][JW16, Theorem 1.3] Let X be a compact
Riemann surface, that is, a smooth projective algebraic curve in PN
C for some N. Then
X can be defined over the field of algebraic numbers Q if and only if there exists a non-
constant meromorphic function β : X →bC (:= C ∪{∞}) ramified over at most three
points.
This deep theorem reveals a surprising connection between algebraic curves over num-
ber fields and combinatorial structures on surfaces. This connection deeply fascinated
Grothendieck and led to his theory of dessins d’enfants.
For any compact Riemann surface X defined over a number field, one can choose a
suitable function β (called a Bely˘ı function) such that all of its critical values lie among
three points. By applying an appropriate M¨obius transformation, these points may be
taken to be 0, 1, and ∞.
For such a pair (X, β) (called a Bely˘ı pair), one can associate a bipartite graph called
a dessin d’enfant (child’s drawing), or simply a dessin (see Definition 2.1).
The dessin for (X, β) is drawn on an orientable surface of the same genus as X, where
the black vertices (•) and white vertices (◦) represent β−1(0) and β−1(1), respectively1.
The faces — that is, the connected components bounded by edges — correspond to
β−1(∞), and the edges correspond to β−1([0, 1]). Each face is homeomorphic to a disk.
By studying the properties of dessins d’enfants, one can combine insights from algebraic
geometry and combinatorics, including graph theory, to enrich both areas of analysis. This
perspective also opens up a range of possibilities for further applications.
Two dessins D and D′ are said to be isomorphic if there exists an orientation-preserving
homeomorphism between the underlying surfaces that induces an isomorphism of the
embedded bipartite graphs, preserving vertex colors and the cyclic order of incident edges
at each vertex. Equivalently, if (X, β) and (X′, β′) are the corresponding Bely˘ı pairs, then
D and D′ are isomorphic if and only if there exists a biholomorphic map φ: X →X′ such
that β′ ◦φ = β.

1.2. Regularity and Automorphism Groups
Two fundamental invariants (up to isomorphism) of a dessin d’enfant with respect to the
Galois action are its regularity and its automorphism group. A dessin is said to be regular
if its monodromy group (see Definition 2.4) acts regularly (that is, freely and transitively)
on the set of edges. From a geometric point of view, the automorphism group of a dessin
is the group of deck transformations of the associated Bely˘ı covering. Equivalently, it can
be identified with the centralizer of the monodromy group in the symmetric group acting
on the edges.
By studying regularity and automorphism groups, one can gain insight into the symme-
try properties of a dessin d’enfant. The order of the automorphism group always divides
the number of edges of the dessin, and the equality of these two numbers is equivalent
to the dessin being regular. Thus, the structure of the automorphism group provides a
precise measure of the symmetry exhibited by the dessin.
Figure 2 shows two dessins of genus 1 with the same passport; that is, they have the
same valency list: each has 8 edges, two black vertices of valency 4, four white vertices of
valency 2, and two faces of valency 4. Although both dessins may appear highly symmetric
at first glance, the left dessin is regular, whereas the right one is not. The automorphism
group of the left dessin has order 8, while that of the right dessin has order 4.

A number field K is called a field of definition of D if both X and β can be defined
over K. Unlike the field of moduli, a field of definition of D is in general not unique, and
there need not exist a smallest field of definition.
The field of moduli depends only on the isomorphism class of D and is contained in
any field of definition of D. For most dessins d’enfants, the field of moduli is also a field
of definition; that is, X and β can be defined over M(D). However, this is not always the
case. In such situations, there is no field of definition of D that is fixed by all σ ∈G(D).
This remarkable phenomenon does not occur for dessins with trivial automorphism
groups. Interestingly, it does not occur for regular dessins either. Thus, in the context of
this problem, the most interesting cases lie between these two extremes, namely nonregular
dessins with nontrivial automorphism groups.
In this paper, we develop methods for analyzing how regular dessins, dessins with trivial
automorphism groups, and nonregular dessins with nontrivial automorphism groups are
distributed. These results provide new information on the interplay between regularity
and automorphism groups, and may help clarify their role in the study of fields of moduli
and fields of definition.
1.3. Uniform Passports and Dessins
A dessin d’enfant is said to be uniform if the valencies of black vertices, white vertices,
and faces are each constant. One also says that it has a uniform passport (or uniform
valency list).
Uniform dessins exhibit a high degree of symmetry. However, while every regular dessin
is uniform, the converse does not hold: a uniform dessin need not be regular. In other
words, uniformity does not represent the highest possible level of symmetry.
As an example, consider uniform passports [ap, bq, cr], where n = pa = bq = rc, and
suppose that n = 6. By symmetry among black vertices, white vertices, and faces, we
may assume c ≥a ≥b (equivalently, r ≤p ≤q).
In genus 0, the uniform passports are [6, 16, 6] and [23, 23, 32]. The corresponding dessins

1.4. Previous Work
Examination of the regularity and automorphism groups of uniform dessins d’enfants
suggests that, as the genus increases, the proportion of regular dessins tends to decrease,
whereas the proportion of dessins with trivial automorphism groups tends to increase.
For a given uniform passport, the following questions naturally arise:
• Under what conditions do regular dessins d’enfants exist, and how many are there?
• How are the automorphism groups distributed?
• How does this distribution change with the genus?

Definition 2.1 (Dessin d’enfant). [JW16, Definition 2] A dessin d’enfant, or simply a
dessin, is a map consisting of a connected, finite, bipartite graph embedded in a connected,
compact, oriented surface without boundary. Here, a bipartite graph is a graph whose
vertices can be colored black and white in such a way that each edge joins a black vertex
to a white vertex.

Definition 2.4 (Monodromy group of a dessin d’enfant). [JW16, 2.1.1] Define two per-
mutations x and y acting on the set of edges E of a dessin d’enfant D as follows. For
each edge e ∈E, define x · e and y · e to be the next edges around the unique black vertex
and the unique white vertex incident to e, respectively, following the counterclockwise
orientation.
The monodromy group of D is the subgroup G = ⟨x, y⟩generated by x and y in the
symmetric group Sym(E) of all permutations of E.
Since a dessin d’enfant is a connected graph, it follows that any edge in E can be
mapped to any other edge by the action of G. Therefore, the monodromy group G acts
transitively on E.
Remark 2.5. Throughout this paper, permutations act on the left, and products are
composed from right to left, i.e. (xy) · e = x · (y · e) for an edge e.
Another important observation concerning the monodromy group is that, in addition to
x and y encoding the cycles of the black and white vertices, respectively, the permutation
z = (xy)−1 encodes the cycles corresponding to the faces. In fact, for each face, half of
the edges incident to it form a cycle of z, while the remaining edges belong to cycles of z
corresponding to the neighboring faces.
It is known that if a group G generated by two elements acts transitively on a set of
edges E, then there exists a dessin d’enfant whose monodromy group is isomorphic to
G[Sco24, Theorem 3.6].
Therefore, studying groups that act transitively is essential for investigating the prop-
erties of dessins d’enfants and, consequently, of algebraic curves.
Definition 2.6 (Regular dessins). [JW16, 2.1.2] A dessin d’enfant is called regular if its
monodromy group acts freely (that is, semiregularly) on the set of its edges.
This implies that the monodromy group of a regular dessin acts freely and transitively
— hence regularly — on its edges.
The following criterion relates the order of the monodromy group to the number of
edges.
Lemma 2.7. A dessin d’enfant with n edges is regular if and only if the order of its
monodromy group is n.
Proof. See [Ohn26, Lemma 2.7].
□
Definition 2.8 (Automorphism group of a dessin d’enfant). [JW16, 2.1.2] For a dessin
d’enfant D, we define its automorphism to be a permutation of the set E of D which
preserves the cyclic order of edges around each vertex, that is, which commutes with x
and y, or equivalently, commutes with G. Thus we can define an automorphism group of
G as the centralizer:
Aut D := CSym(E)(G) = {c ∈Sym(E) | cg = gc for all g ∈G}
= {c ∈Sym(E) | cx = xc, cy = yc},
(2.4)
where Sym(E) is the symmetric group of all permutations of E.
When Aut D ∼= {1}, it is said that D has a trivial automorphism group.

Since ⟨x, y⟩= ⟨x, z⟩= ⟨y, z⟩, where z = (xy)−1, both the monodromy group and the
automorphism group are invariant under any permutation of black vertices, white vertices,

The automorphism group of a dessin d’enfant has the following properties:
• Aut D acts freely on the edges of D.
• |Aut D| divides the number of edges.
• If |Aut D| equals the number of edges, then Aut D ∼= G.
The following proposition is a basic result describing the relationship between regularity
and automorphism groups.
Proposition 2.9. A dessin d’enfant D is regular if and only if its monodromy group G
is isomorphic to Aut D.
Proof. See [JW16, Theorem 2.1].
□
This also implies that a dessin d’enfant D is regular if and only if |Aut D| equals the
number of edges.
Note that regularity does not imply G = Aut D; these groups act on the edges of the
dessin in different ways.
Proposition 2.10. If a dessin d’enfant is regular, then it has a uniform passport.
Proof. See [GGD12, Proposition 4.42] and [Ohn26, Proposition 2.10].
□
The converse of this proposition does not hold in general. This shows that regularity
exhibits a higher degree of symmetry than uniformity.
