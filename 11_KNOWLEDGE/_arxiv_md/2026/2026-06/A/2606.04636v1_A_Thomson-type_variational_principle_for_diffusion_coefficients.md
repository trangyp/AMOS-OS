A Thomson-type variational principle for diffusion coefficients
Assaf Shapira
ABSTRACT. We consider reversible interacting particle systems with conserved number of par-
ticles. A standard variational formulation describes the diffusion coefficient of such models as
the infimum of a certain functional. The purpose of this paper is to derive a new, alternative,
variational characterization, as the supremum of another functional. This is a more natural
framework when one is interested in obtaining lower bounds on the diffusion coefficient. We
present a specific example of a kinetically constrained lattice gas where this variational princi-
ple can be applied.
1. Introduction
The diffusion coefficient is an important characteristic of reversible interacting particle
systems with a conserved quantity. It is given by a positive semidefinite matrix D, depending
on the density ρ. Generically [KL99, Spo91], one expects a density profile converging at
diffusive scale to a solution of the diffusion equation:
∂tρ(t, x) = ∇(D(ρ(t, x))∇ρ(t, x)).
It is, however, instructive to consider the diffusion coefficient even without directly relating it
to a hydrodynamic limit; see, e.g., [Sha23, Sha24, AKM17, AKM18, SMN20, Sas18] for both
theoretical and numerical studies. Understanding the behavior of D gives a good candidate
both for a hydrodynamic limit and for the equilibrium fluctuation field, and provides insights
on time scales of the model. In particular, the positivity of the diffusion coefficient implies (at
least in a soft sense [Sha23]) that the density profile evolves in diffusive time scales; while 0
diffusion coefficient means that the evolution is much slower than diffusive.
One may see D, via a fluctuation-dissipation relation, as describing the space-time density
correlations in equilibrium, see [Spo91, Section II.2.2]. This is expressed in the Green-Kubo
formula given in equation (3.2). As observed in [Spo91, Section II.2.2], it is then possible to
find D by solving the Poisson problem

The purpose of this paper is to present an alternative variational principle for the diffusion
coefficient: rather than analyzing the Poisson problem (1.1) using Dirichlet’s principle, we
consider Thomson’s principle. This gives us a maximization problem over flow functions
satisfying certain properties. Thus, D is expressed as a supremum of some functional, and
can be bounded from below by applying it to test flows. This is the content of the main
result, Theorem 3.7.
After proving this Thomson-type variational principle, we show how to apply it in practice.
Since test flows must satisfy certain conditions, finding them is not a trivial task. We will
see three possible strategies to construct such flows for one particular model, introduced by
Bertini and Toninelli in [BT04]. This model is a simple example of a kinetically constrained
lattice gas, a family of interacting particle systems devised in the physics literature in order
to study the liquid-glass transition; it can be seen as the conservative version of kinetically
constrained spin models [HT25]. Kinetically constrained lattice gases have degenerate jump
rates, slowing down the dynamics and making D very small at high densities. In particular,
while the Bertini-Toninelli model is known to have a strictly positive diffusion coefficient
([GLT09, Sha24], see also equation (5.4)), it is unclear under which conditions this holds
in more generality. Moreover, kinetically constrained lattice gases are notoriously difficult to
analyze (especially out of equilibrium) since they are not attractive, making techniques such
as monotone coupling unavailable. Variational tools are hence essential to understanding
these models.
We will improve the lower bound on D for the Bertini-Toninelli model, and more impor-
tantly develop techniques that could open the door to analyzing other models in a larger
setting, where even positivity of the diffusion coefficient is unknown.
1.1. Perspective and further questions. The variational principle in Theorem 3.7 offers a
promising approach to studying diffusion coefficients of different interacting particle systems.
One example where variational methods are particularly useful, is the study of kinetically con-
strained lattice gases. The methods demonstrated here, in particular in Section 5.3, should
apply to other kinetically constrained lattice gases, including some where we are currently
not even able to prove that D is nonzero.
The maximization principle we introduced could also be used in rigorous numerics: [AKM17,
AKM18], for example, use the minimization principle (3.12) to estimate diffusion coefficients,
obtaining rigorous upper bounds converging to the true value. The work presented here could
be used in order to add exact lower bounds, providing a precise control of the error in such
numerical schemes.
