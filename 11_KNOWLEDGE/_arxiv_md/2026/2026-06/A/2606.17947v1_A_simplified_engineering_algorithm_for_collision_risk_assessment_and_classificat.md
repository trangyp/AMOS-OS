A simplified engineering algorithm for collision risk assessment and classification of LEO
satellites
Federico Tosona,∗
aCISAS “G. Colombo”, University of Padova, Padova, Italy
Abstract
Collision risk in LEO is now a sustainability constraint, not only a mission-level concern. Current risk-assessment standards (ECSS,
NASA) classify risk in discrete categories per single mission and produce no continuous index aggregable across missions or feedable
into broader sustainability assessments. This paper develops an engineering algorithm in which the expected number of collisions is
estimated from the kinetic theory of the debris flux and combined with a severity term to give a continuous, dimensionally consistent
risk index. An individual index, weighted by replacement cost, captures the expected loss to the operator; a collective index with
homogeneous severity captures the burden imposed on the operational population at the mission altitude band, independent of the
mission’s own value. Applied with a May 2026 Celestrak catalogue snapshot to three representative cases (ENVISAT, Sentinel-6,
Starlink V2 Mini), the collective index exceeds the individual one by over two orders of magnitude for the constellation element:
operator-centred assessment understates the collective cost of large constellations. A random sample of 100 catalogued LEO satellites,
swept over 100 random seeds, confirms the asymmetry as structural across the constellation and Earth-observation tiers of the
catalogue. The construction is checked internally against a snapshot proxy of the ECOB family (rank correlation ρS = 0.91 on a joint
sample of 103 objects) and externally against published ECOB values for MetOp-A and Sentinel-2, which the proxy matches within
a factor of two. A parametric analysis then gives an explicit replacement-cost threshold per altitude band, approximately one to
five million USD across the operational LEO bands, below which any satellite is structurally underestimated by operator-centred
frameworks. The index is released as an open MATLAB toolchain and is intended as the orbital component of a multi-domain
sustainability assessment.
Keywords: space debris, collision risk, Low Earth Orbit, risk classification, orbital sustainability, kinetic flux

over the past decade. Catalogued objects above ten centimetres

2. Background and state of the art
2.1. Probabilistic foundations of conjunction analysis
The quantitative assessment of collision risk is grounded in
the probabilistic treatment of conjunction events, expressing
the probability of collision during a close approach as the inte-
gral of a bivariate Gaussian over the projected hard-body region
[13, 14, 15, 16]. For short-term LEO encounters the formula-
tion reduces to a closed form [17], and Monte Carlo treatments
provide validation benchmarks [18]. These methods produce a
numerical probability for an individual encounter; their mapping
into a risk index is performed downstream and is not standard-
ised.
2.2. Categorical risk classification
The dominant practice combines collision probability with a
severity score into a categorical magnitude classification [8, 9].
The categorical structure compresses substantial variation into
a small number of classes, is designed for individual missions
and does not aggregate across them, and does not produce a
continuous index integrable within sustainability frameworks.
2.3. Continuous orbital sustainability indices
In parallel, the orbital sustainability community has developed
continuous metrics quantifying a mission’s contribution to the
orbital environment. The Environmental Consequences of Or-
bital Breakups (ECOB) index [11, 19] is the most consolidated
reference, subsequently extended to fragmentation severity and
disposal scenarios [20, 21]; parallel formulations include the
Criticality of Spacecraft Index [22], the MITRI risk index for
active-debris-removal ranking [23] and its application to opti-
mal ADR mission planning [24], and debris-environment ratings
[25, 26]. At the institutional level, the Space Sustainability Rat-
ing composite indicator [27] aggregates several of these into a
single mission rating. These indices quantify a single mission’s
contribution to a collective long-term outcome but presuppose,
rather than perform, life-cycle accounting.
2.4. The gap and the contribution of this work
The categorical engineering tradition and the continuous sus-
tainability tradition are individually mature but do not bridge:
the translation between them is, in current practice, ad hoc. The
algorithm proposed here provides that bridge, with classification
thresholds compatible with the ECSS five-class scheme and a
continuous index aggregable across missions. A complementary
open-data screening contribution [28] classifies deorbiting com-
pliance via machine learning on fused public catalogues; the
present construction is distinct, quantifying collision risk via a
physics-based kinetic-flux formulation. Both are complementary
to high-fidelity tools such as the ECOB index [11] and long-term
debris-evolution models [29], to which borderline cases can be
routed. The closest risk-index work [23, 24] ranks debris pop-
ulations from the ADR perspective via long-term Monte Carlo
evolution; the present construction is mission-side and prospec-
tive, with a closed-form classification per altitude band evaluated
on a catalogue snapshot.

does not compete with, the full ECOB index [11, 19], which re-

and relies on detailed long-term debris-evolution modelling. The
present formulation is computable in seconds from a catalogue
snapshot and is intended for rapid screening and the classifi-
cation of Section 3.4. Where a rigorous orbital assessment is
required, as in a forthcoming multi-domain framework by the
author, the full ECOB index should be used in place of this
proxy.
3.3. Severity model
The severity differs by scenario, and this is the central choice
of the construction. For the individual scenario, severity is the
expected economic loss to the operator, the mission replacement
value, S ind

= Cm, so that a high impact probability on a low-

For the collective scenario, severity is taken homogeneous across
missions: the harm imposed on the orbital commons depends on
how many operational satellites the mission threatens, a quantity

the value of m. This choice differs from the asset-weighted ag-
gregation customary in financial risk inheritance: it deliberately
decouples the environmental burden from the operator-level as-

between operator-centred and environment-centred assessment
quantitatively explicit. The constant per-target value cancels
in the normalisation, so a cheap object can carry a very high
collective risk when it operates in a congested band. Section 4.3
characterises this divergence parametrically.

3.5. Implementation
The algorithm is an open MATLAB toolchain.
The
databasecreator_real module builds the resident popu-
lation from the real Celestrak General Perturbations cata-
logue (active satellites and tracked debris), deriving each ob-
ject’s perigee altitude from its mean motion and eccentric-
ity. The individual_probability_flux and collective_
probability modules implement the two flux formulations,
sharing the density computation and differing in population,
exposure time and fragmentation weight; the risk_index mod-
ule normalises and classifies. The flux approach makes the
two scenarios consistent and numerically robust across both
dense and sparse bands, unlike discrete conjunction methods.
The toolchain is released under the MIT licence and deposited
on Zenodo (see Data availability); a full three-case evaluation
against more than seventeen thousand catalogued objects runs in
seconds. The toolchain architecture is summarised in Figure 1.
4. Case studies
4.1. Three representative cases
The algorithm is applied, using the real Celestrak catalogue
for epoch May 2026 (14 727 active LEO satellites and 2597
tracked debris objects, the latter comprising the four principal
catalogued fragmentation clouds, namely Fengyun-1C, Iridium-
33, Cosmos-2251 and Cosmos-1408), to three representative
classes of LEO object: a large defunct platform (ENVISAT,
770 km), an operational Earth-observation mission (Sentinel-6
Michael Freilich, 1336 km) and a constellation element (Star-
link V2 Mini, 525 km). Orbital states are from public Two-Line
Element sets [32]; replacement costs from representative com-
mercial valuations [33]; mission data from [34, 35, 36]. Table 1
lists the input parameters and Table 2 reports the resulting in-
dices. The perigee-altitude distribution of the catalogue, with
the case-study altitudes marked, is shown in Figure 2.
The asymmetry between the individual and collective indices
is sharpest for the constellation element. For Starlink the indi-
vidual index is Low (0.09) and the collective is Very High (30);
because both are multiples of the same reference, an individual-
only assessment understates the environmental burden by over
two orders of magnitude (Figure 3). A cheap satellite, individu-
ally low-risk, can dominate the collective degradation of its band
when operating in the most congested LEO region. The index
is computed per element, with no constellation-level weighting:
the value of 30 refers to a single satellite, and the aggregate bur-
den of a shell populated by thousands of such elements scales
linearly with their number, so the per-element asymmetry is a
conservative statement of the constellation problem. ENVISAT
is Very High on both axes, as expected for a massive defunct
platform in a debris-rich band; for a defunct object the individual
axis is retrospective, so the collective is the operationally rele-
vant one. Sentinel-6 is Low on both, reflecting a well-designed
mission in a sparsely populated regime. The two indices measure
different quantities and should not be conflated: a purely eco-
nomic, operator-centred assessment misses the external cost that
large constellations of low-value satellites place on the orbital
commons. Operationally, the three cases map onto three uses:
ENVISAT as an active debris removal priority consistent with
ESA’s Zero Debris position [37]; Sentinel-6 as a low-risk base-
line; Starlink as a regulatory case, given that current guidelines
[38] address the lifetime collective contribution of constellation
elements only indirectly through end-of-life disposal.
4.2. Statistical validation across the catalogue
The three cases above span the operational LEO range but
do not, by themselves, establish that the individual–collective
asymmetry is a structural property of the current catalogue rather
than a feature of the three chosen configurations. To address this,
the algorithm is evaluated on a random sample of N = 100 active
LEO satellites drawn from the same May 2026 Celestrak GP
snapshot (14 727 eligible objects, with the random number gener-
ator seeded as rng(20260605, ’twister’) for byte-identical
reproduction from the public Zenodo deposit). Each sampled
satellite inherits its orbital state from the catalogue.
Non-orbital parameters (replacement cost, exposed and to-
tal surface, operational and residual lifetime) are drawn from
an altitude-stratified prior intended to reflect the typical con-
figuration of the three tiers identifiable in the current LEO
population: small constellation buses below 600 km (cost log-
normal with median 5 × 106 USD, σlog = 0.5), medium Earth-
observation and science platforms between 600 and 1500 km
(median 1.5 × 108 USD, σlog = 0.7), and large specialty plat-
forms above 1500 km (median 4 × 108 USD, σlog = 0.5). Cross-
section and lifetime priors follow the same tiering. The reference
mission of Section 4 is reused without modification, so individ-
ual and collective indices remain interpretable as multiples of
the same baseline.
Across the sample (seed rng(20260605)), R = ˜Rcol/ ˜Rind
has median 73, inter-quartile range [42, 126], p90 = 163 and
p99 = 385; R > 1 on all 100 sampled objects, giving a Clopper-
Pearson 95% confidence lower bound P(R > 1) ≥0.964 on
the underlying population proportion. A seed sweep across 100
independent draws of the validation sample (seeds 20260605 to
20260704) yields median R ∈[63, 95] with sweep median 78 and
p05−p95 interval [68, 91], P(R > 1) with sweep median 0.99 and
range [0.95, 1.00], and Spearman ρ (Section 5.1) in [0.84, 0.94]
with sweep median 0.91: the headline numbers are stable to
within seed-noise. The asymmetry R ≫1 is therefore an alge-
braic consequence of the homogeneous-severity design choice
combined with the empirical cost distribution of LEO satellites:
the design choice predicts it analytically (Section 4.3 eq. 8), and
the sample confirms it numerically. The Starlink V2 Mini ratio
of ∼317 (Section 4) sits near the sample p99; the upper-tail clus-
ter lies entirely between 465 and 546 km, the constellation band,
and is dominated by Starlink-class objects (Table 3, Figure 4).
The dominance of the constellation tier (84 of 100 objects) re-
flects the current catalogue composition; the EO/science tier
alone (n = 16, median R = 6, p90 = 11) shows a smaller but
non-zero asymmetry, the specialty tier (h ≥1500 km) is un-
represented in the May 2026 sample, so the strong-asymmetry
claim generalises empirically only within the constellation and
EO/science bands.

The construction is exposed to two complementary checks.

5.2. Limitations
First, the kinetic-flux formulation assumes objects uniformly
distributed within the altitude shell and a single mean relative
velocity, and captures the average flux through a band but not the
fine geometric structure of individual encounters, for which pre-
cise conjunction analysis is required [17, 18]: the present index
is a band-level screening tool, not a conjunction-warning system.
The formulation likewise does not model collision-avoidance
capability: operational satellites manoeuvre against predicted
conjunctions with tracked objects, so the raw flux overstates
the realised impact rate for manoeuvrable spacecraft. Since col-
lision avoidance is near-universal among active operators, the
overstatement enters as a roughly uniform scale factor largely
absorbed by the normalisation; more fundamentally, the index
quantifies the structural load a mission places on its orbital en-
vironment, not the effectiveness of the operational mitigations
deployed against it, and avoidance is in any case unavailable
against the untracked sub-catalogue population. The collision
cross-section σm is the exposed (frontal) projected area, as in
ESA’s DRAMA convention; for a tumbling convex body the om-
nidirectional mean is Atotal/4 by Cauchy’s formula, so absolute
P-values are upper bounds while normalised ratios are insensi-
tive (the same definition applies to the reference). The index
reflects the tracked catalogue and the four principal fragmen-
tation clouds; sub-catalogue fragments are absent, so absolute
indices are lower bounds while the relative classification is pre-
served against the same reference population.
Second, the homogeneous collective severity is a design
choice, not an oversight. An asset-weighted alternative would
shift the index toward an operator-portfolio view and erase
the asymmetry the construction is designed to expose; the ho-
mogeneous choice corresponds to a regulatory view in which
environment-level harm depends on how many operational mis-
sions m threatens and how large the fragment cloud it produces,
not on whether the threatened missions are cheap or expensive.
The magnitude of the asymmetry reported in Section 4.3 fol-
lows by construction from this choice: it converts the implicit
operator-versus-environment trade-off into a quantitatively ex-
plicit one. The only free parameter beyond the reference is the
exponent α in f frag = (Am/Are f )α, fixed at 0.5; the thresholds
C∗(h) are α-independent by construction (reference geometry
yields ffrag = 1), and re-running for α ∈[0.3, 0.75] yields
ENVISAT ˜Rcol ∈[515, 1825], Sentinel-6 ∈[0.042, 0.053] and
Starlink V2 ∈[26.1, 35.6], leaving the five-class classification
of Table 2 invariant (Figure 7).
Third, the algorithm is a screening layer for prospective clas-
sification on public data, not a substitute for operational conjunc-
tion analysis or for long-term Monte Carlo evolution [23, 24].
The continuous index is compatible by construction with the
orbital-sustainability indices reviewed above and can be incorpo-
rated as the orbital component of a composite indicator aggregat-
ing terrestrial, atmospheric and orbital impacts. The Space Sus-
tainability Rating [27] aggregates multiple orbital-sustainability
inputs into a single mission rating; the present index is intended
as a physical screening layer upstream of such ratings. A forth-
coming multi-domain framework by the author adopts the risk-
based rationale of this work for its orbital component, with the
full ECOB index as the reference and the present algorithm as
the rapid screening proxy.

6. Conclusions
This work has presented a simplified engineering algorithm
for the assessment and classification of collision risk in LEO,
grounded in the kinetic theory of the debris flux and producing
a continuous index for both individual and collective scenarios
while retaining compatibility with the categorical ECSS classifi-
cation. Applied to three representative cases on the May 2026
Celestrak catalogue, it found an individual–collective asymme-
try of over two orders of magnitude for the constellation element,
showing how operator-centred assessment understates the col-
lective sustainability cost of large constellations. A statistical
evaluation on a random sample of 100 catalogued LEO satellites
confirmed the asymmetry as structural (every sampled object
yields collective above individual, sweep-median ratio seventy-
eight, the upper decile clustered in the constellation band); a
snapshot proxy of the ECOB family benchmarked the construc-
tion at Spearman ρ=0.91 on a joint sample of 103 objects and
matched published ECOB values for MetOp-A and Sentinel-2
to within a factor of two on a literature anchor; both checks are
stable on a past-2025 synthetic snapshot. A parametric exten-
sion gives explicit replacement-cost thresholds per altitude band,
naming the regime in which current debris-mitigation guidelines
structurally fail to constrain low-value satellites in congested
orbits. The algorithm is released as an open toolchain, intended
as the orbital input of a multi-domain Space Sustainability Com-
posite Indicator in preparation by the author. Future work will
extend the analysis to constellation and fleet scale, incorporate
sub-catalogue debris, and integrate with the operational compli-
ance assessment of the Zero Debris regulatory pathway.
