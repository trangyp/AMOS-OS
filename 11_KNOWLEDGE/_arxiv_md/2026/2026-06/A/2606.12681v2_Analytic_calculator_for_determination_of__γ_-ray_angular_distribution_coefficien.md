Abstract
A program has been developed to calculate a complete set of precise
γ-ray angular distribution coefficients and statistical tensors in maximally-
and partially-aligned nuclei.
For practical nuclear structure and reaction
purposes, there is no imposed constraint on any arguments that are likely
to arise in the determination of these quantities. The program can also be
used as a stand-alone vector-coupling calculator for the exact evaluation of
Clebsch-Gordan and Racah coefficients, the closely-related Wigner 3-j, 6-
j, and 9-j symbols, as well as Gaunt coefficients. These quantities, which
frequently arise in quantum mechanical applications involving angular mo-
mentum coupling and recoupling schemes, provide the underlying machinery
in angular distribution calculations.

1. Introduction
The angular distribution of γ rays emitted from oriented nuclear states
carries rich spectroscopic information, encoding the multipolarities XL, where
X denotes the electric (E) or magnetic (M) character and L the multipole
order, of γ-ray transitions together with the associated spin J and parity
π assignments of the nuclear levels involved. Many important parameters
associated with angular distributions routinely used in nuclear physics data
analysis are only available in limited tabular forms and there is a need to have
libraries that support direct calculation to enable future automated analy-
sis techniques and applications. However, before discussing the utility and
formalism of γ-ray angular distributions, it is worthwhile to establish pre-
cise definitions of the relevant terminology, since the literature is not always
unambiguous on this point.
A nuclear state of spin J possesses 2J + 1 magnetic substates, each char-
acterized by a quantum number m taking values from −J to +J, defined
with respect to a chosen quantization axis.
When the populations of all
magnetic substates are equal, the nucleus is said to be unoriented, and the
resulting γ-ray angular distribution is perfectly isotropic, e.g., an unoriented
J = 2 ℏstate has five associated magnetic substates each with equal popula-
tion: P(−2) = P(−1) = P(0) = P(+1) = P(+2) = 1
5. Any departure from
this uniform population constitutes nuclear orientation. There are two classes
2


## Page 3


of oriented nuclear states: aligned and polarized. Both classes of orientation
lead to anisotropies in the observed γ-ray angular distribution depending on
the degree of alignment or polarization. A nucleus is said to be aligned if
the population distribution is symmetric about m = 0, thus, our alignment
condition is defined by P(−m) = P(m). In the example of an aligned J = 2 ℏ
state, this naturally implies P(−2) = P(+2) and P(−1) = P(+1), while the
symmetry about m = 0 necessarily ensures that P(|2|) < P(|1|) < P(0). In
polarized nuclear states this symmetry is broken and P(−m) ̸= P(m). Con-
sequently, for a polarized J = 2 state, to use the same example, this implies
P(−2) ̸= P(2) and P(−1) ̸= P(1).
In reactions involving a beam of particles incident upon a target nucleus,
such as compound-nucleus reactions [1], the beam direction naturally defines
the quantization axis, often referred to as the z axis. Here, the reacting sys-
tem possesses a reflection symmetry about the plane containing the beam axis
for an unpolarized beam incident on an unpolarized target. This guarantees
that the residual nuclear state is aligned but not polarized; a consequence
of the axial-reflection symmetry of the reacting system [2], sometimes re-
ferred to as the Bohr theorem. Aligned states are further classified into two
sub-classes depending on the degree of alignment: maximum alignment and
partial alignment. When the alignment is modeled as a Gaussian distribution
of magnetic-substate populations [3, 4] characterized by a width parameter
σ, the degree of alignment is controlled by the ratio σ/J. In the limit of small
σ/J (i.e., as σ/J →0) the Gaussian is narrow and population concentrates
near m = 0, corresponding to the nuclear spin vector aligned predominantly
perpendicular to the quantization axis — this is the maximum (or complete)
alignment limit. As σ/J increases, population spreads symmetrically across
the magnetic substates toward the |m| = J extremes, and in the limit of large
σ/J (i.e., as σ/J →∞) the distribution approaches the uniform statistical
(isotropic) limit. Partial alignment refers to the intermediate regime in which
the symmetry condition P(m) = P(−m) is satisfied but population is atten-
uated and distributed across multiple substates with σ/J neither vanishingly
small nor large.

The theoretical framework underlying γ-ray angular distributions from
aligned nuclei has a long history.
The general theory of angular correla-
tions of nuclear radiations was established by Hamilton [6] and developed
into its modern form by Biedenharn and Rose [5] using the statistical tensor
formalism of Fano [14] and the Racah coefficient machinery [15]. The ex-
tension to angular distributions from reaction-oriented states was developed
by Litherland and Ferguson [2] and placed into a practically-convenient form
by Krane, Steffen and Wheeler [8], who introduced a directional-correlation-
from-oriented-states (DCO) functionality in terms of generalized F coeffi-
cients. The foundational experimental methodology was reviewed by Frauen-
felder [11], while contemporary experiments continue to exploit the DCO ra-
tio method, e.g., Ref. [16], enabling multipolarity characterization of emitted
γ rays and Jπ assignments of the levels involved. The DCO method is a type
of angular correlation analysis based on γ-γ coincidence measurements where
ratios of coincidence γ-ray intensities are measured at two different detector
angles. In experimental applications involving arrays of detectors with large
solid-angle coverage, such as Gammasphere, DCO ratios are usually deduced

perpendicular to the beam ("θ⊥≈90◦"). Here, the DCO ratio (see, e.g.,

Heavy-ion fusion-evaporation reactions provide particularly favorable con-
ditions for the production of strongly aligned residual nuclei [34, 35]. Pro-
jectile ions carry large orbital angular momenta into the compound sys-
tem, leaving the angular momentum vector of the residual nucleus strongly
aligned perpendicular to the beam axis following evaporation of a few neu-
trons. The neutron evaporation process generates an approximately Gaus-
sian distribution of magnetic substates [35], and the resulting pronounced

γ-ray angular distribution anisotropies make these measurements a power-

be exploited in contemporary experimental programs, e.g., as demonstrated
in low-energy inelastic proton scattering experiments using GRETINA [13]
where the strongly aligned nuclear states produced enable determination of

For reactions involving lighter projectiles at lower incident energies, such

In such cases the Gaussian parameterization provides a practical
and experimentally justifiable framework [3, 4], and γ-ray angular distribu-
tion measurements continue to provide important spectroscopic information
[38, 39, 16]. The same statistical tensor formalism has recently been applied
in the context of nuclear fission, where Monte Carlo calculations have been
used to quantify the spin alignment of fission fragments following neutron
emission [40].
The resources most widely used in the analysis of experimental data have
long been the Tables of Coefficients for Angular Distribution of Gamma Rays
from Aligned Nuclei by Yamazaki [3], the Angular Distributions of Gamma
Rays in Terms of Phase-Defined Reduced Matrix Elements by Rose and Brink
[7], and the companion partial-alignment tables of Der Mateosian and Sunyar
[4]. There are, however, significant practical limitations in the use of these
tabulated resources. The tables of Yamazaki [3] provide the population tensor

initial integral spins 1 ≤J ≤15 ℏand half-integral spins 3/2 ≤J ≤31/2 ℏ.

tensor rank to k = 2, 4, 6, and 8, but impose their own spin restrictions de-

Several programs exist that address subsets of the functionality provided
by the current project PyGammaRAD, including a lineage of increasingly capa-
ble Fortran implementations for the underlying vector-coupling coefficients
[41, 42, 43, 44, 45], computer algebra system approaches based on proprietary
software [46, 47], as well as more recent Java [48, 49] and symbolic Python-
based [50] open tools for the calculation of the coupling coefficients alone.
Macias et al. [51] developed a Fortran program specifically for calculating
γ-γ directional correlation coefficients and mixing ratios. This work was later
modified by Rouse et al. [52] to include analysis of angular distributions for
γ rays of mixed multipolarities from partially-aligned nuclei. However, these
codes [51, 52] are written in a language that is no longer straightforwardly ac-
cessible to many users and can only be used to calculate a limited subset of the
angular distribution coefficients presented in the adopted literature [3, 4, 7].
Furthermore, none of the aforementioned capabilities integrates the vector-
coupling machinery with the full apparatus of γ-ray angular distributions
described in the foundational tabular works of Yamazaki [3], Rose and Brink
[7], and Der Mateosian and Sunyar [4], in a single unified openly available
package. PyGammaRAD addresses this gap: an open-source toolkit written in
Python, it performs standard double-precision floating-point arithmetic and
extended-precision arithmetic, as needed, that consolidates Clebsch-Gordan,
Racah, Wigner 3-j, 6-j, 9-j, and Gaunt coefficient calculations, together with
the complete set of maximum [3, 7] and partial [3, 4] alignment angular dis-
tribution formalism presented in the associated canonical reference material.
To that end, PyGammaRAD is a self-contained tool based on conventional nu-
merical computing that is portable, extensible, and accessible to the broad
community of users familiar with the Python programming language and its
associated libraries, making it straightforward to integrate into larger code-

2. Structure of the program
A Python implementation of a suite of calculators has been written to re-
turn precise analytic results for the angular distribution coefficients and ten-
sors defined in Sect. 3, together with the associated angular momentum cou-
pling coefficients which are required in the determination of these quantities.
The closely related angular momentum coupling symbols are also defined and
coded into the suite, thus the complete software package offers broader appli-
cation beyond the scope of the angular distribution focus of this manuscript.
The program is structured in a modular fashion and a single source file
am_formulae.py contains separate classes based on multiple inheritance for
the angular momentum coupling coefficients: am_formulae.ClebschGordan,
am_formulae.Wigner3j, am_formulae.Racah, and am_formulae.Wigner9j
for the Clebsch-Gordan coefficient, Wigner-3j symbol, Racah coefficient,
and Wigner-9j symbol calculators, respectively. The Wigner-6j symbol is
strongly related to the Racah coefficient through a simple phase factor (see
Appendix A.5) and, therefore, a member function for its evaluation is also
contained within the same am_formulae.Racah class. The calculation of the
Wigner-9j symbol is also simplified using am_formulae.Racah class-inherited
methods, as described in Appendix A.6. Gaunt coefficients can be decom-
posed into a product of two Wigner-3j symbols (see Appendix A.3) and
can, therefore, be evaluated within the am_formulae.Wigner3j class. The
callable methods used to return the exact solution of these vector coupling
coefficients are then contained within AngularMomentumCalculations class
of the am_methods.py module.

3. Formalism used in the calculations
The calculators used in the evaluation of the overall angular distribution
coefficients and tensors are based on the well-known sum formulae for the
coupling of angular momenta vectors which have been extensively described
in various dedicated texts on the subject matter, e.g., Refs. [55, 56, 57, 58],
as well as in the published literature, e.g., Refs. [41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 59, 60,
48, 61]. The vector-coupling routines are described in Appendix A, while the
quantities explicitly of relevance to angular distributions — together with the
adopted formalism — are described here and also summarized in the README
documentation [53, 54] distributed with the PyGammaRAD library.
3.1. γ-ray angular-distribution coefficient calculator
Methods have been developed based on the formalism outlined by Ya-
mazaki [3] and Rose and Brink [7] to calculate angular distribution functions
in maximally-aligned nuclei. Additionally, we have further developed meth-
ods for partial alignment in nuclei based on the formalism outlined by Der
Mateosian and Sunyar [4], which in itself is an extension of the earlier work
by Yamazaki [3]. Here, we present an overview of the corresponding expres-
sions used to calculate the various functions and coefficients that are needed
to reproduce all the tabulated data in Refs. [3, 4, 7]. Furthermore, these
methods can be applied for values of J, L, m, and k well beyond the ranges
presented in the original works of Yamazaki [3], Rose and Brink [7], and Der
Mateosian and Sunyar [4].
However, two important special cases are worth noting. Firstly, for states
characterized by J = 0 the single magnetic substate m = 0 means no pre-
ferred direction can be established and emission is necessarily isotropic. Sec-
12


## Page 13


ondly, and for states with J = 1/2 ℏ, the two magnetic substates m = ±1/2
are equally populated under reaction-symmetry conditions imposed by the
reflection symmetry about the beam axis, forcing a symmetric distribution
through the m = ±1/2 substates due to the orbital angular momentum of
the incoming projectile, which again gives rise to an isotropic distribution.
Accordingly, in the canonical reference material [3, 4, 7], there are no initial
states with Ji = 0 or Ji = 1/2 ℏsince the associated anisotropy coefficients
are expected to vanish in these cases.
The adopted formalism based on complete and partial alignment are dis-
cussed, in turn, below.
3.1.1.
Maximum nuclear alignment

bitrarily large integers, so there is no inherent size limit beyond the available
memory. However, for certain very large integers, for example, factorials of
large numbers, the native math.sqrt method may not execute the conversion
to a floating-point representation, resulting in an OverflowError exception.
To handle such exceptions, PyGammaRAD employs a dual-path arithmetic strat-
egy. For the Clebsch-Gordan coefficient, a primary path first attempts eval-
uation using standard IEEE 754 double-precision floating-point arithmetic
throughout, computing both the square-root prefactor and the alternating
summation (see Eq. (A.1), Appendix A.1) in native Python floats. If an
OverflowError is raised at any point, the entire calculation is automatically
retried using the Python method decimal.Decimal with extended-precision
arithmetic at 1000 significant digits. In this fallback approach, the prefactor
is evaluated via decimal.Decimal.sqrt and the alternating sum gets accu-
mulated in a Decimal accumulator before conversion back to a float result
upon return. Wigner 3-j symbols (see Eq. (A.5), Appendix A.2) and Gaunt
coefficients (see Eq. (A.7), Appendix A.3) are closely related to the Clebsch-
Gordan coefficient, allowing for the same inherited treatment via standard
double floating-point or extended precision, as appropriate. For the Racah
coefficient, which involves a product of four triangular delta factors each
containing multiple factorials together with a similarly structured alternat-
ing sum (see Eq. (A.8), Appendix A.4), the potential for numerical overflow
is more acute; accordingly, Decimal arithmetic is employed unconditionally
rather than as a fallback, ensuring that no precision is lost in intermediate
calculations regardless of the magnitude of the angular momentum argu-
ments. Since the Wigner 6-j symbol is obtained directly from the Racah

Appendix A.
Angular momentum coupling calculator
Although the development of a vector-coupling calculator is not a novel
concept in itself, and indeed many such tools already exist that can satisfy
this need, e.g., Refs. [41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 59, 48, 60, 61, 50, 47], for con-
venience, the PyGammaRAD software package is also loaded with a complete
angular momentum calculator that may serve a wide range of applications
beyond the primary motivation of the work outlined in this paper.
The computational treatment of vector-coupling coefficients has a long
history rooted in Fortran implementations.
The foundational program of
Tamura [41] provided subroutines for the Clebsch-Gordan and Racah coeffi-
cients based on direct evaluation of sum formulae; however, as noted by Wills
[42], this approach is susceptible to numerical overflow and round-off errors
for large angular momenta in single precision, owing to the rapid growth of
the intermediate factorials. Wills [42] addressed this for the Clebsch-Gordan
coefficient by recasting the summation as a nested product, thereby avoiding
repeated exponentiation of logarithmic sums. This improvement was sub-
sequently extended to the Wigner 6-j symbol by Bretz [43]. Srinivasa Rao
and Venkatesh [44] then reformulated both the Clebsch-Gordan and Racah
coefficients in terms of generalized hypergeometric functions of unit argu-
ment, specifically sets of 3F2(1) and 4F3(1) hypergeometric series, showing
that this approach attains the same numerical accuracy as the Wills-Bretz
method while achieving a further speed advantage of approximately 5–15%.
A distinct approach was taken by Stone and Wood [45], who developed a
Fortran package based on exact root-rational-fraction (RRF) arithmetic that
addressed the limitations of large prime-factor storage which could cause inte-
ger overflow. Collectively, these Fortran implementations [41, 42, 43, 44, 45]
represent important milestones in the algorithmic development of vector-
coupling coefficient computation, and their role in the lineage leading to
24


## Page 25


modern implementations is recognized here. Computer algebra system ap-
proaches were subsequently developed by Lai [46], who wrote programs in the
Maple language capable of computing both algebraic formulae and numeri-
cally exact values of Wigner 3-j, 6-j, and 9-j symbols; comparable symbolic
capabilities are also available in Mathematica [47]. These tools, however,
require proprietary software environments. Stevenson [48] later addressed
this accessibility concern with a suite of Java applets, openly available and
capable of evaluating Clebsch-Gordan, Wigner 3-j, 6-j, and 9-j symbols,
and which remains accessible via GitHub [49].
More recently, the third-
party library SymPy [50] addressed this need through symbolic manipula-
tion and arbitrary-precision evaluation within the open Python ecosystem.
PyGammaRAD represents a natural continuation of the open-source philosophy
operating within the Python environment based on a conventional numerical
computing approach, whilst broadening the scope to include the full angular
distribution formalism. An important contribution to the efficient handling
of these coefficients in large-scale computations was made by Rasch and Yu
[60], who presented storage schemes for precalculated Wigner 3-j, 6-j, and
Gaunt coefficients that exploit the full set of symmetries these symbols pos-
sess, achieving retrieval speeds more than an order of magnitude faster than
recursion-based evaluation. A further important contribution in this area was
developed by Johansson and Forssén [61] who demonstrated rapid precision
evaluation of Wigner symbols using prime factorization and multiword inte-
ger arithmetic with an emphasis on couplings of very large angular momenta.
Fast evaluation of Gaunt and Clebsch-Gordan coefficients was also demon-
strated by Xu [69, 70] based on a forward substitution lower-triangle linear
system recurrence algorithm.
The PyGammaRAD implementation described
here does not adopt a precalculation-and-storage strategy; instead, coeffi-
cients are evaluated on demand using the direct sum formulae described in
the subsections below. This approach prioritises simplicity, portability, and
exact arithmetic over raw retrieval speed, and is appropriate for the angular
distribution applications motivating this work where the number of required
coefficient evaluations is modest.
In the strict sense, it is only the Clebsch-Gordan and Racah coefficients
that are needed to evaluate the angular distribution coefficients presented
earlier in Sect 3.1. However, given their close relationship to the Wigner
3-j, 6-j, and 9-j symbols typically used to describe coupling and recoupling
schemes involving angular momentum in a quantum mechanical context, to
broaden the utility of the PyGammaRAD software package, we provide methods

to readily evaluate all aforementioned angular momentum coefficients and
symbols.
