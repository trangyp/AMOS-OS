Abstract. We construct an equivariant E-theory and a Baum-Connes assembly map at the level of
Fell bundles of inverse semigroups over separable C∗-algebras. This generalizes previous work of several
authors, and allows to discuss E-theoretic matters in the context of Cartan pairs; maximal and essential
C∗-algebras of non-Hausdorff groupoids; and Fell bundles over discrete groups and étale groupoids,
among others. In order to do this we establish several functoriality properties for maximal, reduced and
essential cross-sectional C∗-algebras associated with a (saturated) Fell bundle of an inverse semigroup.
This allows to discuss when these algebras give rise to short exact sequences, generalizing the classical
case of discrete groups. We also introduce the adequate notion of “proper” Fell bundle, or “proper” action
of an inverse semigroup, and prove a weak containment property for these. Using these functoriality
properties and these proper actions we then introduce (maximal, reduced and/or essential) equivariant
E-theory by means of adequately equivariant asymptotic morphisms, and construct a Baum-Connes
assembly map that is both natural and reasonably well-behaved.
1. Introduction
When one approaches the classification of (a certain class of) C∗-algebras via K-theoretic means
one immediately runs into the necessity to construct a large class of morphisms between C∗-algebras.
Typically speaking, the first class of morphisms φ: A →B one tackles is that of ∗-homomorphisms.
This notwithstanding, this class is, unfortunately, too small: there are in general many morphisms of
abelian groups K⋆(A) →K⋆(B) that cannot be obtained from ∗-homomorphisms A →B. A collateral
consequence of Kasparov’s phenomenal work [38,42] introducing the (abelian) KK-groups KK (A, B) solves
this problem: the elements of KK (A, B) are homotopy classes of “generalized morphisms” φ: A 99K B.
The original definition of this group, however, is technical and somewhat opaque to new readers, which
is one of reasons Connes and Higson, along with Guentner and Trout [36], introduced the E-theory
groups E (A, B). Elements of this group are, by definition, homotopy classes of asymptotic morphisms
φ: C0 (R) ⊗A ⊗K 99K C0 (R) ⊗B ⊗K, that is, tuples φ = (φr)r∈[1,∞) of set-theoretic maps φr : A →B
that satisfy the ∗-homomorphism conditions when r →∞.
KK-theory and E-theory share quite a lot of properties. For instance, both are universal cohomology
theories in their own way. Moreover, both are covariant in the first variable and contravariant in the
second; and any extension 0 →B →E →A →0 canonically defines an element in KK (C0 (R) ⊗A, B)
and E (A, B). Furthermore, both can be defined even when the C∗-algebras A and B are equipped with
an action of a, say discrete, group Γ ↷A, B. In this setting, there is a so-called “descent functor”
descent: EΓ (A, B) →E (A ⋊max Γ, B ⋊max Γ)
that is a morphism of abelian groups. This arrow sends the class of an equivariant asymptotic morphism
φ: A 99K B to the class of the induced asymptotic morphism φ ⋊max Γ: A ⋊max Γ 99K B ⋊max Γ. Lastly,
both E-theory and KK-theory enjoy a “composition product” (also called Kasparov product), which

approach also immediately yields a well-adjusted machinery to study E-groups for (weak and/or non-
commutative) Cartan pairs, for instance. We refer the reader to Section 8 for a more detailed discussion
about these topics.

from singular foliations [1]. Nevertheless, none of these prove all the necessary ingredients to properly
define the equivariant category, or only work for Hausdorff groupoids, such as [14,47,59]. Moreover, none
of these treat twists, let alone Fell bundles. Lastly, we briefly note in passing that we work exclusively
with equivariant E-theory, and this is not due to a personal choice: see Remark 7.16.

2. Dynamical setup and its crossed products
In this section we introduce the actions and spaces/algebras we study. These notions are clearly
inspired by previous literature, see e.g. [16,18,19,21,30,48]. However, we will generalize most of the
previous work, so it is best we construct all objects “by hand”.
Recall that a T0 space X is locally compact if every point in X has a neighborhood base of compact
sets (compacts subsets that contain x in their interior and intersect to the singleton {x}). For all intents
and purposes the reader is welcome to think of X as Prim (B), which will be the primitive ideal space of
a separable C∗-algebra B. We refer to [11, Corollary II.6.5.7] for a proof that Prim (B) is always (i.e.
regardless of whether B is separable) T0 and locally compact. Moreover, should B be separable (resp.
unital) then Prim (B) is second countable (resp. compact), as shown in [11, Corollary II.6.5.7]. We point
out the following, which is known to experts (and will be used virtually without mention in the sequel).
Lemma 2.1. Any locally compact, Baire, T0 space X is sober, that is, every irreducible closed C ⊆X is
C = {x} for exactly one x ∈X. In particular Prim (B) is sober for any C∗-algebra B.
Proof. For the fact that X is sober see [37] (note that [37] uses the term “point-complete” instead of
sober). Moreover, for the fact that Prim (B) is Baire see [11, Theorem II.6.5.14].
□
Let, for a moment, X be locally compact and Hausdorff. Traditionally Kasparov [42] defined an
X-algebra B as a C∗-algebra equipped with some non-degenerate structure map π: C0 (X) →Z(M (B)),
where the latter is the center of the multiplier algebra of B. This definition, however, does not really
work should one be interested in non-Hausdorff spaces, for C0 (X) does not allow for sufficiently many
discontinuities. This approach was later recovered, changed and expanded in several works, see e.g. [34,55]
and references therein. We particularly follow Gabe’s path in [34].
Definition 2.2 (see [34, Definition 10.7]). Let X be sober. An X-algebra is a C∗-algebra B equipped
with an order preserving structure map ΦB : Open (X) →Ideals (B) from the lattice of open sets of X to
the lattice of ideals in B. The X-structure is continuous if ΦB preserves arbitrary suprema and infima.
In all examples of interest for us we will also have that ΦB(∅) = 0 and ΦB(X) = B. Note that these
would be automatic should the X-structure be continuous. Indeed, recalling that inf ∅is the largest
element of a partially ordered set, namely X or B in our cases, we have that ΦB(X) = ΦB(inf ∅) =
inf ΦB(∅) = inf ∅= B, where the second equality is due to the preservation of infima. One similarly
shows that ΦB(∅) = 0 whenever ΦB preserves suprema.

2.1. Acting dynamics. Having introduced the spaces X on which we will act we now introduce their
dynamical counterparts. Inverse semigroups were introduced independently by Preston [60–62] and
Wagner [77,78] in the 1950’s, as a notion of “local groups”. They have turned out to be quite useful in the
study of C∗-algebras, see [18,19,21,30,31], and many more. We will use them here too. A semigroup S is
called inverse if for all s ∈S there is a unique s∗∈S such that ss∗s = s and s∗ss∗= s∗. Furthermore,
an element e ∈S is an idempotent, or projection, if e2 = e (and thus e∗= e by uniqueness of the adjoint).
The set of idempotents will be denoted by E. Recall that E is commutative [52]. Inverse semigroups are
naturally equipped with a partial order, where we say s ≤t if ts∗s = s (equivalently there is some e ∈E
with et = s, and variants thereof). For idempotents e, f ∈E we have that e ≤f if ef = fe = e.
Definition 2.12 (see [22, Definition 2.5]). An inverse semigroup S is quasi-countable if there is some
countable set K ⊆S such that S = KE (equivalently, S is generated by K′ ∪E as an inverse semigroup
for some countable K′ ⊆S, namely the set of finite words in K).
Our dynamical systems will be given by actions of (often quasi-countable) inverse semigroups on (often
separable) C∗-algebras. We recall these notions now, cf. [17,19,21,48,49], among others.

8.1. Actions of groups. We start by the simplest class, namely Fell bundles over discrete groups.
