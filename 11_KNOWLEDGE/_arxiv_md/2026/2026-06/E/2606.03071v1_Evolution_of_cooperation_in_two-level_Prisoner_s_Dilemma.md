Evolution of cooperation in two-level Prisoner’s Dilemma
Yaroslav Ispolatov1 and Michael Doebeli2
Abstract
We consider continuous Prisoner’s Dilemma played in spatial setting by group-
structured populations. The population dynamics consists of individual-level birth
and death and group-level fission and extinction events. Each individual plays
games with all other individuals within their group, while groups play games against
their nearest neighbours. Payoffs from individual-level games affect birth rates
of individuals, and payoffs from group-level games affect group extinction and
fission probabilities. We show that a certain level of cooperation is maintained
due to specific between-group dynamics even though the within-group evolution
by itself always results in a complete loss of cooperation. The spatial nature of
games and resulting fissioning and extinction events is essential for the evolution
of cooperation: without it cooperation is never maintained. Analyzing various
scenarios of between-group fission and extinction events, we find that higher levels
of cooperation evolve when the selection affecting fission and extinction events is
local rather than global.
Keywords: continuous prisoners’ dilemma, group selection, games in space.

Introduction
Evolution of cooperation has been an important topic in biology and so-
cial sciences [Axelrod and Hamilton, 1981]. Game theoretical methods are
perfectly suited and have been widely used to investigate this problem
[Sigmund and Nowak, 1999, Traulsen and Glynatsi, 2023]. The simplest and
the most extreme example of such games is the Prisoner’s Dilemma, where
individual’s contribution adds to well-being of other players only while the
costs are born by the individual itself [Doebeli and Hauert, 2005]. This makes

to formation of spatially segregated domains [Doebeli and Hauert, 2005]. In
these domains, the contributions of cooperators are shared predominantly
among fellow cooperators, resulting in their higher reproduction or lower
death rates. However, the order of upgrade events, death followed by birth
(DB) vs. birth followed by death (BD), becomes of crucial importance for
the evolutionary outcome in the spatial Prisoner’s Dilemma. In its simplest
form, the DB events are implemented as the replacement of a random player
by a close copy of its best-performing co-playing neighbour. Under a certain
constraints on the benefit and cost functions and the spatial arrangements of
players, the cooperation persists under the DB update [Ohtsuki et al., 2006,
Taylor et al., 2007, Zukewich et al., 2013, Hauert and Doebeli, 2021].
Yet
under the BD update, usually implemented as a replacement of a random
neighbour of the best-performing player by an imperfect copy of that best
performer, the cooperation does not evolve. The reason for this, also known
as the ”cancellation effect” [Akdeniz and van Veelen, 2020], is the inability
of cooperators to ’export’ their cooperation to other regions of the system
[Ohtsuki et al., 2006, Taylor et al., 2007]. Since the best performing players
are those within the domains of cooperators, their neighbours are often co-
operators as well, so the BD events replace the cooperators by cooperators
in the bulk of domains. At the same time, these domains and the interfaces
between the domains of high and low cooperation (defection) are susceptible
to the invasion of defectors [Ohtsuki et al., 2006].

• Individuals play games against their group mates; and groups play

• Individual birth rates depend on payoff in the within-group game (higher

• Payoffs of between-group games depend on the groups’ level of coopera-

• Groups are updated according to payoffs of between-group games. The

groups, replacing itself and the group targeted for extinction.
2
The Model
We study a group structured population where each individual has a level
of cooperation x ∈[0, 1] which does not change during its lifetime. An
individual’s birth rate depends on its expected payoff in the game with all
other individuals in its group, which may change during its lifetime due
to changes in the composition of its group. An individual’s death rate is
proportional to the size of its group, constraining the group size even in the
presense of group-level events.
The groups also play games but only against their neighbours. Each
group’s strategy ¯x ∈[0, 1] is the average of the strategies of all individuals
in the group. Group games are spatially structured: groups are positioned
on a ring and play games with their neighbours to the left and to the right.
Although an individual’s level of cooperation does not change during its
lifetime, groups change in time due to internal individual-level births and

events: a group fissions, breaking randomly into two pieces, which replace
the ancestral group and its neighbour marked for extinction.

The key to the group-level events are the update rules that determine
how to choose the target group and which of the neighbours is replaced.
For single-level games two update schemes are traditionally considered, the
”Death-Birth” (DB) and the ”Birth-Dearth” (BD). In DB the target player
(a group in our case) is chosen randomly and is replaced by a daughter of
its highest payoff neighbour, while in BD the highest payoff group fissions
and replaces its random neighbour. Here we broaden the selection of update
schemes and consider two parametrized families of scenarios:
1. Best-Worst (BW) update. The globally highest payoff group fissions
into the spot of its worst performing neighbour. It adds the selection
between neighbours (the ”Death” choice) to the traditional BD update,
where the choice of a neighbour to be removed is random.

2.3
Simulation
The simulation works as follows. G groups are arranged on a circle and
their number stays constant throughout the simulation. In each group, bin
populations wj are seeded randomly with the same average, so the initial states
of all groups are statistically identical and the average initial cooperation
level is ⟨¯x⟩≈1/2.
The update proceeds with numerical integration of discretized version
of Eq. (6) with a fairly small timestep. The scales β and δ of population
dynamics are chosen to be large, so the group populations relax to their
equilibrium size after initiation or fissioning very fast on the evolutionary
timescale (which is determined by both β and s, or their product βs in the

limit of weak selection). Once every 1/G time units, all groups play games
with their neighbours and their payoffs are computed. This time increment
means that on average each group is updated once per time unit, making
it possible to compare the strengths of within-group and between-group
selections. Depending on the update rules, the target group is selected with
the probability given by (9) or (10), and the neighbour with the lowest or
highest payoff is chosen. It is followed by extinction and fissioning according
to (8). The simulation runs up to time T or until a definite steady state is
reached. The principal values of parameters are summarized in Table 1. Any
changes of parameters, necessary to illustrate the main points of the work,
are described in the text.

3.1
Cooperation disappears due to within-group games
and under the BD between-group update
We first note that in the absence of between-group update, the cooperation
never establishes itself, Fig. 1, left panel. Since the within-group Prisoner’s
Dilemma is played in the well-mixed population, this is a well-known result.
In Appendix we derive that the average cooperation level ¯x, (the first moment

of Eq. (6)) decays to zero with the rate proportional to βs. The same
derivation confirms the well-known fact that the between-group Prisoner’s
Dilemma played without any spatial structure, that is, among all groups, is
equally unable to maintain cooperation.
We then turn on the between-group games and first investigate whether
the group-level BD update results in the maintenance of cooperation. We
find out that even in the absence of within-group selection (s = 0), the
cooperation cannot be maintained, Fig. 1, right panel. This is somewhat
expected because without the within-group games our model is essentially
a one-level process, for which this result is well known [Ohtsuki et al., 2006,
Taylor et al., 2007, Zukewich et al., 2013, Hauert and Doebeli, 2021]. There
is still some novelty in this result as the group fissioning process is not totally
identical to the mutations in single-level games and in principle could have
maintained the cooperation by increasing the between-group variance. Note
also that the more strict targeting of the best group for fissioning (larger
σ) results in a faster decrease of cooperation compared to a more random
(smaller σ) targeting.
3.2
BW update
Consider now the general BW update, where the best group, determined
probabilistically according to (9), fissions and replaces its worst performing
neighbour. In Fig. 2 we show that this allows the cooperation to evolve. The
cooperation persists not only when the within-group games are turned off
(s = 0, left panel), but also in the presense of within-group games (s > 0)
that on their own bring the cooperation level down to zero. Note also that
without within-group games (s = 0) the cooperation level is the highest when
the selection of fissioning group is completely random (σ = 0). However,

panel of Fig. 2). The cooperation persists for a range of within-group selection
strength s and fission randomization parameter r as shown in various panels
of Fig. 2. There appears to be a pattern that relates the group selection
parameter σ, for which the cooperation reaches its maximum value, to s and

3.3
WB update
Under the Worst-Best update, the globally worst performer, chosen according
to (10), is removed and replaced with a fission product of its best-performing
neighbour. The random choice of the group to be removed, σ = 0, corresponds
to the traditional DB update. We find that the cooperation is maintained for
an interval of within-group selection strengths s and the fissioning random-
ization parameter r, Fig. 5. While these results qualitatively resemble the
conclusions for the BW update and even the onset of maintained non-zero
cooperation occurs for approximately the same r and s (panel A), there are
several notable differences. First, the average level of cooperation under WB
update is somewhat lower than that for the BW update, which are also shown
for comparison all panels in Fig. 5 by thin dashed lines. Second, under the
WB update we observe that for all parameters that have we explored, the
highest level of cooperation is achieved when the selection of the target group
is totally random, that is, for σ = 0 (solid black line in all panels in Fig. 5
compared to other solid lines). It is also interesting to compare the level of
correlation between the cooperation level of neighbouring groups introduced
by these two types of updates, Fig. 6. Positive correlations inevitably develop
in both cases due to the local character of replacement of the worst group
by a fission product of its better-performing neighbour. Yet under the WB
update, the strictness of selection of the target group σ does not affect or
even decrease the nearest neighbour correlation, while under the BW update
the correlation increases with σ.
3.4
Discussion
We have explored the scenario of the Prisoner’s Dilemma being played at
two levels: within groups and between groups that are positioned on a circle.
While the within-group games are played between all group members, the
between-group games are played with the nearest neighbours. Despite the
well-known fact that the within-group games bring the cooperation level
down to zero, the between-group games turn the cooperation demise around
and maintain the within-group and system-average cooperation at some non-
vanishing level. For both considered scenarios, BW and WB, the cooperation
is shown to persist for a range of relative strengths of between-group and
within-group games and for various values of the parameter that controls the
randomization during group fissioning. Overall, both types of updates allow

In addition to this general conclusion, another trend is observed that
might constitute a common principle: the global selection is not necessary
and even sometimes detrimental to the evolution of cooperation. In our model,
this selection is controlled by the parameters σ that determines how strict is
the choice of the system-wise best-performing (or worst-performing) group.
When σ = 0, the selection is totally random, while for larger σ, the absolutely
best- (or worst-)performing group is targeted. We observed that for the BW
selection, the highest cooperation level evolves for σ = 0 in the case of weak
within-group selection (small s) or strong fissioning randomization (large r),
while in the complimentary cases the highest cooperation level is achieved for
moderate between-group selection, σ ≈1. Even more so, the totally random
(σ = 0) WB update is always the most beneficial for the cooperation level.
At the same time, the local selection in between-group games is essential for
the evolution of cooperation. The BW update without local selection, that is,
when a random neighbour is replaced by a fission copy of the best-performing
group, is exactly the BD update, well-known to result in zero cooperation
even without the within-group games. Same is true for the WB update when
a random neighbour fission product replaces the worst-performing group. So
the common for both the BW and WB updates principle can be formulated
as that the local competition is vital for the cooperation maintenance, while
the global competition is optional and often unnecessary.
