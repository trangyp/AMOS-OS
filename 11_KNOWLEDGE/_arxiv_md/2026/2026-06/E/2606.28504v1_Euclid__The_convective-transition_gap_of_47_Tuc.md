ABSTRACT
We report the first detection of the ‘convective-transition gap’ (also known as ‘M-dwarf gap’) in the globular cluster 47 Tuc (NGC 104) thanks
to Euclid data. This feature, linked to a change in the physical properties of late-type dwarfs, has remained elusive, with only two detections so
far. Leveraging the large number of stars, high resolution, and photometric precision enabled by Euclid, we detect a statistically significant, sharp
discontinuity in the main-sequence luminosity function of 47 Tuc at IE ≈22.9, which we identify as the convective-transition gap. We compare the
observed properties of the gap in 47 Tuc with theoretical models, showing how the gap can be a powerful diagnostic to probe the internal chemical
structure of globular clusters, and their multiple stellar populations. Following its initial discovery in the metal-poor cluster NGC 6397, the
identification of a convective gap in the metal-rich 47 Tuc suggests that this feature might be more general than previously thought. These results
demonstrate that Euclid can be transformative well beyond cosmology, with impact across multiple areas of astrophysics, including resolved
stellar populations.

1. Introduction
Although globular clusters (GCs) have been studied for cen-
turies, new telescopes can still yield surprising discoveries. One
of the most recent and exciting findings is the detection of the
so-called ‘convective-transition gap’ (hereafter ‘convective gap’
or simply ‘gap’; also known as the ‘Gaia gap’, ‘M-dwarf gap’
or ‘Jao gap’) in a GC (Griggio et al. 2026, hereafter G26). This
gap is an elusive feature present in colour-magnitude diagrams
(CMDs) of resolved stellar populations, which is attributed to
a change in the internal properties (the transition from partial
to full convection, and 3He production and mixing) of late-type
dwarfs (van Saders & Pinsonneault 2012; Baraffe & Chabrier
2018).
The convective gap was first identified by Jao et al. (2018)
using the Gaia Data Release 2 (Gaia Collaboration: Prusti et al.
2016; Gaia Collaboration: Brown et al. 2018) catalogue (see
also Feiden et al. 2021). Their analysis of solar-neighbourhood
dwarfs revealed a narrow, distinct drop in stellar counts along
the lower main sequence (MS) at about 0.35 M⊙. More re-
cently, Marchuk et al. (2026) found a discontinuity in the
MS of the open cluster NGC 2158, which they tentatively
associated with the same mechanism responsible for the gap.
They also suggested that a lack of comparable detections in
GCs – despite numerous Hubble Space Telescope (HST) and
JWST programmes – implied that the gap might be confined
to young- or intermediate-age populations. This hypothe-
sis contrasts with the predictions of Mansfield & Kroupa
(2021), who suggested that GCs are instead ideal systems for
studying the gap, and with the recent detection of the gap
in the metal-poor ([Fe/H] = −1.9; Correnti et al. 2018) GC
NGC 6397 (G26), enabled by the high-precision, wide-field
Euclid (Euclid Collaboration: Mellier et al. 2025) data of the
Early Release Observations (ERO; Cuillandre et al. 2025).
⋆This paper is published on behalf of the Euclid Consortium
Detecting the gap in stellar clusters constrains theoretical
models: since asteroseismology is challenging for low-mass
stars (e.g., Rodríguez-López 2019), the gap morphology may be
the most direct observable of the fundamental physical processes
that govern the interiors and evolution of these objects. Unlike
the heterogeneous Gaia sample, clusters – being composed
of nearly-coeval stars with (almost) the same metallicity – are
ideal benchmarks that simplify the interpretation and theoretical
calibration of this feature. However, with only one statistically
robust finding to date, characterisation of the gap’s underlying
physics remains limited.
This paper reports a clear detection by Euclid of the convec-
tive gap in the GC NGC 104 (hereafter 47 Tuc), a significantly
more metal-rich GC ([Fe/H] = −0.75; Gerasimov et al. 2024b)
than NGC 6397. This suggests the feature might be universal,
having been previously missed only due to data limitations.

KS2 brings two specific features to the analysis: it uses all
images at once to improve the detection of faint sources (which
would be lost in the noise of a single image); and it performs
neighbour subtraction prior to measuring the position and flux
of each given object. The latter step is of critical importance
in crowded environments like GCs. Photometry is obtained by
KS2 in two ways: ePSF fitting; and fixed-position aperture pho-
tometry weighted for the ePSF model. We refer to G26 for
more details (but see also Bellini et al. 2017, for a description
of the implementation of the photometric methods). Empirical
tests have shown that the former method is best with VIS data,
while the latter method is preferred for NISP images. Instru-
mental magnitudes were calibrated in the AB system by com-
puting the magnitude zero-point with the official Level-2 stack
calibrated photometry produced by the official Euclid pipeline
(Euclid Collaboration: Mellier et al. 2025).
KS2 also provides quality parameters, which can be used to
select well-measured stars. In our work, we considered as well-
measured all sources passing the following criteria in both IE and
HE data.
– The star is not saturated.
– The quality of the PSF fit (QFIT) value is lower than the
97.25-th percentile of the distribution at any given magni-
tude. The closer the QFIT is to zero, the better the ePSF fit.
We kept all stars with QFIT < 0.05 and rejected all measure-
ments with QFIT > 0.5.
– The magnitude root-mean square uncertainty σmag is lower
than the 97.25-th percentile of the distribution at any given
magnitude. We retained all sources with a magnitude σmag <
0.025 mag, and discarded those with σmag > 0.25 mag.
– The absolute value of the normalised excess/deficiency of
flux outside the core of the star with respect to what pre-
dicted by the ePSF (hereafter RADXS; Bedin et al. 2008) is
lower than the 90-th percentile of the distribution at any
given magnitude. An object with a |RADXS| < 0.05 is always
included in the well-measured sample, while all sources with
|RADXS| > 0.25 are excluded.
– The rejection rate (ratio between the number of images used
to measure the flux of a source over the total number of im-
ages where the source was found) is lower than 40%.
– The fractional flux within the fitting radius prior to neighbour
subtraction is lower than unity.
– The flux is at least 3.5 σ above the local sky.

5. Convective-gap modelling and properties
The detailed modelling of the complex morphology of the gap
and more detailed quantitative conclusions are left to a future,
dedicated work. Below, we provide a preliminary analysis of the
properties of the gap by comparing the observed LF with theo-
retical models.
5.1. Theoretical modelling of the luminosity function
Our theoretical LF model is based on the grid of model atmo-
spheres computed in Gerasimov et al. (2024b) specifically for
the parameters of 47 Tuc, which were partly adopted from the
literature and partly determined by the authors fitting their mod-
els to the HST photometry of Kalirai et al. (2012). In particular,
we employed the ‘ridgeline’ model grid from Gerasimov et al.
(2024b), tailored to the modal oxygen abundance of the cluster,
which the authors estimated as [O/Fe] = 0.33. All available
models were computed for the metallicity of [Fe/H] = −0.75
and a helium mass fraction of Y = 0.25.
We computed synthetic photometry in the Euclid IE band
using the synphot utility of BasicATLAS (Larkin et al. 2023),
a distance modulus of 13.24 (Chen et al. 2018), an optical
reddening of E(B −V) = 0.04 (Gerasimov et al. 2024b), a
total-to-selective extinction of RV = 3.1, and the reddening
law from Gordon et al. (2023) and Gordon (2024), but see also
Gordon et al. (2009), Fitzpatrick et al. (2019), Gordon et al.
(2021), and Decleir et al. (2022).
We calculated new evolutionary models using Modules
for Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics (MESA) code, version
23.05.1 (Paxton et al. 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, 2019). Our
MESA setup was largely adopted from Gerasimov et al. (2024a);
however, we used the aforementioned model atmospheres from
Gerasimov et al. (2024b) as the outer boundary conditions, as
well as a different mass-sampling algorithm. All evolutionary
calculations were carried out to the target age of 11.5 Gyr.
The stellar mass range near the convective gap exhibits
three distinct regimes of stellar evolution, described in detail
by van Saders & Pinsonneault (2012). At masses below some
threshold value MC, the star remains fully convective at the tar-
get age. At masses above another threshold value MR > MC, the
star arrives at the target age with a stable radiative zone in its in-
terior that separates the convective core from the envelope. The
third regime is found at stellar masses between MC and MR, and
represents a transition phase between those two stable states. In
this transition region, the star oscillates between sustaining a ra-
diative zone and brief episodes of full convection that are known
as the ‘convective kissing instability’.
To choose appropriate initial stellar masses for our MESA
models, we first determined the values of MC and MR by cou-
pling MESA with a bisection root-finding method. We calculated
MC and MR as 0.3317 M⊙and 0.3358 M⊙, respectively. We then
computed our final grid of evolutionary models using the fol-
lowing mass-sampling algorithm. For masses M in the ranges
0.3 M⊙≤M ≤MC, and MR ≤M ≤0.4 M⊙, we used the adap-
tive mass-sampling algorithm from Gerasimov et al. (2024b),
which keeps luminosity differences between adjacent models un-
der 0.005 dex. Within the transition region (MC ≤M ≤MR), the
mass–luminosity relationship is expected to have abrupt discon-
tinuities near stellar masses that experience convective kissing
instabilities near the target age. To capture the full complexity of
this behaviour, we used a dense uniform sampling with the mass
difference between adjacent models of 2 × 10−5 M⊙.
In our MESA models, episodes of full convection associated
with convective kissing instabilities persist for only a single time
step, and are followed by abrupt, discontinuous changes in stellar
parameters. We attempted to resolve these discontinuities by re-
ducing the maximum allowed time step; however, they remained
present even at very small step sizes. Furthermore, requiring the
inter-step variations in stellar parameters to be smaller than those
discontinuities resulted in MESA being entirely unable to evolve
the star past the instability. These numerical difficulties suggest

sensitivity to mass sampling. The adopted selection has the key
advantage of producing a mass–luminosity relationship that is
minimally affected by such artefacts. Our final mass-luminosity

masses were then mapped to IE magnitudes via interpolation of
the theoretical mass–luminosity relationship and subsequently
binned into 100 uniform magnitude bins. The final LF was
obtained by averaging the bin counts across all realisations.

5.2. Physical properties
The gap’s magnitude is expected to depend on chemical compo-
sition through changes in both stellar structure (which shift the
mass at which the gap occurs) and the spectral energy distribu-
tion of the atmosphere (which alters the mapping between the
mass of the gap and its magnitude). We estimate the sensitivity
of the convective-gap magnitude to the bulk metallicity of the
cluster [Fe/H], as well as oxygen-to-iron abundance [O/Fe], us-
ing isochrones from the literature. Isolating oxygen is especially
important, since it is both the dominant metal and exhibits large
star-to-star variations in most GCs (Bastian & Lardo 2018).
We used isochrones from the BaSTI-IAC database to probe the
relationship between the magnitude of the gap and metallicity,
and the isochrones from Gerasimov et al. (2024b, henceforth re-
ferred to as G24 isochrones) to probe the equivalent relationship
with the oxygen abundance.
While BaSTI-IAC isochrones do not sample stellar mass
finely enough to resolve the convective gap directly, they
do capture the associated discontinuity in the slope of the
mass–luminosity relation (Kroupa & Tout 1997). We therefore
define a characteristic magnitude of this feature as the point
where

0.4, and assume that this magnitude is correlated with the
location of the gap. Comparing BaSTI-IAC isochrones with
[Fe/H] = −0.75 and −0.70, we find that the slope discontinuity
(and, by extension, the magnitude of the gap) becomes fainter by
0.031 mag per 0.1 dex increase in [Fe/H].
The G24 isochrones are even more limited in mass resolu-
tion, preventing a direct identification of the slope discontinuity.
To address this, we supplemented the G24 isochrones at
[O/Fe] = 0.33 and 0.48 with new evolutionary models and
synthetic photometry in Euclid filters, computed following the
procedure described in Sect. 5.1. We then identified the mass
bounds of the convective transition, and approximated the mass
of the gap as their midpoint. Interpolating the corresponding
G24 isochrones to the estimated mass of the gap then yielded
the associated gap’s magnitudes. We found that the gap becomes
fainter by 0.026 mag per 0.1 dex increase in the [O/Fe].
We also found that the mass of the gap itself is only weakly
sensitive to chemical composition, varying by < 0.001 M⊙
per 0.1 dex change in either [Fe/H] or [O/Fe]. This suggests
that most of the chemical sensitivity of the gap is due to the
mass-luminosity relationship, not variations in the stellar mass
corresponding to the gap.
The similarity of these two magnitudes suggests that oxygen
may act as the dominant determinant of the IE gap’s magnitude
in 47 Tuc. This contrasts with results for the more metal-poor
GC NGC 6397 (G26), where the influence of oxygen is signif-
icantly weaker than that of the overall metallicity. This result
suggests that, in 47 Tuc, the primary driver of the chemical
sensitivity of the gap may be the spectral energy distribution,
which is strongly affected by light-element abundances (e.g.,
VandenBerg et al. 2012). Such abundance variations are a

magnitude space, then the magnitude and broadening of the
gap will encode information about the star-to-star variations in
chemistry of 47 Tuc (noting that the offset is also sensitive to
photometric zero-points, distance, and reddening). In principle,
this provides a sensitive probe of mPOPs, potentially offering
higher precision than spectroscopic measurements.

the increase in Y could compensate for the effects of

6. Conclusions
The convective gap in the lower MS of 47 Tuc manifests itself
as a clear drop in the present-day LF at IE = 22.954 ± 0.002
(internal errors). This represents the second detection of this
feature in a GC – the clearest to date, enabled by Euclid’s field
of view, resolution, and precision. The Euclid-based analyses
establish the convective gap as a CMD feature identifiable with
very high precision. While the underlying physics requires
further characterisation, the magnitude of the gap has the po-
tential to become a new, more precise standard candle, provided
that standard uncertainties – such as differential reddening and
average metallicity – are adequately accounted for (G26).
We carried out a preliminary analysis of the properties of
the gap by comparing the observed LF with theoretical models.
The gap and its morphology can be important tools to probe
the chemical composition of GCs, in particular metallicity and
light-element abundances. Intriguingly, the gap broadening
and location seem driven by the abundance of light elements,
in particular oxygen, which in 47 Tuc has a significant spread
(Cordero et al.
2014;
Marino et al.
2024;
Gerasimov et al.
2024b; Scalco et al. 2025). In addition, the observed radial
variation of the convective-gap magnitude Igap
E
is remarkably
similar (decrease out to about 20 arcmin and then a constant
plateau) to the radial trend of the fraction of oxygen-poor
stars in 47 Tuc observed by Mehta et al. (2025). These findings
suggest that the gap could be a sensitive tool for investigating
mPOPs with potentially higher precision than spectroscopic
measurements. However, a more detailed modelling is still
needed to better understand this feature.
The convective gap of 47 Tuc provides an important bridge
between the metal-poor gap of NGC 6397 (G26), and the
(super-)solar-metallicity gap of the stars in the solar neigh-
bourhood (Jao et al. 2018). The comparisons shown in Figs. 5
and 6 seem to support our preliminary interpretation of the
gap properties. Indeed, despite uncertainties in photometric
transformations, the gap’s position and slope vary non-linearly
with metallicity, indicating that other factors play a crucial
role in shaping it. Future observations with Euclid will be
instrumental in mapping this subtle feature across a broader
range of stellar populations, further refining our understanding
of the internal structure of low-mass stars.

Appendix A: Artificial-star test
We performed artificial-star tests to assess completeness and ac-
curacy. We generated 107 artificial stars with random magnitudes
that follow an LF with an exponential sampling (i.e., the number
of stars exponentially increase towards the faint-end of the dis-
tribution). We associated random positions selected from either
a flat (like that of field objects) or Gaussian distributions (cen-
tred in the centre of 47 Tuc and with σ of 5000 pixels) with a 1:1
ratio. We fed this list of artificial stars to KS2. KS2 added each
source one at a time to each Euclid image, measured it analo-
gously to real stars, and then removed the object from the images
so as not to bias the measurement of the other artificial stars. Fig-
ure A.1 shows the result of the artificial-star test. This figure is
an analogue of Fig. 2, but using artificial sources. The absence
of any gap in our synthetic CMD confirms that the observed dis-
continuity the LF of 47 Tuc is not an artefact.
We compared the input and output magnitudes of the artifi-
cial stars to search for the presence of systematic errors (as in,
e.g., Bedin et al. 2009; Libralato et al. 2024b). We found no clear
trend, in concordance with the analysis of G26 for the Euclid
data of NGC 6397.
We also estimated the completeness of our data. Figure A.2
presents an overview of the completeness level in the field of
47 Tuc that were used to correct the observed LF (see Sect. 3).
We considered an artificial star as recovered if the input and
output positions agree to within half a pixel, the input and
output magnitudes differ by less than 0.75 mag (i.e., a factor of
2 in flux), and the artificial star passes all photometric criteria
described before. We split the field into seven regions, using the
same radial boundaries used for real stars in Sect. 3. The level
of crowding is important in this data set, and the NISP data of
the centremost region suffer the most from incompleteness. For
this reason, in the analysis of the LF of 47 Tuc, we excluded all
stars within 8 arcmin of the cluster’s centre.
