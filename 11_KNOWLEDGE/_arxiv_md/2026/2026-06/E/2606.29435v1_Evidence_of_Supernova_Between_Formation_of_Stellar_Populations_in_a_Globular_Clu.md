Globular clusters do not undergo conventional chemical evolution driven
by supernova enrichment. Instead, they exhibit unique abundance pat-
terns of the light elements, which cannot be fully explained by any of the
proposed enrichment mechanisms [1, 2]. “Normal” stars of low sodium
abundances comprise the first population of cluster stars, and “enriched”
stars of high sodium abundances, which are found only in globular clus-
ters [3], comprise the second population. Here we show from a differential

line-by-line analysis of stars that span a small range of effective tem-
perature that the globular cluster M92 has higher Fe abundances in
second-population (sodium-enhanced) stars than first-population stars.
The two populations are well separated in Na, Al, and Fe abundances.
The rise in Fe abundance between the first and second stellar populations
suggests that M92 was able to retain at least some supernova ejecta, all
of which exploded after the first population finished forming. This result
provides a lower limit for the time delay between populations.
Until recently, the globular cluster (GC) M92 seemed to conform to the expectation
that GCs do not retain appreciable amounts of supernova ejecta [4, 5]. Like the major-
ity of GCs, it has a dispersion in Fe abundances less than 0.1 dex [6, 7], and it is not
chemically anomalous in its s-process elements [8]. Like all globular clusters, it exhibits
anti-correlations in light element abundances, such as the Na–O anti-correlation [9].
Anomalous GCs, which are defined by star-to-star variation in Fe-peak abundances
and represent about 15% of all GCs [8], rarely show correlation between the abso-
lute abundances of light and heavy elements. The different metallicity populations in
anomalous GCs sometimes show different strengths of light element anti-correlations
[10, 11]. Differential line-by-line analyses in a few GCs show correlations between Na
and Fe-peak elements, such as in the “RGB bump” sample of NGC 6752 [12] and in
M22 [13]. A similar analysis revealed statistically significant dispersion in heavy ele-
ments, including s-process elements in NGC 288 and NGC 362 [14]. M22 and NGC 362
are classified as anomalous from space-based photometry, but NGC 6752 and NGC 288
are not [8]. The implied question is whether even “ordinary” GCs have heavy-element
abundance variations at some level.
The apparent lack of correlation between light and heavy elements in most clus-
ters means that the light element abundance patterns are made before supernovae
explode or that the clusters do not retain supernova ejecta. Proposed sources of
the light element abundance patterns include asymptotic giant branch (AGB) stars
[15], fast-rotating massive stars (FRMSs) [16], and extremely massive stars (EMSs)
[17]. Nucleosynthesis of Fe-peak elements (from core collapse supernovae) is expected
to happen on a shorter timescale than AGB stars. Therefore, explaining the abun-
dance patterns with AGB stars requires that the GCs do not retain supernova ejecta
but do retain AGB ejecta. FRMSs can make the Na–O anti-correlation, and they
have much shorter timescales than AGB stars, but they cannot produce the Mg–Al
anti-correlation seen in many clusters, nor can they explain the small He abundance
variations seen in smaller clusters [18]. EMSs can explain the light element abundance
patterns [17], but they might not be able to explain a different Fe abundance in the
second population of stars than in the first population.
Many theories of GC formation have been proposed, but most of them violate
multiple observational constraints [2]. The most likely path to identifying the source of
the light element abundance patterns is to measure the timescale of production because
AGB stars, FRMSs, and EMSs have such different timescales. Tying the timescale of
light element production to Fe, which is produced on the well-known timescale of core
collapse supernovae, would be illuminating.

Most GCs have uniform compositions of neutron-capture elements formed in the
rapid process (r-process). However, M5, M15, M92, and NGC 3201 have a dispersion
in r-process elements [19–21]. M92’s first-population (1P) stars show a measurable
dispersion in the r-process elements, whereas the second-population (2P) stars do not
[22]. M15 and NGC 2298 have also recently been shown to display star-to-star r-
process variations in 1P but not 2P [23–25]. This pattern is subtle, and it is easier
to find in metal-poor clusters, so it is not clear whether these clusters are unusual or
simply the first to be shown to have r-process variations [26]. The patterns of neutron-
capture abundances in these clusters are distinct from those in the anomalous clusters
that show s-process enrichment, like NGC 288 and NGC 362 [14, 27]. The neutron-
capture abundances in M5, M15, M92, NGC 2298 and NGC 3201 are from r-process
enrichment based on their [Ba/Eu] ratios, as discussed more in the Supplementary
Discussion.
Although prior spectroscopic studies have not shown a variation of Fe-peak ele-
ments in M92, it has been photometrically classified as a “metal-complex” cluster
[28]. Narrow-band photometry in the Ca HK spectral region hints that the stars in
M92 clump into two groups separated in metallicity by about 0.12 dex. However, this
spectral region is sensitive to the abundances of many elements in addition to Fe.
Spectroscopy can definitively identify the elements with varying abundances.

Measurements
In order to further investigate the origin of the abundance patterns in detail, we
obtained high-resolution spectra of 11 stars in M92 and 9 stars in M15. We focus our
discussion on M92. M15 is used as a contrasting case. For example, the fact that we do
not see an obvious difference in Fe abundance between the stellar populations of M15
shows that GCs evolve differently from each other. It is also evidence (in addition to
evidence presented in the Supplementary Discussion) that our results for M92 do not
result from a systematic error.
We chose the sample from a very narrow range of color and magnitude within each
cluster. This approach minimizes systematic differences in the abundance measure-
ments between stars. Color and magnitude at fixed distance roughly correspond to
effective temperature (Teff) and surface gravity (log g), respectively. For stars of iden-
tical Teff and log g, the abundance of an element almost solely determines the strength
of its absorption lines. Figure 1 shows the Gaia DR3 [29] color–magnitude diagrams
of M92 and M15. Figure 2 shows select absorption lines in the spectra of M92 stars.
Their small temperature range allows line strengths to be interpreted as abundances.
We observed all but two of the stars with Keck/HIRES [30] on 2022 Aug 13–14 UT.
We used a 1.148” slit for a resolving power of 45,000 (determined by the smoothing
required to match a synthetic spectrum to the observed line profiles). For M92, we
obtained two exposures of 1080 s for each star in 2022. We observed M92-star-13 on
2024 Aug 14 with two exposures of 1200 s and one exposure of 1320 s. M15 is farther
and fainter, so we obtained two exposures of 1320–1380 s in 2022. We observed M15-
star-4 on 2025 May 9 with two exposures of 810 s each. Methods describes the data
reduction.

To maximize the benefit of choosing stars of similar temperature, we determined
Teff photometrically. We used a calibration based on the infrared flux method [31]
between Gaia GBP −GRP colors and Teff. (Methods discusses the effects of pho-
tometric crowding.) The surface gravity was determined from the relation between
luminosity, temperature, and gravity (see Equation 1 of Kirby et al. 2023 [22]). We
adopted apparent distance moduli of 14.74 and 15.42 for M92 and M15, respectively
[32]. We corrected for reddening assuming E(B −V ) = 0.023 and 0.10 [32], respec-
tively, using the reddening and extinction formulae appropriate for Gaia photometry
[33]. We inferred the luminosity from the absolute, extinction-corrected G magnitude
after applying a bolometric correction [34]. Table 1 gives the names, coordinates,
extinction- and reddening-corrected photometry, signal-to-noise ratio (S/N), Teff, log g,
and microturbulent velocity ξ for each star. The S/N is measured as 0.6745 times
the inverse median absolute deviation from 1 of continuum-normalized flux values in
the range 5715–5795 ˚A, which is relatively free from absorption lines. (The factor of
0.6745 scales the median absolute deviation to the standard deviation for a normal
distribution.) The ranges of Teff and log g are 42 K and 0.06 in M92 and 21 K and
0.06 in M15, respectively.
Measuring absolute stellar abundances is complicated by uncertainties and assump-
tions. The largest sources of uncertainty are the atmospheric parameters, especially
Teff, and the oscillator strengths of the absorption lines. One of the biggest often-used
assumptions is local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE). These effects can be miti-
gated by measuring differential abundances [12–14, 35, 36], where stars are compared
to a reference star of similar parameters. Instead of averaging the absolute abundances
of all the lines of a species, like Fe i, the differences in abundance are computed line
by line, and the differences in abundance for all lines of the same species are aver-
aged. For stars that span a small range of Teff, log g, and metallicity, systematic errors
from stellar parameters, oscillator strengths, and the assumption of LTE are mini-
mized because they affect the same line by about the same amount in different stars.
Therefore, the correlated errors are subtracted away.
We computed the differential abundance of each line by computing the difference
between the abundance of that line and the average abundance of that line in all stars
in the cluster. (Differential abundances are often computed with respect to a reference
star. Taking the difference with respect to the average lessens the effect of noise in
a single reference star.) We use only those absorption lines measured in at least five
stars within each cluster. Tables 2–5 report the differential abundances. Methods gives
more details on the abundance measurements.
Results
The light element abundance patterns define the stellar populations. The populations
in M92 are particularly well defined in Al abundance [37]. The Na–Al plane (Figure 3)
shows two distinct populations. The upper left and lower right quadrants, defined by
[Na/H] −⟨[Na/H]⟩= 0.0 and [Al/H] −⟨[Al/H]⟩= 0.0 are devoid of stars, and there
is gap of 0.450 dex in Na abundance and 0.835 dex in Al abundance. We define the
Na-poor, Al-poor population in the lower left quadrant as 1P and the Na-rich, Al-rich
population in the upper right quadrant as 2P.

The top panel of Figure 3 shows the anti-correlation of Na and Mg abun-
dances. This pattern reflects high-temperature (“advanced”) hydrogen burning. Na
is enhanced in the Ne–Na cycle, and Mg is depleted and Al enhanced in the Mg–Al
cycle. The observed pattern of Mg vs. Na in M92 shows that Mg depletion and Na
enhancement occur in stars with a range of properties, such as burning temperature.
If the abundances were explained solely by a linear combination of a single primordial
(1P) source and a single enriched (2P) source, then the abundances would follow the
dilution curve [2, 38] in Figure 3 (assuming that the diluting gas had uniform compo-
sition). Instead, Mg abundances exceed the dilution curve: two stars have higher Na

abundance than the curve by 4σ. Therefore, the Na–Mg pattern in M92 cannot be
explained by two sources each with a single yield of Na and Mg. It is only in sources
where Na is enriched to its highest value that Mg is depleted.
The wide range of Mg abundances in 2P indicates that it was polluted with
nucleosynthetic sources with timescales that vary by more than the gas mixing time.
Otherwise, the 2P gas would have uniform composition (or at least fall on the dilution
curve). The cluster gas homogenizes over a short time (∼1 Myr). The reason is that
the gas turbulently mixes on a timescale comparable to the cluster orbital or cross-
ing time [39]. The crossing time today is less than 1 Myr, but it might be shorter in
the past, when the cluster was more massive and possibly more compact than today
(tcross ∝R3/M). The cluster undoubtedly has lost gas and stellar mass [40, 41]. It has
also expanded due to tidal interaction with the Milky Way [42]. Therefore, the cross-
ing/mixing time of the cluster was not likely to be much longer than ∼1 Myr. As a
result, the nucleosynthetic sources that span the range of the Na–Mg relation evolve
continuously over many Myr.
Unlike most GCs—even anomalous GCs—M92 shows evidence that its 2P retained
core collapse supernova ejecta. The bottom panel of Figure 3 shows the abundances
of Fe vs. Na. Extended Data Figure 8 additionally shows Ca and all of the other Fe-
group elements. 2P is more enhanced in all of these elements compared to 1P. An
exact permutation test with Monte Carlo resampling (see Methods) shows that the
mean Fe abundance of 2P is higher than 1P, even accounting for the sample size, at
a significance of p = 0.002. Fe in 2P is enhanced by 0.082 ± 0.016 dex (68% C.I.)
compared to 1P. If 2P formed after 1P, then 2P must have acquired Fe-peak elements
from supernova explosions. Another GC that has evidence of retaining supernova
ejecta is Terzan 5 [43], but the [α/Fe] ratio in its 2P is lower than in 1P, meaning that
it retained Type Ia supernova ejecta. The timescale for Type Ia enrichment is so long
that Massari et al. (2014) [44] concluded that Terzan 5 is not a GC at all.
On its own, a dispersion in Fe would not be a highly significant finding. Other GCs
also have Fe-peak dispersions, like NGC 6852 [12] and M22 [13]. Small dispersions in
Fe can be explained by the initial heterogeneity of the gas [5]. Space-based photometric
measurements of abundance have shown metallicity dispersion even within the first-
generation stars [45–50]. The fact that Fe is is higher in every 2P star than every 1P
star suggests that the Fe was generated by 1P and retained by 2P.
The most massive supernovae take ≳3 Myr [51] to explode. Therefore, 2P started
forming at least ≳3 Myr after 1P. The supernovae would produce α elements, such
as Mg and Ca, in addition to Fe-peak elements. Figure 3 shows that Mg is depleted in
2P, whereas Fe is enhanced. Therefore, Mg nucleosynthesis is dominated by advanced
hydrogen burning, whereas Fe nucleosynthesis is done by supernovae.
Some GCs, like ω Centauri [10, 52], show discrete populations. The vast majority
of GCs show a continuum along the Na–O anticorrelation [9]. M92 shows highly dis-
tinct low-Na and high-Na populations, a distinction previously seen in Al abundances
[37]. M92’s 1P shows an immeasurably small scatter in most elements with atomic
number less than 30 (see “Uncertainty estimation” in Methods). Fe is the most pre-
cisely measured element by virtue of its many absorption lines spanning two ionization
states and many excitation potentials. The upper limit on Fe abundance dispersion in

The pattern of Fe in M92 places timescales on the formation of 1P and 2P. The
small scatter in the light elements in 1P indicates that M92 had an initial starburst. 1P
completed its formation before the first high-temperature hydrogen burning sources
and before the first supernovae. 2P did not begin forming until supernovae yielded Fe
and high-temperature hydrogen-burning sources yielded Na and Al.
FRMSs are disfavored sources of high-temperature hydrogen burning in M92
because they produce the light element anti-correlations on the same timescale as Fe.
Therefore, FRMS nucleosynthesis is inconsistent with a large 2P dispersion in light
elements but a small dispersion in Fe. EMSs are disfavored because they make all the
light-element abundance variations in less than 3 Myr [17]. In M92, the Mg abun-
dances continued to evolve over the course of 2P, in which all the stars formed after
supernovae exploded.
AGB stars could plausibly explain some (but not all) of these patterns [15, 53–
56]. The most massive AGB stars, around 8 M⊙, produce Mg-depleted, Na-enhanced
material after about 30 Myr. Then, after 70 Myr, the Mg returns to a near-primordial
enhancement, and the Na returns to near its initial value. In this scenario, the evolution

## Page 14


of the cluster proceeds from the top left of the Mg–Na relation to the bottom right
without forming any stars along the way. Then, the evolution proceeds to higher Mg,
then lower Na, forming 2P stars along the way. Although the existing AGB yield tracks
reproduce the qualitative abundance patterns that we observe, they do not reproduce
the trends in quantitative detail [54]. In any case, AGB stars are still inconsistent with
other GC observations, such as Li abundance [57] without fine-tuning of Li production
by the Cameron–Fowler mechanism [58, 59].
The heaviest elements in most GCs are created in the r-process. The r-process has
a main component, which generates the full range of neutron-capture elements, and a
“weak” component, which produces only the first r-process peak (Sr, Y, and Zr) [60].
The main r-process must be generated in rare sources, such as neutron star mergers
[61] or magnetorotational supernovae [62]. The weak r-process, which is about ten
times as abundant as the main r-process [63], could be produced more commonly in
ordinary core collapse supernovae [64]. Figure 4 shows the weak and main r-process
elements in M92 and M15. We corroborate the discovery of a larger scatter for the
main r-process in M92’s 1P than in 2P [22].
We conclude that the proto-cluster experienced a main r-process event prior to the
formation of 1P. The gas had not mixed evenly before 1P finished forming. The smaller
dispersion in the weak r-process supports that those events (putatively ordinary core
collapse supernovae) could be more common than main r-process events because the
primordial gas cloud(s) that formed M92 was enriched with many sources that had
time to mix well before the 1P stars formed. Scenarios where an r-process event
happened after 1P formed would predict different abundances for stars below the first
dredge-up than above it, which has been shown not to be the case for at least one GC,
M15 [65].
The short gas mixing timescale (∼1 Myr) of the GC indicates that 1P formed in
a nearly instantaneous burst. Otherwise, 1P would have homogenized. The dispersion
in the main r-process contrasts sharply with the very low dispersion in Fe in 1P.
Somehow, the proto-cluster mixed evenly in Fe without mixing in the r-process, or
the proto-cluster formed from separate clouds of gas that had identical Fe content but
disparate r-process content.
Conclusions and Interpretation
Our conclusions about the formation of M92 are as follows:
• 1P formed in a nearly instantaneous burst, lasting ≲1 Myr.
• High-temperature hydrogen burning was not active during the formation of 1P.
• The gas that formed 1P was not well-mixed in main r-process elements, but it was
well-mixed in other elements, including Fe.
• 2P began forming at least ≳3 Myr later, having retained a small amount of core
collapse supernova ejecta. The Fe-peak elements are enhanced only in 2P stars.
• 2P formed over an extended duration, sampling hydrogen burning at a range of
temperatures.
The r-process dispersion in 1P, coupled with the homogeneity of Fe, indicates that
the r-process event happened in the proto-cluster gas cloud. It has been proposed [22]
14


## Page 15


that M92 could have assembled from multiple gas clouds, only one of which experienced
an r-process event. However, the Fe abundances of those clouds would be distinct, and
the resulting 1P would have some Fe abundance dispersion. Similarly, the r-process
could not have come from an external “interloper” source that fortuitously happened
near the GC. Otherwise, there would also be more common interloper sources of
nucleosynthesis, like core collapse supernovae, that would have produced Fe dispersion.
An r-process source in the proto-cloud suggests that there was a population of proto-
cluster stars that pre-dated 1P. This population also would have come with its own
core collapse supernovae, but they would have had to be numerous enough to pollute
the cluster gas evenly with Fe. However, these stars are not seen today. Either the
cluster lost them, or they had a top-heavy initial mass function, such that no stars
from this “zeroth” population are still burning today.
The discovery of supernova ejecta retention in 2P but not 1P was possible through
choosing a narrow Teff range of the stars and through differential, line-by-line abun-
dance analysis. Without this level of precision, M92 appears like most other ordinary
GCs. Therefore, the abundance patterns in M92 might be found in other GCs observed
in the same way. This approach has promise for new discoveries about chemical
evolution of other GCs.

Methods
Photometry and atmospheric parameters.
We determined atmospheric param-
eters from Gaia photometry. Gaia magnitudes are susceptible to photometric crowding
in dense fields. The Gaia archive provides the flux excess, C, in the GBP and GRP
bands, which is often inflated by crowding sources. A corrected flux excess, C∗, can be
computed based on the star’s color. We computed C∗for all our stars. It exceeds 0.05
for two stars, M92-star-8 and M15-star-12, which indicates that the Gaia magnitudes
for these stars could be suspect. These stars are indicated with footnotes in Table 1
and with hollow points in Figures 3, 4, and 6–9.
To estimate the effect of crowding, we also computed color temperatures based on
2MASS infrared magnitudes. We found all of the stars in our sample in the 2MASS
catalog [66]. Crowding also affects 2MASS photometry, and it can be diagnosed by
the “C” flag and by proximity to another bright source less than 5 arcsec. Two stars
had nearest-neighbor separations of 4.6 and 4.7 arcsec (M92-star-8 and M15-star-7).
The C flag is raised for the Ks filter in both of these cases. The Ks magnitude of one
star (M92-star-9) was given only an upper limit.
We diagnosed the effect of crowding by comparing Teff derived from the GBP −GRP
and GBP −Ks colors [31]. The absolute deviation between the temperatures ranges
from less than 1 K to 113 K. The crowded stars (M92-star-8, M15-star-7, and M15-
star-12) have deviations of 61 K, −18 K, and 9 K. We conclude that crowding probably
does not add significant error to the photometric temperatures in excess of the scatter
in the color–temperature relations (83 K for GBP −GRP and 49 K for GBP −Ks).

Data reduction.
We reduced the HIRES spectra with the software package makee
[67]. This set of programs performs flat-fielding, wavelength calibration, sky subtrac-
tion, and echelle order extraction. The final product is a one-dimensional spectrum for
each order. We stitched all of the echelle orders into a single, continuous spectrum, as
described below.
The first step in that process is continuum normalization. We constructed an
approximate model of the spectrum in order to divide out the absorption lines. We
used the same model spectrum for each star because all the stars in the sample have
similar stellar parameters. MOOG was used in conjunction with a model atmosphere
with Teff = 5009 K, log g = 2.11, [Fe/H] = −2.40, and ξ = 1.65 km s−1. It is not
necessary for these parameters to match the spectrum exactly. Each echelle order was
shifted to the rest frame and divided by the model spectrum. Then, the quotient was
fit with a spline with a breakpoint spacing of 500 pixels coupled with ±1.5σ clipping.
The continuum-normalized spectrum is the original echelle order divided by the spline
fit. Each echelle order was interpolated onto a common, logarithmically-spaced wave-
length array. Some of the blue orders overlap in wavelength. The overlapping pixels
were averaged with inverse variance weighting.
We used only one absorption line for potassium: K i λ7699. It is contaminated by
telluric O2 absorption. We removed the telluric absorption following a procedure based
on fitting an adjacent O2 line, scaling the fit by the ratio of oscillator strengths, and
subtracting the O2 line that overlaps K i [22]. We did not estimate the uncertainty
introduced by this subtraction. As a result, our K measurements could have error that
is not represented by our error estimates.
Abundance measurements.
We measured abundances of lines from the line list
of Ji et al. (2020) [68]. We omitted some weak or badly blended lines, including
Na i λ5683, Na i λ5688, Y i λ5200, Y ii λ5206, Eu ii λ4436, Eu ii λ4523, Eu ii λ6645,
and Dy ii λ4450. We measured abundances from a combination of equivalent widths
(EWs) and spectral synthesis. We measured EWs with a custom GUI written in IDL
[22]. Gaussians were fit to the absorption lines using a local determination of the con-
tinuum. We computed abundances with the LTE radiative transfer code MOOG [69].
MOOG’s abfind driver was used to compute abundances from most EWs. The blends
driver was used for Fe-group odd-numbered elements with hyperfine splitting (Sc, V,
Mn, and Co). For the strong Na D lines, the Mg b lines at 5173 ˚A and 5184 ˚A, the
Al iλλ3944, 3962 doublet, and neutron-capture elements with significant hyperfine
splitting or blends (Sr, Y, Zr, Ba, La, Nd, Eu, and Dy), we used spectral synthesis.
(Mg b λ5167 is excluded from the line list due to blending.) We use the solar r-process
pattern for the hyperfine splitting [70]. We measured carbon and nitrogen abundances
from synthesis of the CH 4300 ˚A and NH 3875 ˚A molecular bands. MOOG’s synth driver
was used to compute synthetic spectra in 10 ˚A windows (60 ˚A for CH and 20 ˚A for
NH) around the lines. The line lists for the syntheses were sourced from linemake
[71, 72]. MPFIT found the best fit abundance by minimizing the χ2 of the fit. The local
continuum was re-determined in each iteration of MPFIT by fitting a low-order spline
to the quotient of the observed and synthetic spectra. Extended Data Figure 5 shows
an example of spectral syntheses for one star in M92. The line cores for the strong

Uncertainty estimation.
To estimate abundance uncertainties, we recomputed
the abundances using 1,000 Monte Carlo (MC) trials. In each trial, we sampled the
atmospheric parameters and the EWs from their error distributions. It is computa-
tionally infeasible to recompute a stellar atmosphere model for the exact temperature
and composition of the trial, so we used a precomputed grid [77] of ATLAS9 model
atmospheres.
To determine Teff for a given MC trial, we isolated the random component of the
Gaia color–Teff relation [31] from the systematic component. The random component
is the propagation of the GBP −GRP color through the fitted polynomial relation. The
systematic component determined by Mucciarelli et al. (2021) is 83 K. We assumed
that the systematic component is highly correlated from star to star. Therefore, we
sampled the random component of the Teff uncertainty using a different random num-
ber for each star, but we sampled the systematic component of the uncertainty using
the same chain of 1,000 random numbers (i.e., same random number generator “seed”)
for each star. Below, we show that the assumption of correlation of the systematic
error from star to star does not lead to an underestimate of the uncertainty.
We determined the surface gravity log g by assuming a mass of M = 0.9 M⊙and a
radius from the luminosity and Teff via the Stefan–Boltzmann law. We propagated the
uncertainty from the Gaia G magnitude to calculate the luminosity uncertainty. The
luminosity also requires a bolometric correction [34], which depends on Teff. The Teff
used is consistent within the same MC trial, which properly preserves the correlation
between Teff and log g. We further propagated an assumed error of 0.1 M⊙on the
stellar mass.
We fit for ξ for the abundance measurements, but that would have been computa-
tionally prohibitive for MC trials. Therefore, we used an empirical relation between ξ
and log g [84]. The MC trials account for correlation between these two atmospheric
parameters as well as a random 0.05 km s−1 scatter in the original fit between ξ and
log g.
The atmospheric composition in the ATLAS9 atmosphere grid is characterized by
[Fe/H] and [α/Fe]. We took these values from the abundances measured as described
above, where α is the average of Mg, Si, and Ca abundances. We sampled from uncer-
tainties on [Fe/H] and [α/Fe], which are estimated as the standard error on the mean
of the abundances from the lines of those elements.
The Gaussian profiles fit to the absorption lines also have uncertainties estimated
from MPFIT. Each MC trial samples the EW of each line from a normal distribution
18


## Page 19


centered on the EW measurement with a standard deviation given by the MPFIT uncer-
tainty. Although the atmospheric parameters are correlated with each other within
each MC trial, the EW errors are not.
We test whether the resulting abundance uncertainties accurately represent the
data using the ANOVA F-test. The F-statistic is the ratio of variance among the
population to the average variance of the Monte Carlo samples for each star. A value
of F ∼1 indicates that the measurements have no intrinsic variance beyond the
uncertainties. A value of F ≫1 indicates that there is an intrinsic variance or that the
uncertainties are underestimated. A value of F ≪1 indicates that the uncertainties
are overestimated. Extended Data Table 7 gives the F-statistics in M92 for different
elements where the stars are grouped by population (1P and 2P). Each F-statistic is
accompanied by a p-value, which quantifies the significance of detection of variance in
the population. Small p-values indicate highly significant detections of dispersion.
The F-test simultaneously tests for intrinsic population variance and the rea-
sonableness of error bars. F-statistics should not be much less than one unless the
uncertainties are overestimated. The F-statistic for nearly all Fe-group elements (Sc,
Ti, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, and Zn) in 1P is less than one. Therefore, the uncertainties are
at least as large as the population variance. The only elements in 1P with detected
variances at > 95% significance (p < 0.05) are Si, K, V, and neutron-capture ele-
ments. These are also the elements reported to have a dispersion (σ) in Extended Data
Figures 6 and 8. The K abundances could be affected by telluric contamination, as
discussed in the Supplementary Discussion. The Si and V abundances either have a
systematic error that we have not accounted for, or they have a genuine 1P dispersion.
The F-statistic for most Fe-group elements is higher in 2P than in 1P. This could
indicate a genuine dispersion in those elements in 2P, but the significance exceeds
95% only for Sc and Cr, whose dispersion is driven by one star. The neutron-capture
elements other than Sr also have no detected dispersion in 2P. On the other hand, the
light elements (Na, Mg, Al, Si, and K) have highly significant dispersions in 1P.
Tests of significance of abundance differences between populations.
We
tested the significance of our finding that the abundances of some elements, like Fe,
are different in 2P than in 1P in M92. First, we computed the mean value of the
abundance of an element in 1P for all 1,000 MC trials. We separately computed the
mean of 2P in the same way. Then, we calculated the fraction of those trials had a
mean abundance in 1P greater than in 2P. This number is reported as f(1P > 2P) in
Extended Data Table 7.
Second, we performed an exact permutation test. We assigned five stars (the sample
size of 1P) to “Set A” and the six remaining stars (the sample size of 2P) to “Set
B.” Then, we computed the mean abundance of each set. We repeated this for all 462
combinations of A and B, including the combination where A and B correspond to 1P
and 2P. The significance of a detection of a difference is the fraction of combinations
for which the difference in means between A and B meets or exceeds the difference in
means between 1P and 2P. This value is reported as pperm in Extended Data Table 7.
It cannot be less than 1/462 = 0.002 because the actual 1P and 2P separation are
part of the 462 combinations.

Si was lower than 2P for all the MC trials, the significance from the exact permutation

Supplementary Discussion
Note on relative abundances.
We report abundances relative to their averages
within the cluster. Extended Data Table 6 gives these average abundances. The abso-
lute abundances may be computed from the relative abundances (Tables 2–5 and
Figures 3–4 and Extended Data Figures 6–10) by adding the relative abundances to
the average abundances in Extended Data Table 6. However, the absolute abundances
are subject to all the systematic uncertainties (oscillator strengths, the assumption of
LTE, and more) that we mitigated through the differential analysis.
Further discussion of abundance patterns.
Figure 3 shows only three of the
five light elements that we measured (Na, Mg, Al, Si, and K). Extended Data Figure 6
shows these abundance patterns in two ways: vs. Na abundance and vs. Mg abun-
dance. All of these elements participate in advanced hydrogen burning. At higher
temperatures, Si can be created by proton capture on Al [86]. It might even be possi-
ble at temperatures exceeding 108 K to convert Ar into K [86, 87]. In agreement with
many previous studies on GC light element abundances, we find that Mg and Al are
anti-correlated. Si and Mg are also anti-correlated, as expected. Even K seems to be
anti-correlated with Mg. Ventura et al. (2012) [87] speculated that Ca could be cre-
ated in extremely high-temperature hydrogen burning, but our results show that it is
20


## Page 21


created in the same ratio as Fe. Therefore, the source of Ca in M92’s 2P is supernovae,
not hydrogen burning.
The abundances of Al and K should be treated with some suspicion. We measured
Al exclusively from the Al i λλ3944, 3962 resonance doublet, which is extremely strong.
Furthermore, both lines lie in the wings of the even stronger Ca HK doublet. The
strength of the lines and their unfortunate proximity to some of the strongest lines in
the optical spectrum make it difficult to place the continuum in order to measure EWs.
Our error analysis does not account for possibly large errors in continuum placement.
Likewise, we measured K exclusively from K i λ7699. As described in Methods, it is
contaminated by telluric O2. Our error analysis does not account for the uncertainty
introduced by telluric removal.
We formally detected a dispersion in K abundances, even in 1P. If this dispersion is
real, it could mean that extremely high-temperature hydrogen burning was in place in
the earliest stages of cluster formation, yet it somehow did not affect the abundances
of Na, Mg, and possibly not even Al, though our upper limit on Al dispersion in 1P is
not very restrictive. Given the possibly large systematic uncertainty in K abundances,
we do not consider the K dispersion significant enough to alter our conclusions.
The infrared spectrum is better suited to measuring Al abundances. In fact,
APOGEE spectra show a distinct separation between Al-normal and Al-enhanced
stars in M92 [37]. This result supports our discovery of an abundance gap between 1P
and 2P based on Na abundances.
Extended Data Figure 7 shows differential abundances of C and N. Both of them
are measured with spectral synthesis of molecular absorption. We could not apply
the Monte Carlo technique to estimate measurement uncertainty. The error bars are
those calculated by Equation 2, which does not account for covariance between the
atmospheric parameter error terms.
Extended Data Figure 8 shows Ca and nine Fe-group elements to supplement Fe,
shown in Figure 3. All of their abundances are enhanced in 2P compared to 1P, albeit
with varying levels of significance. None of them except V, whose dispersion is driven
by a single star, show a dispersion in 1P detected at better than 95% confidence.
Neutron-capture elements can be generated in the s-process, r-process, or other
sub-dominant processes. An s-process vs. r-process origin can be diagnosed by the
ratios of neutron-capture elements. The most common diagnostic is [Ba/Eu] because
Eu is made nearly exclusively in the r-process. Extended Data Figure 9 shows the
absolute abundances of [Ba/Eu], based on adding the relative abundances to Table 6
and subtracting the solar abundance pattern [78]. The pure r-process abundance ratio
is [Ba/Eu] = −0.94 [63], based on the solar system abundance pattern coupled with
a chemical evolution model. However, metal-poor stars that are extremely r-process-
enhanced have slightly higher values (e.g., 2MASS J22132050–5137385, [Ba/Eu] =
−0.73 [88]). We measured [Ba/Eu] ≈−0.6 in all stars in M92, regardless of stellar
population, whereas an s-process origin would have [Ba/Eu] > 0. Regardless, the
strength of our study is in relative abundances, whereas [Ba/Eu] must be placed on
an absolute scale to interpret it in the context of s- vs. r-process.
Effect of He abundance.
Helium variation in GCs has been suspected for a long
time as the possible solution to the diversity of horizontal branch morpohologies [e.g.,
21


## Page 22


89]. Spectroscopic measurements [90] and photometric inferences [91–93] show that
He abundances in 2P are higher than in 1P in some clusters.
He abundance can affect the spectroscopic abundance measurements of all elements
in multiple ways. Some of these effects might mimic the small abundance differences
between 1P and 2P that we found for some elements, like Fe. First, He is the second
most abundant element in most stars. Increasing the He fraction necessarily decreases
the H fraction. This effect causes [X/H] to increase when He abundance is increased.
Second, less H means less continuum opacity due to the H−ion. To first order, line
strengths are set by the ratio of line opacity to continuum opacity. Therefore, increas-
ing He abundance (and decreasing H abundance) increases the strengths of modeled
absorption lines. The result would be to decrease abundance to match the observed
line strength. Third, the damping wings of some strong lines are caused by collisions
with hydrogen. Increasing He abundance (and decreasing H abundance) will decrease
the strengths of strong lines and cause inferred abundances to increase. Fourth, the
atmospheric structure changes, generally becoming more transparent with increasing
He abundance. Most lines will weaken, causing the inferred abundances to increase.
The effect of changing He mass fraction on differential abundances in NGC 6752
has been considered extensively [12]. We repeat some of that analysis for M15 and
M92. Whereas the baseline He mass fraction is Y = 0.25, we recomputed abun-
dances for all stars following the procedures above after changing it to Y = 0.28
in the ATLAS9/BasicATLAS model atmospheres. This value is propagated to MOOG so
that both the atmosphere and the synthetic spectrum use consistent values of H and
He abundance. The number density of all species relative to H is adjusted accord-
ingly. Therefore, this procedure accounts for all four effects discussed in the previous
paragraph.
Extended Data Figure 10 compares the differential Na and Fe abundances for the
Y = 0.25 and Y = 0.28 analyses. In the former case, all stars have Y = 0.25. In the
latter case, 1P stars have Y = 0.25 and 2P stars have Y = 0.28. The intent of this
choice is to investigate whether He abundance differences between 1P and 2P could
spuriously cause the observed differences in Fe and other element abundances. As
found previously [12, 14], the effect of varying He is nearly negligible. For example, the
changes in differential Fe abundances between the two helium treatments range from
−0.010 to +0.009, much smaller than the observed difference between the populations.
Independent validation of abundance measurements.
To unambiguously
demonstrate that the [Fe/H] discrepancy between 1P and 2P stars of M92 is genuine
and not an artifact of our analysis, we re-measured [Fe/H] and [Na/H] for all M92 and
M15 stars in this study using an independent method described in this section.
We determined the abundances by fitting forward models to the entire spectrum
of each star redward of 4000 ˚A. The model spectra were synthesized at each iteration
of the fitting process similarly to the second stage of the method of Henderson et al.
(2025) [25]. The atmospheric pressure–temperature profiles used in the synthesis were
generated by interpolating a pre-computed 5D grid of ATLAS9 [76] atmospheres that
spans wide ranges of Teff, log g, metallicity, enhancement of α-elements, and carbon-
to-oxygen ratio. The synthetic spectra were then generated with BasicATLAS/SYNTHE
[81, 94], downsampled to the HIRES instrument resolution with Gaussian convolution
22


## Page 23


and corrected for the flux normalization using a third-order spline. In addition to the
five free parameters of the ATLAS9 grid, the synthetic spectra were also varied in Fe
abundance (independently of the overall metallicity), Na abundance, radial velocity
and instrument resolution. Since purely spectroscopic Teff estimates are sensitive to
NLTE effects, we also included the difference between synthetic photometry and Gaia
GBP −GRP colors of the stars in our goodness-of-fit parameter. The results of our
measurements are shown in Extended Data Figure 11 with open markers.
The [Na/H] measurements shown in the figure are largely driven by the resonance
Na D doublet at λλ5890, 5896, which is significantly stronger than all other Na lines in
our spectral range. However, the strength and profile shapes of the Na D doublet are
strongly affected by NLTE corrections, especially at the low metallicities of M92 and
M15 [95, 96]. Excluding the Na D doublet from the fit would improve the accuracy
of the measurements, but yield very low precision due to the relative weakness of
other Na lines in the spectrum. We analyzed the spectrum of each star twice: with
the Na D doublet included and excluded from the fit. We then fit a linear relationship
to the obtained [Na/H] values, which can be determined accurately due to the much
larger number of stars in the sample (20 stars across both M92 and M15) compared
to the small number of free parameters (2 for a linear relationship). We then used this
relationship as an empirical NLTE correction for our [Na/H] measurements with all Na
lines included. These NLTE-corrected values are shown in Extended Data Figure 11
with error bars.
Our measurements in Extended Data Figure 11 are in qualitative agreement with
the lower panel of Figure 3, and clearly show that in M92, 2P has a higher Fe content
than 1P. In contrast, M15 does not display a comparable trend.
Comment on color temperatures.
Lee (2024) [28] found a difference between
the color temperature scale of Mucciarelli et al. (2021) [31] and evolutionary tem-
peratures, based on stellar luminosities. Lee (2024) argued that this effect spuriously
introduced the apparent spread in r-process abundances detected in 1P [22]. However,
the difference between the two temperature scales in our present sample is only about
25 K at the luminosity of the stars in our sample. Such a small difference would not
translate to a large enough abundance change to explain our results. Furthermore, the
differential nature of our analysis coupled with the small range of luminosities ren-
ders negligible the effect on differential abundances of systematic difference between
temperature scales.
Lee (2024) also surmised that M92 is the result of a merger between two sub-
clusters of different metallicity. Our measurements do not support this hypothesis
because the low-[Fe/H] population is exclusively 1P from the light element abundances,
and the high-[Fe/H] population is exclusively 2P. The sub-clusters would have had to
evolve independently, with one lacking 1P stars, the other lacking 2P stars. We rule out
the merger hypothesis on the basis that there is no known cluster that is exclusively
2P.
