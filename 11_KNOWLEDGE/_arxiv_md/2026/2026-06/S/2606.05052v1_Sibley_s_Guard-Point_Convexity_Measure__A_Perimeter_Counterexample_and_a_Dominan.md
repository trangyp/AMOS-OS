Sibley’s Guard-Point Convexity Measure:
A Perimeter Counterexample and a Dominance Bound
Masahito Nakano
Independent researcher, Japan
math@nakano-masahito.jp
June 2026
Abstract
We study Sibley’s guard-point convexity measure for simple polygons and compare
it with the exterior and perimeter convexity measures. We prove the exterior inequality
G(F) ≤E(F) and disprove the pointwise perimeter inequality G(F) ≤P(F) by an explicit
nonconvex pentagon with G(F) = 62/63 and P(F) = 185/189. Nevertheless, we prove the
uniform bound G(F) ≤2P(F) for every simple polygon. Thus the pointwise perimeter
inequality is false, but the corresponding asymptotic non-domination conclusion remains
true. We also record an auxiliary guard-point-adapted anisotropic perimeter ratio, which
isolates the directional loss in the Euclidean perimeter comparison.

Introduction
Convexity is a binary property, but non-convex sets may be closer to convex in several different
geometric senses. Sibley [2] compared several such measures for polygons together with their
interiors, including an exterior measure, a guard-point measure, and a perimeter measure.
Related questions were later highlighted as Question 2, “Thomas Sibley’s Conjectures,” in
the CCCG 2025 open-problems summary [1]. The present note uses Sibley’s notation and
terminology.

Sibley compares convexity measures not only by pointwise inequalities, but also by an
asymptotic relation called domination. In Sibley’s sense, a measure B dominates a measure A,
written B ≫A, if there exists a sequence of polygons Fn such that

Thus B ≫A means that a sequence may look nearly convex according to B while becoming
extremely non-convex according to A. In other words, the word “dominates” refers to being
close to convex according to B while being far from convex according to A. We write B ̸≫A
for the negation of this relation. Equivalently, G ̸≫P means that no sequence of polygons can
satisfy G(Fn) →1 while P(Fn) →0. The two pointwise inequalities in Sibley’s Conjecture 2,
G(F) ≤E(F),

6
Discussion and further questions
The main point of this note is that Sibley’s pointwise comparisons and Sibley’s domination
comparisons need not coincide. At the pointwise level, the two inequalities associated with
Sibley’s Conjecture 2 behave differently: the exterior inequality G ≤E is true, whereas the
Euclidean perimeter inequality G ≤P is false. At the level of domination, however, the perimeter
side is not reversed by the counterexample: the uniform estimate G(F) ≤2P(F) shows that G
still does not dominate P in Sibley’s sense.
A useful way to view this distinction is through invariance. The quantities G(F) = |K(F)|/|F|
and E(F) = |F|/| conv(F)| are invariant under nonsingular affine transformations, since visibility
and area ratios are preserved. The Euclidean perimeter ratio P(F), on the other hand, is invariant
under similarities but not under general affine transformations, a distinction already emphasized
by Sibley. Thus anisotropic elongations of a fixed polygon can change P while leaving G and E
unchanged. It is therefore natural to formulate sharp comparisons between G and P as suprema
over noncompact shape families, and one should not expect equality or sharpness questions to
be settled only by isolated examples.
The role of the anisotropic perimeter should be understood in this light. The pentagonal
counterexample is not merely a numerical accident; it exposes a mismatch between an isotropic
perimeter and the directional geometry of the guard-point set. The measure G(F) = |K|/|F|
is sensitive to how F surrounds K in different directions, while the ordinary perimeter treats
all directions equally. The support function of K supplies the missing directional weights.
Proposition 1 therefore gives an explanatory correction rather than a replacement for Sibley’s
original perimeter measure: with the guard-point-adapted perimeter ratio,
PK(F)2 ≤G(F) ≤PK(F).
We do not claim here that PK is fully developed as a new convexity measure. It is used as
an anisotropic auxiliary quantity that explains why the Euclidean perimeter comparison fails
and how a pointwise control can be recovered. Its purpose here is explanatory: it identifies the
directional information lost by the ordinary Euclidean perimeter.

Other related directions are the following.
1. Analyze symmetric pentagonal notch families. The present counterexample replaces one hull
edge by two Pythagorean notch edges. A parametrized treatment of this construction may
clarify how far above 1 the ratio G(F)/P(F) can be pushed within this natural family.
2. Describe equality and near-equality cases in the chain proving G(F) ≤E(F). The present
paper records only the equality mechanisms visible in the proof and does not give a full
classification. A sharper stability statement would likely relate the gap E(F) −G(F) to the
deviation of conv(F) from the Wulff shape determined by K(F).
3. Clarify whether PK can be developed into a convexity measure in its own right, and compare
such guard-point-adapted quantities systematically with the measures in Sibley’s domination
diagram.
