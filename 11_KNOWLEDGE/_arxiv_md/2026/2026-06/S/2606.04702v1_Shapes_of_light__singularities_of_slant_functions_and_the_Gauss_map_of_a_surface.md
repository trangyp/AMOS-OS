Shapes of light: singularities of slant functions
and the Gauss map of a surface
Shuhei Honda∗
Yutaro Kabata†
June 4, 2026
Abstract
The slant function on a regular surface in three-space, introduced by
Koenderink, is the inner product of a fixed light direction with the unit
normal; under the standard Lambertian model it gives the brightness of
the surface illuminated from that direction. We study the singularities of
this function and show that their type is characterised by the differential
geometry of the parabolic set and its spherical image under the Gauss
map. At every point of the surface, we first describe the light directions
along which the slant function has a degenerate singularity; such singu-
larities arise only at the parabolic points. We then determine the precise
singularity type at representative parabolic points. At the first-order in-
flection and first-order vertex of Gauss, and at the cusp of Gauss at which
the parabolic set is regular, the finer geometry of the spherical image is
reflected in the singularity of the slant function.
Keywords. Slant function; parabolic points; Gauss map; singularity theory;
cusp of Gauss; inflection of Gauss; vertex of Gauss.

Introduction
In this paper we study the slant function on a regular surface M ⊂R3, following
the terminology of Koenderink [20]. For a fixed unit vector v ∈S2, called the
light direction, the slant function Iv : M →R is defined by
Iv(p) = ⟨v, N(p)⟩,
where N : M →S2 denotes the Gauss map. Although the definition is ele-
mentary, the singularities of Iv capture the local geometry of M and of the
Gauss map. The slant function also has a natural interpretation in computer
vision: under the standard Lambertian reflectance model [15], Iv models the
brightness distribution on M illuminated from direction v, and its level curves

In the present paper, we provide such
a classification and determine the precise relationship between the singularity
type of Iv and the differential geometry of the Gauss map.
It is well known that the parabolic set Σ ⊂M, on which the Gauss curvature
K vanishes, coincides with the singular set of N, and that the geometry of its
spherical image Γ = N(Σ) ⊂S2 reflects finer geometric invariants of M beyond
the Gauss curvature itself [2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 13, 17]. One of the central points of the
present paper is that the singularity type of Iv detects precisely these invariants.
To state the stratification, at a parabolic point p at which Σ is regular, we
let γ denote a local arc-length parametrisation of Σ near p, and κ denote the
geodesic curvature of the spherical curve N ◦γ at p.
• A parabolic point p is called a fold of Gauss if the restriction of N to
Σ is regular at p; equivalently, the height function along N(p) has an
A2-singularity. A parabolic point that is not a fold of Gauss is called a
degenerate singularity of Gauss; a classical example is the cusp of Gauss
[17], at which the height function along N(p) has an A3-singularity.
• Among folds of Gauss, p is an inflection of Gauss if κ(p) = 0, and a
first-order inflection of Gauss if in addition κ′(p) ̸= 0.
• Similarly, p is a vertex of Gauss if κ(p) ̸= 0 and κ′(p) = 0, and a first-order
vertex of Gauss if in addition κ′′(p) ̸= 0.
Remark 1.1. By definition, a cusp of Gauss is a parabolic point at which the
height function along N(p) has an A3-singularity. Note that this condition alone
does not imply that the Gauss map N itself has a cusp singularity in the sense
of A-equivalence [13, 17]. We use cusp of Gauss in its conventional sense.
The spherical image Γ = N(Σ) has been studied extensively from the view-
point of singularity theory [2, 3, 6, 13, 17, 23], and the geometric meaning of
the strata above is reflected in Γ: the cusp of Gauss corresponds to a singularity
of Γ, the inflection of Gauss to a geodesic inflection of Γ, and the vertex of
Gauss to a vertex of Γ, that is, a critical point of its geodesic curvature. The
cusp of Gauss has long been a classical object in singularity theory [2, 3, 17],
and the inflection of Gauss has appeared in connection with certain projection
singularities [2, 13, 17]; see Remark 4.6 for further discussion. The vertex of
Gauss, by contrast, is introduced here for the first time. Our main results show
that these four geometric types are detected exactly by the A≥2-directions of
the slant function.

an Ak-singularity at p. We call v an A≥2-direction at p if Iv has a non-Morse

The existence and types of A≥2-directions are dictated by the differential-
geometric type of the point on M. To describe them, for a parabolic point
p ∈M we call the great circle on S2 lying in the plane spanned by N(p) and
the asymptotic direction at p the asymptotic normal section at p; it coincides
with the set of directions v ∈S2 along which Iv has a critical point at p (a fact
established in Proposition 3.2).
Theorem 1.3. Let p ∈M. The A≥2-directions and A1-directions of the slant
function at p are described as follows.
1. Elliptic or hyperbolic point: No A≥2-direction exists, and the only
A1-direction is the normal direction N(p) (of type A+
1 ).
2. Fold of Gauss: There exist exactly two A≥2-directions, one of which is
the normal direction N(p) (of type A3). The A1-directions are precisely
the remaining directions on the asymptotic normal section at p.
3. Degenerate singularity of Gauss with Σ regular at p: The nor-
mal direction N(p) is the unique A≥2-direction (of type A≥5). The A1-
directions are precisely all remaining directions on the asymptotic normal
section at p.
4. Degenerate singularity of Gauss with Σ singular at p: Every di-
rection on the asymptotic normal section at p is an A≥2-direction; no
A1-direction exists on the asymptotic normal section at p.
5. Flat umbilic point: Iv has a singularity at p for every direction v ∈S2.
Remark 1.4. The coarser distinction between Morse and non-Morse directions
in the above theorem was established in [21] via Hessian analysis. The present
paper refines this by classifying each non-Morse singularity by its precise Ak-
type, yielding a finer stratification.
Our second main theorem completely determines the singularity type of Iv
at each A≥2-direction, for the four first-order representative cases of parabolic
points. In each case, the geometric order of the point—expressed through the
order of vanishing of the relevant invariants—fixes the singularity type uniquely.

Remark 1.6. The phenomena above show that the slant function Iv, despite
its formal resemblance to the height function hv(p) = ⟨v, p⟩, behaves quite dif-
ferently. Both are scalar functions on M defined by an inner product with a
fixed vector v, but they capture different geometry: hv measures the height of
p along v, whereas Iv(p) = ⟨v, N(p)⟩measures the cosine of the angle between
N(p) and v. The singularity theory of height functions on surfaces is a classical
subject [2, 17]. On a generic surface they admit at most an A3-singularity, and
yield a stratification into elliptic (A+

1 ), fold-of-Gauss (A2),

Gauss to a cusp of Gauss, only the odd-index singularities A1, A3, A5 arise; the
even-index types A2 and A4 never occur in this family.

2.1
Surfaces in R3 and the Gauss map
Let M ⊂R3 be a regular surface, locally parametrised by a smooth immersion
g: U →M, (u, v) 7→g(u, v), with U ⊂R2 open.
The tangent plane TpM

N : M →S2 sends each p ∈M to its unit normal N(p) = (gu × gv)/|gu × gv|.
The first and second fundamental forms of M are given as
E = ⟨gu, gu⟩, F = ⟨gu, gv⟩, G = ⟨gv, gv⟩;

the meaning is always clear from context. The Gauss curvature of M is

A point p ∈M is called elliptic, hyperbolic, or parabolic according to whether
K(p) > 0, K(p) < 0, or K(p) = 0. A point with k1(p) = k2(p) is umbilic; in
particular, k1(p) = k2(p) = 0 defines a flat umbilic. The parabolic set of M is
Σ := {p ∈M | K(p) = 0}.
At a non-flat parabolic point p ∈Σ, the second fundamental form has rank 1,
and the unique direction w ∈TpM\{0} on which the second fundamental form
vanishes is the asymptotic direction at p. The set Σ coincides with the singular
set of the Gauss map N, in the sense that the differential dNp fails to have full
rank precisely at p ∈Σ. We refer to the image
Γ := N(Σ) ⊂S2
as the spherical image of Σ.

terminology of [19].) At a fold of Gauss, the slant function admits a non-normal
