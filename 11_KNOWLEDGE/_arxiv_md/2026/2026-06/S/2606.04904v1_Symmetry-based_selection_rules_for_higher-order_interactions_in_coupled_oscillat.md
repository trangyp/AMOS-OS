Symmetry-based selection rules for higher-order interactions in coupled oscillators

Pairwise interactions among general nonlinear oscillators can be reduced, via phase reduction,
to a Kuramoto-type phase coupling sin(−θj + θk). For higher-order interactions, multiple phase
couplings exist—such as sin(−2θj + θk + θl) and sin(−θj + 2θk −θl). Since different nonpairwise
coupling functions produce qualitatively different dynamics, it is important to understand which
phase couplings should be included in coupled phase oscillator models. In this Letter, we establish
selection rules for higher-order phase coupling functions. These selection rules, which can be applied
without the need of explicit phase reduction, are solely based on the symmetry of the isolated
oscillator velocity field and the n-body interaction functions. As phase reduction established the
mechanistic basis for the Kuramoto model, our results provide a theoretical link between physical
systems and higher-order phase models.
Synchronization is ubiquitous in nature, from the col-
lective flashing of fireflies to the coherent firing of neu-
rons [1, 2].
The Kuramoto model has emerged as the
canonical framework for understanding such phenom-

remarkable success at capturing the dynamics of diverse
systems of weakly-coupled nonlinear oscillators [5–9].

physical systems a given phase model represents.
In this Letter, we uncover a symmetry principle gov-
erning the structure of higher-order phase interactions
generated by weakly-coupled nonlinear oscillators.
In-
version symmetry of the isolated dynamics, F (−X) =
−F (X), acts as a selection rule suppressing entire classes
of phase interactions, with the surviving couplings fur-
ther determined by the parity of G. This holds for group
interactions of any size n: the parity of the harmonic
of the focus oscillator is fixed by the parity of G with
respect to Xj. When the symmetry of the isolated dy-
namics, or that of the original coupling, is broken, a
mixture of all phase interactions is generally present.
We prove these rules analytically via Fourier parity con-
straints, validate them across five representative oscilla-
tors spanning neural, chemical, and generic oscillatory
systems, and demonstrate continuous tuning of the cou-
pling type through controlled symmetry breaking in a
modified Stuart–Landau oscillator.

sign principle: to control the type of three-body phase
coupling, one can tune the inversion symmetry of F [56].
To demonstrate this, we modify the Stuart-Landau os-
cillator by adding an inversion-symmetry-breaking term

For c = 0, FMSL satisfies F (−X) = −F (X) exactly, and
the symmetry rules predict a single phase coupling type
depending on G. For c > 0, inversion symmetry is bro-
ken in a continuous and controlled way, introducing even
harmonics into Xc and Z with magnitude proportional
to c (Fig. 4a).

Without symmetry of F or of G in Xj,

also apply to oscillators of any dimension d > 2 because
they are solely based on symmetry arguments that are
independent of oscillator dimension.
These rules also shed new light on the well-studied
pairwise case n = 2. The resonance condition imposes
β = −α, so the coupling reduces to sin(α(θk −θj))[4].
So, for most choices of F and G, the Kuramoto-coupling
sin(θk −θj) dominates, sometimes mixed with the weaker
second harmonic sin(2θk −2θj)[57]. However, the Ku-
ramoto interaction can be completely suppressed if F
and G are odd in Xj, such as G = (xjx2
k, 0).
Thus,
the selection rules provide (non-generic) conditions un-
der which the oscillator’s dynamics are not described by
the Kuramoto model.
These results have a natural physical interpretation.
Odd F arises generically near any supercritical Hopf
bifurcation—from rotational symmetry—or from phys-
ical inversion symmetry, as in van der Pol or mechanical
oscillators [58]. Non-odd F characterizes oscillators far
from onset whose fixed point is displaced from the ori-
gin, as in circadian rhythms [59] and conductance-based
neurons [60], generically activating both coupling types.
This classification provides principled guidance for choos-
ing phase models without full phase reduction.
Three interesting directions remain open. First, our
results apply to systems with explicit higher-order cou-
pling G, not to higher-order terms arising as O(κ2) cor-
rections to pairwise coupling [36, 37, 39], where our
rules may require modification. Second, we focused on
lowest-order harmonics justified by rapid harmonic de-
cay, but higher harmonics may matter for strongly non-
linear oscillators. Third, the resonance condition holds
for nearly-identical oscillators—for more heterogeneous
populations, non-resonant terms παβγ can emerge, allow-
ing phase couplings forbidden by our rules.
As phase reduction established the mechanistic gen-
erality of the pairwise Kuramoto model, the symmetry
rules derived here provide an analogous foundation for
higher-order network dynamics—linking the structure of
phase models directly to the symmetry class of the un-
derlying nonlinear physical system.

High harmonic decay
The amplitude |παβγ| decays rapidly with increasing
harmonic order |α| + |β| + |γ|, which can be quanti-
fied from standard Fourier regularity. If the limit cycle
Xc(θ) is Cp, its Fourier coefficients decay algebraically,
|an| ∼n−p for large n, or exponentially if Xc is ana-
lytic [63]. Since the adjoint equation Eq. (S19) involves
the same smooth periodic coefficients, Z inherits com-
parable Fourier decay, |zm| ∼m−p. For polynomial or
analytic couplings G, the coefficients of P obey compa-
rable bounds, and the convolution in Eq. (12) then yields
|παβγ|∼(|α| + |β| + |γ|)−p (or faster). Thus, the smooth-
ness of the underlying limit cycle controls the spectral
content of the phase coupling: higher harmonics are nec-
essarily suppressed, explaining the numerical dominance
of the lowest terms. We confirmed numerically that this
decay already occurs at low n in the five oscillators we
considered (Fig. S3).
Hence, although many combina-
tions are in principle allowed, this spectral decay justifies
focusing on the lowest-order three-body resonant terms:
sin(θk + θl −2θj) and sin(2θk −θl −θj).

NUMERICAL RESULTS
A.
Numerical phase reduction
Numerically computing the phase reduced equation (3) in the main text requires computing the frequency ω and
the interaction function Π, for which we need to compute the limit cycle solution Xc(θ) and the phase sensitivity
function Z(θ) (Eq. (4) and Eq. (5) in the main text). First, the limit cycle and frequency are obtained through
numerical integration of the evolution equation of an isolated oscillator, Eq. (2) in the main text, with κ = 0. After
a long transient, the system reaches the limit cycle, and we can record one period. Second, the phase sensitivity
function is the periodic solution to the adjoint equation
ω dZ(θ)
dθ
= −J⊤(θ)Z(θ)
(S19)
where J⊤is the transpose of the Jacobian matrix of F evaluated on the limit cycle solution Xc(θ). The phase
sensitivity is then obtained by numerically integrating the adjoint equation backward, imposing the normalization
condition Z(θ) · dXc(θ)
dθ
= 1 [5, 64].
With this numerical scheme, the phase sensitivity function can be obtained with high accuracy. The accuracy can
always be increased by decreasing the integration time step and considering larger transient times to reach the periodic
solution. The precision of the computed phase sensitivity function and limit cycle directly affect the precision of the
phase interaction function Eq. (4) and its Fourier components (12). In this paper, we employ the Euler algorithm
with a time step of δt = 10−4 and a transient of 1000 periods to reach the periodic solution.

Odd F
If the vector field is odd F (−X) = −F (X), then Z contains only odd Fourier harmonics. This, in turn, implies in

All further constraints arise from the symmetries of G. In fact, the parity of index m is entirely determined by the
parity of G with respect to Xj. Indeed, we know from the previous section that if G is odd (even) with respect to
Xj, then, m must be odd (even), because pmβγ = 0 for all even (odd) m. So, m inherits its parity from G, and if G
has not parity, all m are allowed.
Combining this constraint with Eq. (S34), we see that the parity of G in Xj fully determines the parity of α in
παβγ. Indeed,
• if G is odd in Xj: m is odd and hence α is even. The resonance condition further imposes that β + γ is even
too.
• if G is even in Xj: similarly, m is even, and thus α is odd and β + γ is also odd.
• if G has no parity in Xj: m is not constrained, and thus α, β, γ can have any parity as long as their sum
respects the resonance condition.
As seen in the previous section, the parity of G in Xk and Xl imposes additional constraints on the parity of β and
γ. Indeed, oddness (evenness) in any of the two parameters induces oddness (evenness) in the associated coefficient.
Note that the constraints α, β, and γ, inherited from G and from the resonance condition, can be incompatible. In
this case, the coefficient παβγ = 0 because the associated phase coupling function is not allowed.
