Abstract
We study semistable reduction of smooth plane quartic curves over
discretely valued fields, including residue characteristic p = 2. Building
on [33], we use GIT to compute plane models whose special fibers are close
to stable, providing a practical route from an explicit equation to stable
reduction in the non-hyperelliptic case.
We prove that a smooth plane quartic admits a unique GIT-stable
plane model if and only if its stable reduction is non-hyperelliptic. When
this holds, the stable model is obtained from the GIT-stable plane model
by a canonical local modification supported at the cusps of the special
fiber.
This description isolates the geometric mechanism behind the
hyperelliptic/non-hyperelliptic dichotomy. The explicit cusp-resolution
step is carried out in the companion paper [39] and is implemented in [31].

At this point we encounter a crucial dichotomy. In the first case, the plane
model X0 is actually GIT-stable. For plane quartics, this means that X0,s is
reduced and has at most nodes and cusps as singularities (plane singularities of
type A1 or A2). By the general theory, X0 is then the unique GIT-semistable
model of XL. We show that, after enlarging the extension L/K further, there
exists a modification
π : X →X0
such that X is the stable model of XL. In fact, π is an isomorphism except over
the cusps of X0,s, and the exceptional divisors are precisely the 1-tails of the
stable curve Xs.
The existence of the modification π : X →X0 is a consequence of the
Semistable Reduction Theorem; in fact, X is the stable hull of X0, see [22].
What is needed for computations is an explicit construction of π and of the finite
extension required for its existence. This second stage—the explicit resolution
of the cusps of X0,s by weighted blow-ups, producing the 1-tails of the stable
special fiber—is the subject of the companion paper [39].
Implementation. The algorithms of the present article and of [39] are imple-

In the second case, X0 is GIT-semistable but not GIT-stable and then our
method fails. Here we show that this happens if and only if X has hyperelliptic
reduction, i.e. the special fiber Xs of the stable model lies, as a point on M3,
in the closure of the hyperelliptic locus. In this case, the relationship between
GIT-semistable plane models and the stable model is considerably more subtle,
and it is unclear how our basic strategy can be adapted. We hope to consider
this problem in a future article.

1.1
Basic definitions
In this section we fix an algebraically closed field k.
Definition 1.1
(i) An algebraic curve C over k is called semistable if it is
connected, projective, reduced, and its only singularities are ordinary
double points.

(C, S) stably marked if 2gi −2 + ri > 0, for all i.

Definition 1.3 A stably marked curve (C, S) is called hyperelliptic if there

This section summarizes the main results from [29] regarding the geometric
and combinatorial properties of hyperelliptic stable curves, which are necessary
for our main theorem. We refer the reader to [29, Chapter 2] for the detailed
proofs and full exposition, as the details will be published in a separate article.
To classify the nodes of a hyperelliptic stable curve, we first introduce the
following combinatorial terminology.
Definition 1.5 (Combinatorial Terminology) Let C be a semistable curve
over k.
(i) A node p is called a separating node if the partial normalization of C at p
disconnects the curve.
(ii) A pair of distinct nodes {p, q} is called a separating pair if the partial
normalization at {p, q} disconnects the curve, while normalizing at either
p or q individually does not.
(iii) The curve C is called inseparable2 if it has no separating nodes, and
separable otherwise. It is called 2-inseparable if it is inseparable and has
no separating pairs, and 2-separable otherwise.
(iv) The curve C is of semicompact type if every node p is part of at most one
separating pair.
With these definitions, we can classify the nodes of a hyperelliptic stable
curve based on the action of the hyperelliptic involution.
Proposition 1.6 ([29, Proposition 2.18]) Let (C, S) be a hyperelliptic sta-
bly marked curve with hyperelliptic involution σ, and let π: C →T be the
quotient map. Any node p ∈C falls into one of the following three mutually
exclusive categories:

(i) Separating Node. A node p is a separating node if and only if it is a
fixed point of σ at which σ preserves the local branches. In this case, its
image π(p) is a node of T, and σ respects the decomposition of C induced
by p.
(ii) Part of a Separating Pair. A node p is part of a separating pair if
and only if it is not a fixed point of σ. In this case, its image under the
involution, p′ := σ(p), is the unique node that forms a separating pair
{p, p′} with p. Both points map to a single node π(p) = π(p′) in T, and σ
respects the decomposition of C induced by the pair {p, p′}.
(iii) Non-Separating Fixed Node. A node p is a non-separating fixed node if
and only if it is a fixed point of σ at which σ interchanges the local branches.

its image π(p) is a smooth point of T, and the irreducible components of
C forming the node p are either identical or both rational.

the following lemma.
Lemma 1.7 ([29, Lemma 2.28]) Let (C, S) be an n-marked, 2-inseparable,

(i) C is irreducible.

are no marked points (n = 0).

1.3
Classification of stable curves of genus 3
We will now restrict our
attention to stable curves of genus g = 3. It is well known that there are precisely
42 different combinatorial types of stable curves of genus g = 3. We refine this
classification using the notion of the core and 1-tails, shedding some new light
on the distinction between hyperelliptic and non-hyperelliptic curves.
Definition 1.10 Let C be a stable curve over k, and let E be an irreducible
component of C.
(i) E is called a 1-tail if it is attached to the rest of the curve at a single node
and has an arithmetic genus of 1.
(ii) An elliptic tail is a 1-tail that is smooth (i.e., has geometric genus 1).
(iii) A pigtail4 is a 1-tail that has a single node (i.e., has geometric genus 0).
The node at which a 1-tail E is attached is called the attachment point of E.
For stable curves of genus g = 3, a simple argument using the additivity of the
arithmetic genus shows that separating nodes are in one-to-one correspondence
with 1-tails. More precisely:
Lemma 1.11 ([29, Lemma 2.34]) Let C be a stable curve over k of genus 3,
and let r be the number of 1-tails of C. Then 0 ≤r ≤3. The curve has exactly
r separating nodes, which are precisely the attachment points of the 1-tails. The
partial normalization of C at these nodes results in the disjoint union of r 1-tails
and an inseparable curve Cc of genus g(Cc) = 3 −r.
This observation motivates the following definition.

from Lemma 1.11 the core of C.

Plane models of smooth quartics
In this section we discuss a different notion of (semi)stability of curves. It
only applies to plane curves and is directly derived from Mumford’s Geometric
Invariant Theory (GIT).
2.1
Plane curves
Let k be a field. By a plane curve of degree d over k we
mean a hypersurface
X = V+(F) ⊂P2
k,
where F ∈W := k[x, y, z]d, F ̸= 0, is a homogeneous ternary form of degree
d. We consider such plane curves up to projective equivalence. More formally,
we identify the isomorphism class of the plane curve X with the orbit of the
homothety class [F] ∈P(W) under the natural action of PGL3(k).
Geometric invariant theory (GIT) distinguishes among orbits of PGL3(k) on
P(W) according to their stability properties (see [25]). An orbit may be stable,
semistable, or unstable, where stable implies semistable, and unstable simply
means not semistable. We also use strictly semistable meaning semistable but not
stable. For our purposes we do not need the general definitions, since in the case
of plane quartics the distinction can be expressed by a simple geometric criterion
(see Proposition 2.3 at the end of this subsection). To avoid confusion with the
notion of stable curves from §1, we shall say that a plane curve X = V+(F) ⊂P2
k
is GIT-stable (resp. GIT-semistable, etc.) if the orbit of [F] has the corresponding
property.
Before stating the geometric criterion for GIT-(semi)stability we recall some
basic terminology for plane curve singularities. Let X ⊂P2
k be a plane curve and
P ∈X a closed point. We say that P is an n-fold point of X if the multiplicity
of X at P equals n. In particular, an ordinary double point (or node) is a double
point whose tangent cone consists of two distinct lines.
