Abstract
We compare two existence principles for maximal ideals, a classical
one for vector lattices with a strong unit and a second, newly intro-
duced one for vector lattices with a strong truncation. Although the
latter strictly generalizes the former, we show that the two statements
are equivalent over ZF set theory.
2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. Primary: 06F20; 46J20.
Key words and phrases. disjoint complement; maximal ideal; strong truncation;
strong unit; vector lattice

Introduction
Following Ball [1], we call a truncation on a vector lattice X any unary
operation ∗on the positive cone X+ satisfying
x∗∧y = x ∧y∗
for all x, y ∈X+.
In [3], such a truncation is said to be strong if, for every x ∈X+, there exists
λ ∈(0, ∞) such that (λx)∗= λx. A basic example arises when u is a positive
strong unit of X. In this case,
x∗= u ∧x
for all x ∈X+
defines a strong truncation on X. Hence, every vector lattice with strong
unit admit a strong truncation. The converse fails in general; for example,
C (Y ), where Y is a non-compact locally compact Hausdorff space, admits
plenty of strong truncations but has no strong units.
Let X be a vector lattice with a strong unit u. Since no proper ideal of
X contains u, the union of any chain of proper ideals is again a proper ideal.
Hence, by Zorn’s Lemma, X admits a maximal ideal. The same argument
yields the classical Krull’s Theorem, asserting that every commutative ring
with identity has maximal ideals. Despite this formal similarity, the under-
lying logical strength of these results differs substantially. Indeed, Krull’s
Theorem is equivalent to Zorn’s Lemma [2, 5], whereas its vector lattice
analogue is equivalent to the strictly weaker Ultrafilter Theorem [4].
This naturally raises the question whether a maximal ideal principle per-
sists in the broader setting of vector lattices equipped with a strong trunca-
tion. As we shall see below, the answer is affirmative. Although the class
of vector lattices with strong truncation is substantially larger than that of
vector lattices with a strong unit, the following two statements are equivalent
in ZF set theory.
(T) Every vector lattice with a strong truncation contains a maximal ideal.
(U) Every vector lattice with a strong unit contains a maximal ideal.
The proof is not entirely straightforward.
In the absence of a strong
unit, one lacks a canonical reference point on which the usual maximality
arguments rely. In particular, Zorn’s Lemma plays no role here, neither in a
technical nor in a conceptual sense.

The proof of the equivalence
In what follows, X denotes a real vector lattice. In order to make the note
reasonably self-contained, we first recall from [7] the terminology, notation,
and results that will be used in the proof of the main theorem.
A vector subspace J of X is called an ideal if, for every x, y ∈X, it
follows from |x| ≤|y| and y ∈J that x ∈J. It is clear that any ideal of X is
itself a vector lattice with respect to the operations and order inherited from
X. The disjoint complement (or the polar) of an element y ∈X+ is defined
by
y⊥= {x ∈X : |x| ∧y = 0} .
It is easily checked that y⊥is an ideal of X. On the other hand, the smallest
ideal of X containing y is given by
Jy = {x ∈X : |x| ≤λy for some λ ∈(0, ∞)} .
The element y is said to be a strong unit if X = Jy.
Let J be an ideal of X, and let J (x) denote the equivalence class of any
x ∈J in the quotient vector space X/J. Accordingly, we use the symbol
J to refer, depending on the context, either to the ideal J itself or to the
canonical projection from X onto X/J. A natural order can be defined on
X/J by putting
J (x) ≤J (y)
if and only if
x ≤a + y for some a ∈J.
Under this order, X/J becomes a vector lattice, and J is a lattice homomor-
phism., i.e., a linear map preserving absolute value. It is quite straightforward
to verify that if J is a (maximal) ideal of X/J, then its preimage
J−1 (J ) = {x ∈X : J (x) ∈J } .
is a (maximal) ideal of X containing J.
We now prove our main theorem.
Theorem 1 The axioms (T) and (U) are equivalent.
Proof. The implication (T) ⇒(U) follows immediately from the observation
in the introduction that any positive strong unit induces a strong truncation.
Let us prove the converse implication (U) ⇒(T).
