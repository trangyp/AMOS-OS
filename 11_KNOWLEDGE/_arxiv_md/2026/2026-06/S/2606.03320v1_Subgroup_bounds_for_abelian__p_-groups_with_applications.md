1. Introduction
The number of subgroups of a finite p-group is one of the most natural arithmetic invariants one
can attach to it, and one of the more stubborn to describe. The point of departure of the work
reported here was our desire to have, for a finite p-group, an upper bound for this number that
responds to some of the internal structure of the group and not merely to its order. For groups
whose nilpotency class is less than p such a bound is within reach; the route to it, due to Groves,
passes through an associated abelian p-group and so throws the whole weight of the problem onto
the abelian case. It is the abelian case that we settle here.
Let p be a prime, and let A be a finite abelian p-group; we write s(A) for the number of subgroups
of A. By the classification theorem, A is uniquely of the form
Aλ = Cpλ1 × Cpλ2 × · · · ,
where λ = (λ1, λ2, . . .) is a partition. Formulae for the number of subgroups of each given type go

The proof of Theorem A proceeds in three steps. First, once Delsarte’s formula is rewritten with
q = p−1, the leading power of p in each summand separates from a bounded factor in q; the
largest power that occurs is pη(λ), attained by exactly θ(λ) summands, so the lower bound and
the asymptotic for fixed λ are immediate. The whole difficulty lies in the upper bound, that is,
in controlling the normalised sum that remains. Here everything turns on a single observation: a
Ferrers diagram is not the product of its blocks of constant height, yet the monotonicity conditions
that tie adjacent blocks together may, once the sum has been normalised, simply be dropped,
since they only constrain a sum of nonnegative terms. The problem is thereby reduced to a single
homocyclic block. The estimate for such a block is obtained by decomposing Ferrers diagrams into
successive Durfee squares; this yields the constants ξh(q). These three steps, namely reading off the
leading term, estimating one homocyclic block, and decoupling the blocks of a general diagram,
occupy Sections 2–5.
The sections that follow give three applications of Theorem A. Section 7 treats groups of nilpotency
class less than p, where Groves’s comparison theorem reduces subgroup counting to the abelian
group determined by the logarithmic Ω-profile; Theorem A then gives the corresponding bound.
Section 8 fixes the shape λ and lets the base integer vary; the result is a multiplicative arithmetic
function whose diagonal summatory function is governed by η(λ) and θ(λ). Section 9 returns to
a single prime and reads off leading coefficients for abelian p-groups of fixed rank. We close, in
Section 10, by recording three problems that we have been unable to settle.

10. Open problems
Theorem A and the three applications above leave several natural questions open. We single out
three that we find appealing, and that we have not been able to settle.
Question 10.1. The lower bound of Theorem A records only the leading term θ(λ)pη(λ), whereas
the upper bound carries the homocyclic block constants ξh(q) and so responds to the height of each
column block. Is there a lower bound for s(Aλ) of comparable precision, sensitive to the shape of λ
and equipped with explicit block constants of its own?
Question 10.2. The comparison theorem of Section 7 is, by its very hypothesis, silent at the
prime 2, since a 2-group of nilpotency class less than 2 is already abelian. Likewise, for p-groups of
exponent p it gives only the elementary abelian comparison group, and hence no shape-sensitive
refinement. Can one obtain meaningful subgroup bounds for these two classes?
Question 10.3. Groves’s reduction requires the nilpotency class to be less than p. Is there anything
useful to be said, in the spirit of Theorem A, about the subgroup count of a p-group of arbitrary
nilpotency class?
We would be glad to see progress on any of the three.
