SELF-INVERSE LINEAR SUBSPACES OF MATRICES
RODICA ANDREEA DINU AND MARTIN VODIˇCKA
Abstract. We study linear subspaces of matrices whose inverse spaces are also linear.
Based on the fact that any linear space containing the identity matrix and whose inverse
space is linear must be a self-inverse space, we introduce such spaces as self-inverse spaces.
In fact, as we will show, self-inverse spaces are finite-dimensional complex unitary Jordan
algebras. We provide an algebraic classification of all self-inverse spaces of small type
and a classification of small-dimensional self-inverse spaces up to isomorphism.
1. Introduction
The structural study of matrix subspaces is a cornerstone of modern linear algebra, with
important implications for algebraic geometry, quantum information and algebraic statistics.
While a matrix A is classically known as involutory if A = A−1, this paper shifts the
focus toward a more global structural property: the characterization and classification of
linear subspaces of matrices L such that the set of inverses of all regular matrices within L,
denoted L−1, also forms a linear space.
Our investigation establishes a fundamental connection: any linear space L containing the
identity matrix and satisfying the condition that L−1 is a linear space must be a self-inverse
subspace, i.e., L = L−1. Based on this fact, we call such spaces self-inverse spaces. We
establish key structural and algebraic properties of these subspaces, demonstrating that they
are closed under matrix powers and a symmetric product operation. As we will show, self-
inverse spaces are precisely Jordan subalgebras of the Jordan algebra Cn×n which contains an
identity. We also refer the reader to [4] and [2]. We proved that there is a close relationship
between self-inverse spaces and the root multiplicities of the minimal polynomials of their
elements. By using this fact, we provide a classification of all self-inverse spaces of small type
and a classification of all small-dimensional self-inverse subspaces up to an isomorphism.
The motivation for this study is deeply rooted in [9], where Bernd Sturmfels poses critical
problems regarding the degree of the inversion map and the geometry of spaces where the
inverse space is also linear.
In algebraic statistics, a central problem in Gaussian graphical models is the relationship
between the covariance matrix Σ and the concentration matrix K = Σ−1, see, for e.g. [4] and
[10]. Statistical constraints often require K to lie within a specific linear subspace L defined
by the absence of edges in a graph, see [6] and [4]. A self-inverse subspace represents a
symmetric statistical scenario where both the covariance and concentration matrices belong
to the same linear space (i.e., K ∈L ⇒K−1 ∈L). Such models provide scenarios where
the Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) exhibits unique algebraic properties, such as a
rational ML estimator or a specific ML degree.
By classifying self-inverse spaces, we are essentially finding the building blocks of these
statistical models. For instance, the space of symmetric 2×2 matrices (S2) and the space of
upper-triangular 2 × 2 matrices (U2) identified in our dimension 3 classification, correspond
to specific constraints in statistical interactions. Understanding these subspaces allows for
a clearer view of how internal matrix symmetries (captured by the minimal polynomial)
translate into global model constraints.

When the matrices are symmetric, a study of linear spaces whose inverse space is a linear
space was done by Bik, Henrik, and Sturmfels in [2]. A structure result for Euclidean Jordan
algebras (so, over R) appeared already in 1934, due to Jordan, von Neumann, and Wigner in
[5]. Results that generalize their work are due to Zel’manov, in the case of simple and prime
Jordan algebras, but in infinite dimensions [11, 12, 13]. In addition, Jordan algebras are
related to matroids: they can be viewed as a nonabelian generalization of partition matroids,
i.e., direct sums of uniform matroids, [2, Remark 3.7].
This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we establish the foundational lemmas,
proving that any linear space containing the identity with a linear inverse space is closed
under all non-negative matrix powers, ultimately showing that such spaces are self-inverse.
In fact, some of these properties appear in a different shape in the literature; we refer here
to [4] and, for symmetric matrices, [2]. However, our approach is different, and we present
them for the completeness of the article.
In Section 3, we study several properties of self-inverse spaces of matrices in relation to
Jordan algebras. It is already known a decomposition result for Jordan algebras, namely
the so-called Peirce decomposition, [7]. However, the uniqueness of the decomposition is not
that clear in the literature and we prove in Theorem 3.11, the fact that there is a unique
way to write a Jordan algebra as a product of its irreducible Jordan subalgebras.
In Section 4 we extend the concept of minimal polynomial for matrices to self-inverse
spaces and, implicitly, to Jordan algebras. In Theorem 4.2, we prove that the spaces gener-
ated by two matrices are isomorphic if and only if their minimal polynomials have the same
number of roots with the same multiplicities, which shows that the minimal polynomial
of a matrix plays an important role in understanding the self-inverse spaces of matrices.
Based on this fact, we introduce an algebraic variety defined by fixed minimal polynomial
types. In Section 5, we fix the type of the Jordan algebra, hence the type of minimal poly-
nomial of its generic element. We give a classification of self-inverse spaces of small type.
Section 6 provides a comprehensive algebraic classification of low-dimensional self-inverses
spaces. Specifically, we identify exactly one unique space in dimension one, two spaces in
dimension two (in Theorem 6.1), six spaces in dimension three (in Theorem 6.2), and six-
teen spaces in dimension four (in Theorem 6.5). Finally, we propose several open problems
regarding higher-dimensional classifications, the finiteness of these spaces, and their orbits
under the action of the general linear group.
2. Linear spaces of matrices with linear inverse space
We start the study of linear spaces of matrices with linear inverse spaces by proving several
foundational lemmas. They will lead to a generalization of [2, Theorem 1.1] corresponding
to the symmetric case matrices.
Lemma 2.1. Let f ∈C[x] be a polynomial of degree k and let R = C[x]/(f). Assume
that a1, . . . , ak ∈C are different numbers that are not roots of f. Then (x −a1)−1, (x −
a2)−1, . . . , (x −ak)−1 generate R as a vector space. The inverses are considered in the ring
R.
Proof. It is sufficient to show that these elements are linearly independent. Assume
α1(x −a1)−1 + α2(x −a2)−1 + · · · + αk(x −ak)−1 = 0
for some αi ∈C. We can multiply this equation by (x −a1)(x −a2) . . . (x −ak) and obtain
α1(x −a2)(x −a3) . . . (x −ak) + · · · + αk(x −a1)(x −a2) . . . (x −ak−1) = 0.
Note that the polynomial on left-hand side is of degree k −1, therefore this equation
holds also in C[x]. Thus we may plug in x = ai to obtain αi = 0 which proves the linear
independence.
□

linear space. For any A ∈L and any k ∈N, we have Ak ∈L.

Lemma 2.3. Let L be a linear space of matrices such that Id ∈L. Assume that L−1 is a
linear space. Then L = L−1.
Proof. Let A ∈L be a regular matrix and let R be the vector space generated by all non-

Hence, we can conclude that L−1 = L.

Theorem 2.4. Let L be a linear space of matrices that contains at least one regular matrix.
Then the following are equivalent:
(1) L−1 is a linear space,
(2) AL−1A = L for some matrix A ∈L,
(3) AL−1A = L for all regular matrices A ∈L,
(4) AB−1A ∈L for all regular matrices A, B ∈L.
Proof. Clearly (3) ⇒(2) and (4) ⇒(3).
Since multiplication by a regular matrix is a
bijective linear map, it follows that (2) ⇒(1). We are left with the implication (1) ⇒(4).
Let A be a regular matrix from L. Then the space L′ := A−1L is a linear space. We
have (L′)−1 = L−1A which is a linear space. Moreover Id ∈L′. By Lemma 2.3, we have
L′ = (L′)−1. Thus, for any regular matrix B ∈L we have
B−1A = (A−1B)−1 ∈(L′)−1 = L′ = A−1L.
Multiplying by A from the left, we obtain AB−1A ∈L, what we wanted to show.
□
Definition 2.5. We call a linear subspace of matrices self-inverse if L−1 = L and Id ∈L.
From the proof of Theorem 2.4, it can be seen that any linear space L that satisfies
the condition that the space L−1 is linear can be written in the form AL′ where L′ is a
self-inverse space. Thus, from now on, it makes sense to consider only self-inverse spaces.
Theorem 2.6. Let L be a linear subspace of matrices that contains the identity. Then the
following are equivalent:
(1) L is self-inverse,
(2) Ak ∈L for all k ∈N, A ∈L,
(3) A2 ∈L for all A ∈L,
(4) AB + BA ∈L for all A, B ∈L.
Proof. Clearly (2) ⇒(3).
To prove (3) ⇒(4), one notices that for A, B ∈L we have
(A + B)2 −A2 −B2 = AB + BA ∈L.
For (4) ⇒(2), one can proceed by mathematical induction. For k = 1 the statement
holds. Now assuming that Ak ∈L by substituting B = Ak into condition (4), we obtain
2Ak+1 ∈L, proving the statement.
(1) ⇒(2) holds by Lemma 2.2.
We will finish the proof with the implication (2) ⇒(1). Assume that A ∈L is regular.
A−1 is a polynomial in A, therefore A−1 ∈L. Thus, L−1 ⊂L, which implies that L−1 = L,
since they both have the same dimension and both are irreducible.

3. Jordan algebras
In this section, we will make connections between self-inverse spaces and Jordan algebras.
Definition 3.1. A Jordan algebra over field k is a vector space J over a field k equipped
with a bilinear product star : J × J →J that satisfies
X ∗Y = Y ∗X, ((X ∗X) ∗Y ) ∗X = (X ∗X) ∗(X ∗Y )
for all X, Y ∈J.
It is a standard result that the set of all n × n matrices with the operation X ∗Y =
1
2(XY + Y X) forms a Jordan algebra (see for e.g., [7, 4]). In fact, this construction works
for any associative algebra.
Theorem 3.2. Self-inverse spaces are precisely Jordan subalgebras of the Jordan algebra
Cn×n, which contains an identity.
Proof. Follows from Theorem 2.6.
□
Thus, when we speak about self-inverse spaces, we may instead consider them as Jor-
dan algebras. For example, we can ask if two self-inverse spaces are isomorphic as Jordan
algebras.
Self-inverse spaces are, therefore, finite-dimensional complex Jordan algebras with a unity
element (the identity matrix). For a unitary Jordan algebra J, we will denote by IdJ, its
unity element.
The following lemma is trivial to check, but it will be important later.
Lemma 3.3. For any regular matrix P the conjugation A 7→PAP −1 is an automorphism
of the Jordan algebra Cn×n.
The next result is known as Peirce decomposition ([7, Part II, Chapter 8]):
Theorem 3.4. (Peirce Decomposition) Let L be a Jordan algebra. Let I ∈L have the
property that I ∗I = I. Then the two vector subspaces:
• FI := {A ∈L : A ∗I = A}
• ZI := {A ∈L : A ∗I = 0}
are both unitary Jordan algebras. Moreover, we have FI ∗ZI = 0. In addition, the space
FI ⊕ZI is a Jordan subalgebra of L.
Let HI := {A ∈L : A ∗I = A/2} be a vector subspace. Then
L = FI ⊕ZI ⊕HI.
Moreover, HI ∗HI ⊆FI ⊕ZI and HI ∗(FI ⊕ZI) ⊆HI.
Proof. Even though it is a well-known result, we will sketch the visualization of the proof
in the case where L is a subalgebra of Cn×n.
Since I2 = I, we may assume that I is a diagonal matrix with only 1 and 0 on the diagonal
(otherwise, due to Lemma 3.3, we can get to this case by conjugation). Let us assume that
the first m entries are equal to 1 and the rest n −m entries are 0.
The set FI is formed by all matrices from L that have non-zero entries in the left upper
m × m corner. Clearly, this set is closed under the operation ∗. Moreover, we can see that
IdFI = I.
Analogously, the set ZI is formed by all matrices from L that have non-zero entries only
in the right bottom (n −m) × (n −m) corner. This set is also closed under the operation
∗. It follows that ZI is a Jordan algebra with identity element Id −I.

Definition 3.5. We say that a Jordan algebra L is irreducible if it is not isomorphic to a
product of two non-trivial self-inverse subspaces. That is L ∼= S ×T implies S = 0 or T = 0.
Otherwise, we call it reducible.
Remark 3.6. Usually, in the literature, it is called a direct sum and not a product of two
Jordan algebras. In the case of finite-dimensional Jordan algebras, it is clearly the same.
However, sometimes we want to differentiate between the direct sum of underlying vector
spaces and the direct sum of Jordan algebras. For this reason, we prefer to speak about the
direct sum of vector spaces and about the product of Jordan algebras.
Every Jordan algebra can be written as a product of irreducible Jordan algebras. The
next statements will tell us when this is possible.
Theorem 3.7. Let L be a Jordan algebra and S, T be subalgebras such that L = S ⊕T as
a vector space. Then φ : S × T →L given by φ(A, B) = A + B is an isomorphism if and
only if A ∗B = 0 for all A ∈S, B ∈T.
Proof. (⇐): We have
φ((A1, B1))∗φ((A2, B2)) = (A1 +B1)∗(A2 +B2) = A1 ∗A2 +A1 ∗B2 +B1 ∗A2 +B1 ∗B2 =
A1 ∗A2 + B1 ∗B2 = φ(A1 ∗A2, B1 ∗B2) = φ((A1, B1) ∗(A2, B2)).
(⇒): For any A ∈S, B ∈T we have 0 = (0, B) ∗(A, 0) = (0 + B) ∗(A + 0) = A ∗B which
implies that A ∗B = 0.
□
Theorem 3.8. Let L be a Jordan algebra and let I ∈L be an element with the property
I ∗I = I. Then φ : FI × ZI →L given by φ(A, B) = A + B is an isomorphism if and only
if FI ⊕ZI = L.
Proof. (⇐): Clearly FI ∩ZI = 0. Thus, if the spaces L and FI ×ZI have the same dimension,
we must have L = FI ⊕ZI.
(⇒): By Theorem 3.4, we have A ∗B = 0 for any A ∈FI, B ∈ZI. Thus, by Theorem
3.7, the map φ is an isomorphism.
□
Theorem 3.9. Let L be a unitary Jordan algebra. Suppose that L ∼= S × T for two Jordan
subalgebras S and T. Then there exists an element I ∈L, I /∈{IdL, 0} such that I ∗I = I
and FI ∼= S, ZI ∼= T.
Proof. Clearly, S and T must also be unitary. Consider an element I := (IdS, 0) ∈S × T.
It is easy to check that I ∗I = I and FI = S × {0} and ZI = {0} × T.
If φ : S × T →L is an isomorphism, then clearly I := φ(I) is the element that satisfies
the condition.
□
The following result is a consequence of Theorem 3.8 and Theorem 3.9.
Corollary 3.10. Let L be a Jordan algebra. Then L is isomorphic to a product of two
non-trivial Jordan subalgebras if and only if there exists an element I ∈L with the property
I ∗I = I and HI = 0.
The following result, we show that there is a unique way to write a Jordan algebra as a
product of its irreducible Jordan subalgebras.

Remark 4.3. Note that Lk1,...,km ∼= Lk1 × · · · × Lkm.
We have seen that the minimal polynomial of a matrix plays an important role in under-
standing self-inverse spaces of matrices. More specifically, the essential data captured by a
minimal polynomial is found in its degree and multiplicity of roots. Thus, for k1 ≥· · · ≥km,
it makes sense to define the following:
Mn
k1,...,km = {A ∈Cn×n : minimal polynomial of A has m different roots
with multiplicities k1, . . . , km}.
It is easy to see that Mn
k1,...,km is an algebraic variety since all conditions are of polynomial
type. Sometimes (mainly when we speak abstractly about self-inverse spaces), we will omit
the upper index n. We assume that this index is taken appropriately, i.e., it is the same as
the size of matrices in our self-inverse space.
We say that a matrix A is of type (k1, . . . , km) if its minimal polynomial has m differ-
ent roots of multiplicities k1, . . . , km. Hence, a generic matrix from Mk1,...,km is of type
(k1, . . . , km).
We can extend this concept even into finite-dimensional unitary Jordan algebras. For
an element X ∈J, is it polynomial well-defined, so also every element has its minimal
polynomial. By Jk1,...,km we denote the closure of the set of all elements in J with the
minimal polynomial of corresponding type. Again, it is an algebraic subvariety of the vector
space J.
Since J is a union of all such sets and a vector space is irreducible as an algebraic variety,
it follows that there exist k1, . . . , km such that Jk1,...,km = J. This means that a generic
element in J is of this type, which is a generic type of minimal polynomial for the Jordan
algebra J.
5. Classification of self-inverse spaces of small type
In this section, we will classify all self-inverse spaces (or unitary Jordan algebras) up to
dimension four. In the literature, there is a general classification, but only for simple Jordan

6.1. Dimension 1. Since we require that a self-inverse space contains the identity, there is
only one one-dimensional self-inverse space, and that is the space generated by the identity.
6.2. Dimension 2.
Theorem 6.1. There exist (up to isomorphism) only two different two-dimensional self-

7. Future directions
We classified all self-inverse spaces up to dimension four. It is clear that if we increase the
dimension, a similar analysis will get much more complicated. Maybe it can be simplified, or
at least one can find an estimate on the number of different self-inverse spaces. We propose
the following questions:
Question 7.1. Classify all the different self-inverse spaces in higher dimensions.
Question 7.2. Let us fix the dimension d.
Is it true that there are only finitely many
d-dimensional self-inverse spaces (up to isomorphism)?
Question 7.3. If the answer to the previous question is positive, how many d-dimensional
self-inverse spaces are there? If the precise answer is too difficult to obtain, can one find at
least an asymptotic behaviour of the number of self-inverse spaces?
