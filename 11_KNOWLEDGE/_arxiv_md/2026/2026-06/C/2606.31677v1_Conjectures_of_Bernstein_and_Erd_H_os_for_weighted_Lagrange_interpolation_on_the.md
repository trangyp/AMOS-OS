CONJECTURES OF BERNSTEIN AND ERD˝OS
FOR WEIGHTED LAGRANGE INTERPOLATION
ON THE HALFLINE WITH EXPONENTIAL WEIGHTS
SZIL´ARD GY. R´EV´ESZ AND PATR´ICIA SZOKOL
Abstract. Let I = [a, b] and consider the degree n Lagrange interpolation at the nodes x,
where x ∈S := {x = (x0, x1, . . . , xn) : a = x0 < x1 < . . . < xn = b}. Then the norm of the
Lagrange interpolation operator L : f →L(f) ∈Pn, (where Pn is the space of real algebraic
polynomials of degree at most n), is the maximum of the Lebesgue function L(x, t) on I.
Bernstein conjectured that the norm of L becomes minimal exactly for node systems which
exhibit an equioscillation property in that the interval maxima mk(x) := max[xk−1,xk] L(x, ·)
(k = 1, . . . , n) are all equal. Erd˝os added to the conjecture the sandwich property that if y is
an extremal (minimal norm) system, then for any other node system x there have to be indices
i, j with mi(y) < mi(x) and mj(y) > mj(x).
The conjectures were proved by Kilgore and de Boor–Pinkus in 1978. Since then, analogous
results were obtained only for a few cases when interpolation is made to certain very special
spaces of polynomials (instead of Pn), or when we apply weighted interpolation with rather
special weights. Worse than that, it turned out that published proofs of results on infinite
intervals and weighted interpolation were seriously flawed.
Here we prove the Bernstein and Erd˝os Conjectures for the case of exponentially weighted
polynomials on the halfline. This is the first proof of these conjectures in a situation where,
contrary to all existing successful proofs, we encounter singularity of certain derivative matrices.

1. Introduction
The aim of the present paper is to establish an appropriate form of the Bernstein and Erd˝os
Conjectures for exponentially weighted polynomial interpolation in the halfline.
The original Bernstein Conjecture was formulated for algebraic polynomial interpolation on
a finite interval [a, b] over a century ago [2]. It proved to be a hard problem – cracking it lasted
for half a century. In the meantime, Erd˝os [7, 8] proposed an additional conjecture, furnishing
yet another challenge. In the classical setting of unweighted algebraic polynomials on a compact
interval, both problems were solved in the seventies of the last century [18, 6, 17].
After the successful solution of the classical case, it naturally occurred to investigate the
analogous questions for weighted interpolation in weighted spaces of continuous functions. For
definiteness, let us formulate these questions concretely. Let an interval I be given with a say
continuous and positive weight function w : I →(0, ∞), and consider the space Cw(I) := {f ∈
C(I) : ∥f∥w := ∥fw∥∞< ∞}.

So the quest for the optimal, i.e., minimal norm of the Lagrange interpolation operator boils
down to the task of minimizing the maximum of these interval maxima with an appropriate
choice of the node system vector x.
Now the adapted version of the Bernstein Conjecture [2] is that this optimization happens
exactly when these interval maxima are all equal, which we will term as equioscillation property,
or that the interval maxima equioscillate. Similarly, the appropriate form of the Erd˝os Con-
jecture [7, 8] states that if x is an optimal, i.e., equioscillating node system, then the interval
maxima mi(y), belonging to any other node system y, will “sandwich” the optimal Lagrange
interpolation norm m1(x) = · · · = mn+1(x); that is, there exist indices i, j such that both
mi(y) < mi(x) and mj(y) > mj(x) hold.
For the classical case of unweighted interpolation the breakthrough was achieved by Kilgore,
who showed the Bernstein Conjecture [18], and came close to the Erd˝os Conjecture as well,
although this was recorded immediately following him by de Boor and Pinkus [6]. Moreover,
the paper of de Boor and Pinkus contained several important further advances, one being the
proof of an even stronger property, which we will call here intertwining. This property says
that for any two node systems x ̸= y we necessarily have two indices i, j with mi(y) < mi(x)
and mj(y) > mj(x), brutally generalizing the Erd˝os Conjecture, which formulated this only
when one of the node systems (i.e., x) is the optimal one.
In the following decades attempts were made to extend the solutions to various spaces of
weighted polynomial interpolation or interpolation into spaces of various more general features
like incomplete polynomials etc., see [20, 19, 21, 22]. These proved to be difficult for the original
argument used several ways the special feautres of algebraic polynomials in the form of they
forming a Chebyshev-Haar System1, or admitting the celebrated Markov theorem about the
interlacing of roots of oscillating polynomials inherited by the roots of their derivatives. In
all these degree calculations and cancellation by factors played key roles. Moreover, all these
general investigations were based on the same approach in that the key step, ever since Kilgore
and de Boor-Pinkus, was the nonsingularity of the derivative matrices

property of the difference mapping” was inspired by successful application of the approach in
other contexts, see for example, [9] and [12]. We are certain that it will play a crucial role if
further settings will be treated.
We should record here that our approach to the equioscillation and minimax questions is
also rooted in another, independent and nice discovery of Fenton, who formulated for sums of
translates of concave kernels a general equioscillation theorem [14]. Developing his idea with
the work of Kilgore [18] and de Boor-Pinkus [6] in mind, we could prove very general minimax
and equioscillation results in contexts where nonsingularity was not available [10, 11, 13]. In
the case of periodic functions, there is still another predecessor of the approach, due to Kendall,
Hardin and Saff [16], and further developed in [9]. However, in all these works nonsingularity
could be circumvented because we had other strong structural properties available. One was
concavity of the appearing kernels, and it was also important that the summands in the sum
corresponding to the |hk(x, t)| in the Lebesgue function (3) here, all were univariate, depending
on just the difference t −xk with the respective xk. This allowed to apply certain univariate
techniques, which of course cannot be used here.
This is the first proof of the conjectures of Bernstein and Erd˝os in a setting where nonsingu-
larity is not available. Therefore, we hope that the methods are capable of extending to other
situations, where the same obstacle – possible singularity, i.e., attaining the interval maxima at
endpoints – excludes the standard approach. This motivates us in formulating several auxiliary
results in a more general setting. This was already started by Kilgore [19], who in particular
proved an elegant proposition about linear independence of certain functions with interlacing
zeros. We will make good use of this elegant proposition as well as the Markov-type inheritance
theorems of Milev and Naidenov [25] for exponentially weighted polynomials.

2. General lemmas for Extended Chebyshev-Haar Systems
A vector space of functions W := Wn is a rank n + 1 Extended Chebyshev-Haar System –
to be shortened as ECHS henceforth –, if its dimension is n + 1, and the only function f from
the family and having n + 1 zeros – counted according to multiplicities – is the identically zero
function 0. In this part of the preliminary analysis we will consider an almost arbitrary ECHS,
which, however, will be assumed to have suitable differentiability properties, as need be.
Most of the time we will assume Wn ⊂C2(I) and will count multiplicities of zeros up until
3: so for f ∈Wn and z a zero of it the multiplicity of the zero is 1 if f ′(z) ̸= 0, is 2 if
f(z) = f ′(z) = 0, but f ′′(z) ̸= 0, and is 3 if f(z) = f ′(z) = f ′′(z) = 0. Our notion of ECHS
will refer to this zero multiplicity count – for a similar convention see, e.g., [25]. (In general,
when we do not want to assume higher order differentiability of the functions of our system,
then we can neither interpret higher order multiplicities.)
Our model study in this direction is the work of Kilgore and Cheney [17]. However, our main
concern here will be the removal from the set of conditions the standing assumption of [17] that
1 ∈W. As is exposed in [28], this innocent-looking assumption is a very crucial one, lacking of
which causing collapse of the “standard approach” via nonsingularity of (6) to the Bernstein
and Erd˝os Conjectures. So, here our aim is to save what can be saved from the analysis of [17],
and to generalise, extend and push further them towards the particular needs in our approach.
The results obtained here will be essentially used throughout, from results on derivatives of
the interval maxima functions mi in Section 4 to connectedness of the “regular set” X and
continuity of the “separator function” ϕ in Section 8.

corresponding statement appears in [24, Corollary 3]. In Bojanov’s original setting, however,
both functions were assumed to be oscillating.

lating while the other is only nearly oscillating. This yields the corresponding extension of the
Markov-type theorem for weighted polynomials associated with a general C1 weight, which in
