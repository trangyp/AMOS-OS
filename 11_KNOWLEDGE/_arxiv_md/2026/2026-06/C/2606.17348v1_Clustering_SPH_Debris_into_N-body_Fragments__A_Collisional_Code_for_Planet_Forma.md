Clustering SPH Debris into N-body Fragments: A Collisional Code for Planet Formation
Samuele Crespi,1 Mohamad Ali-Dib,1 and Ian Dobbs-Dixon1
1Center for Astrophysics and Space Science (CASS), New York University Abu Dhabi, PO Box 129188, Abu Dhabi, UAE
ABSTRACT
Giant impacts among planetary embryos generate long–lived debris that feeds back on late-stage
terrestrial planet growth, yet most N-body models either assume perfect merging or treat fragments
in ad hoc ways. We present SHARD, a collision–resolution framework that couples a hybrid integrator
(REBOUND/mercurius) to a six-dimensional catalog of smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH)
outcomes spanning impact speed, geometry, total mass (up to 2M⊕), mass ratio, and target/projectile
water fractions. For each detected impact we multi-linearly interpolate to return the two largest rem-
nants with self-consistent kinematics and volatile budgets, and we reconstruct the unresolved fragment
population by aggregating nearby SPH debris snapshots and compressing them with mass-weighted
k-means in velocity space into a tractable number of fragments above a tunable minimum mass. Exact
conservation of total mass and water mass is enforced across survivors and debris, with immediate,
energy-based reaccretion checks performed within the timestep. Debris interpolation is constrained
to the tabulated SPH grid (no extrapolation), and special handling of hit-and-run and catastrophic
regimes is included. We benchmark our code against SyMBA in a Mercury-formation experiment and
find broad qualitative agreement, though our final embryos distribution is dynamically hotter and more
top-heavy in mass. The benchmark outcome suggests that debris clustering is dynamically consequen-
tial: fragment resolution controls reaccretion, damping, and volatile retention. This SPH-anchored
debris treatment provides a drop-in, compositionally aware alternative to perfect merging, enabling
late-stage accretion studies that retains fragment feedback without saturating the integrator.
Keywords: Planet formation — Terrestrial planets — Planetary dynamics
1. INTRODUCTION
The final assembly of terrestrial planets proceeds through a sequence of giant impacts among Moon– to Mars–sized
planetary embryos embedded in a dynamically excited inner disk (Chambers 2001; O’Brien et al. 2006). As collisions
accumulate, a non-negligible fraction of the solid inventory is converted into a spectrum of fragments spanning dust to
lunar sizes. These fragments are neither instantaneously re-accreted nor negligibly small; their long-lived dynamical
and collisional processing can reshape the growth history and final architectures of rocky worlds (Agnor & Asphaug
2004; Leinhardt & Stewart 2012a).
In particular, imperfect merging outcomes such as hit-and-run collisions are
expected to be common— Kokubo & Genda (2010) find that nearly half of embryo–embryo impacts result in hit-
and-run events—implying that perfect accretion is an over-simplification of late-stage growth. A central challenge for
predictive models is therefore the physically faithful treatment of collisions and post-collisional debris within N-body
simulations that, by necessity, cannot resolve all size scales (Kobayashi & Dauphas 2013).
Modern N-body studies commonly adopt one of four classes of collision solvers. The historically prevalent Perfect
Merging scheme treats every impact as a perfectly inelastic merger, an assumption now known to shorten formation
timescales and overestimate final planet masses and water budgets (Chambers 2001; Agnor & Asphaug 2004). More
realistic approaches allow for fragment production. Unresolved Debris methods, for example, preserve the two largest
collision remnants and deposit the remaining mass into one or more tracers (massless or low-mass particles) to represent
a cloud of debris (Levison et al. 2012; Chambers 2013). Same-Mass Debris prescriptions distribute the fragmented mass
into numerous equal-mass super-particles guided by scaling laws for collisional outcomes (Chambers 2013), ensuring

In this paper we develop and implement SHARD (SPH-Hydrodynamic Accretion and Remnant Debris): a colli-
sion–resolution scheme that maps N-body impacts onto smoothed-particle hydrodynamics catalogs and statistically
reconstructs the unresolved fragment population through mass-weighted k-means clustering to provide a framework
that explores the dynamical and compositional consequences of debris production during late-stage planetary accre-
tion. Our work builds upon a catalog (Sch¨afer et al. 2016, 2020) of smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH) collision
outcomes spanning the dominant region of parameter space for embryo–embryo impacts (Benz & Asphaug 1994; Mon-
aghan & Pongracic 1985; Sch¨afer et al. 2016). This enables a statistical characterization of the mass, size, and orbital
distributions of post-impact debris as functions of total mass, mass ratio, velocity, and geometry, and anchors de-
bris prescriptions directly to hydrodynamic physics rather than purely empirical extrapolation (Leinhardt & Stewart
2012b).
2. METHODS
2.1. Overview and numerical units
We model late-stage planetary assembly with a hybrid N-body integrator that resolves pairwise impacts using
outcomes calibrated to smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH) experiments, and that replaces unresolved collision
debris by a small number of self-consistent fragments obtained via mass-weighted clustering in velocity space. The
implementation is built on top of REBOUND (Rein & Liu 2012) with the hybrid mercurius integrator (Rein et al.
2019), and leverages REBOUNDX (Tamayo et al. 2020) to carry passive per-particle parameters (notably the bulk
water fraction, wf). All quantities are expressed in REBOUND units with G = 1, distance in AU, mass in M⊙, and
time in yr/2π.2
The code tracks bodies gravitationally, and these are split into active and test particles via the N active parameter
in REBOUND. Collisions are identified with the direct geometry, and close encounters are handled by switching
locally to the high-accuracy IAS15 integrator. The collision resolution module queries an SPH outcome catalogue
to (i) return the two largest post-impact bodies via multi-linear interpolation across the SPH grid (Sect. 2.4) and
(ii) reconstruct the velocity distribution of small fragments by aggregating neighbouring SPH debris snapshots and
clustering them into a prescribed number of fragments (Sect. 2.5–2.6). Throughout, we enforce mass and water-mass
conservation as described below.
2.2. Integrator configuration and outputs
By default, we adopt mercurius with Hill switching factor hfac = 5 and global time step ∆t = 10−2 × 2π (i.e.
0.01 yr); during close encounters the minimum IAS15 step is restricted to 10−4 ∆t. Collisions are handled by a custom
resolver collision solver. Every checkpoint we write system snapshots, a human-readable events log, and a machine-
readable collisions table. Snapshots and events include each body’s mass, orbital elements, water fraction, Cartesian
state, and a code label to track identity (Sun, gas giants, embryos, planetesimals, debris).
To emulate an open system, by default, we remove bodies according to: (i) unbound orbits (e ≥1), (ii) semimajor
axis a > 15 AU, (iii) orbits too close to the Sun (a < 0.1 AU or perihelion q < 0.01 AU). Removed particles are
recorded.

2.7. Frame mapping: from SPH to the simulation SoC
The SPH outputs are given relative to the collision centre-of-mass (CoM) in a local spherical frame. To embed
outcomes consistently in the global simulation frame, we compute a deterministic rotation that aligns the SPH “y-
axis” with the larger body’s velocity vector a finite time prior to impact, following these steps:
1. Form the relative state (r0, v0) at the instant of contact, expressed in the smaller body’s frame, i.e., r0 =
xlarge −xsmall and v0 = vlarge −vsmall.
2. Rotate so that the specific angular momentum h = r0 × v0 lies along +ˆz; from (ϕ, θ) of h derive the yaw/pitch
that accomplishes this alignment.
3. Compute the Keplerian elements (a, e, f0) of the relative orbit in the aligned plane and rotate by ω such that
f = 0 occurs at y = 0 (placing periapsis on the x-axis).
4. Back-trace along the conic to the distance rmin = 5 (R1 + R2) (radii at impact) and take the unit vector of the
large body’s velocity there as the SPH y-axis proxy.
5. Map this proxy back into the original simulation frame to obtain two Euler angles (χ, ψ) that rotate SPH outputs
into the simulation frame; if hz > 0 an extra 180◦rotation about ˆy is applied to ensure a right-handed mapping.
We then convert SPH spherical coordinates (r, θ, ϕ) to Cartesian, rotate by (χ, ψ), and translate by the instantaneous
collision CoM state (xCoM, vCoM) before updating particles. For the radius–mass relation we use R = C(m/M⊕)S
with C = 1.008 R⊕and S = 0.279; dependence on water fraction is presently neglected at the radii level although
these effects should be negligible.
2.8. Reaccretion checks
Immediately after instantiating survivors and debris in the global frame we iterate simple pairwise reaccretion tests:
1. Survivor–survivor: if two survivors have relative speed vrel and separation d, and v2
rel < 2(m1 +m2)/d, we merge
them inelastically by conserving linear momentum and water mass.
2. Debris–survivor: for each debris piece, if v2
rel < 2(msurv + mdeb)/d with respect to any survivor, we accrete it
onto that survivor. After any accretion, we re-test the survivor–survivor condition once.
These checks provide a minimal correction for immediate bound re-collisions within the same time step, without
introducing new integration sub-steps.
2.9. Activation thresholds and test particles
Debris are sorted by mass and assigned labels D1, D2, . . . . Immediately before appending debris, we set N active
equal to the number of debris with m ≥mact (default mact = 3×10−7 M⊙≈0.1 M⊕). Particles with index ≥N active
are treated by Rebound as test particles (sim.testparticle type=1): they feel the gravity of active bodies but not of
each other, which stabilises performance while preserving the gross dynamical influence of the ejecta cloud. Survivors
are not added to N active by a separate increment; because they occupy earlier indices and debris are appended
afterward, survivors typically remain within the active range, following index ordering rules in Rebound rather than
an explicit survivor-specific rule. This activation policy, together with MFM, sets the effective mass–space resolution
of the collisional cascade.
2.10. Special cases and bookkeeping
Impacts involving the Sun or designated gas giants (indexed among the first NGG bodies) are treated as perfect
mergers: the secondary is merged into the primary, and the event is logged. Impacts with test-particle debris default
to perfect merging with the active body. All collision events append a line to a structured table recording: time;
(v0, α, Mtot, γ, wf,t, wf,p); CoM state; rotation angles (χ, ψ) and hz; survivor count and properties; integrated debris
mass and water fraction; and the list of labels assigned to the newly created debris. Additionally, a full system snapshot
(including Cartesian states) is written at each collision time, enabling the exact restart of a run from any event.

2.11. Numerical safety and diagnostics
To guard against spurious contact detections, the resolver first rejects pairs with instantaneous separation > 2(R1 +
R2) (where Ri is computed from the current mi). Energy is tracked with sim.track energy offset=1. A progress
log records wall-clock timing and the relative energy error at each output. Optional 2D/3D visual diagnostics of the
pre- and post-impact geometry can be produced for debugging.
3. TEST CASE
3.1. Our code vs SyMBA
We test our code through a comparison against Scora et al. (2024) who used SyMBA (Duncan et al. 1998) to study
the formation of Mercury from excited initial conditions. We hence setup a simulation equivalent to their nominal
case of a flat annulus planetesimals disk (between 0.7 to 1 AU) containing 160, 0.025 M⊕embryos, with an average
eccentricity of 0.1 drawn from a Rayleigh distribution.
As a first and basic sanity check, we compare the total mass remaining at the end of the simulations (at 100 Myr) and
we find Mtot,SyMBA/Mtot,here=0.88, indicating a small difference of only 12%.
For an in depth comparison of the objects mass distributions we show the masses histogram in Fig. 1. This plot shows
a slightly more top heavy distribution and a lack in leftover small planetesimals for our code compared to SyMBA. This is
not surprising since a central feature of our approach is the clustering of small debris into bigger objects. Additionally,
in Fig. 2 we show the final semimajor axis vs eccentricity of all objects. While, qualitatively, the two distributions
seem to share the same general features (the presence of two dominant objects in addition to a tail of smaller hotter
objects at larger semimajor axis), our distribution seem to be dynamically hotter overall.
We interpret these offsets
as a consequence of the debris-resolution prescription rather than merely a stochastic difference between runs. In
SHARD, the SPH-interpolated debris mass and water budgets are conserved, but the unresolved reservoir is represented
by Nfr = ⌊Mfr/MFM⌋mass-weighted clusters; thus the choice of MFM controls how the same debris is dynamically
discretized. Concentrating debris into fewer, more massive tracers makes individual clumps easier to reaccrete through
the local energy criterion and leaves less long-lived low-mass material to provide dynamical damping. This naturally
explains the more top-heavy mass spectrum, the deficit of leftover planetesimals, part of the higher retained mass
relative to debris-loss prescriptions such as SyMBA, and the dynamically hotter final state. It also implies that fragment
resolution is physically consequential for Mercury formation, where the survival, mass, and core-mass fraction of a
Mercury analogue depend on whether stripped mantle-rich debris is reaccreted or removed; by the same mechanism,
water-rich ejecta can either be returned to growing planets or lost from the resolved system.
Finally, we implement the same “nine collisional types” postprocessing code to track and evolve the core-mantle
composition of the objects. Our results are found to be consistent with Scora et al. (2024) with Mercury-analogues
forming with the right mass and orbit starting from an Earth-like core mass fraction.

4. SUMMARY & CONCLUSIONS
We have introduced and validated a collision–resolution framework for late-stage terrestrial planet formation that (i)
maps detected N-body impacts to a six-parameter catalogue of smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH) outcomes and
(ii) statistically reconstructs the otherwise unresolved debris into a small number of dynamically consistent fragments
by mass-weighted k-means clustering in velocity space. The method is implemented on top of REBOUND/mercurius,
and the code is available for use at https://github.com/malidib/shard .
4.1. Summary of the collision-resolution pipeline
Given a detected contact between two non-test bodies, the pipeline is:
1. Compute (v0, α, Mtot, γ, wf,t, wf,p) and the collision CoM state.
2. Interpolate within the SPH table to obtain the two largest remnants (mass and water fractions, local SPH-frame
kinematics), drop sub-resolution survivors, and enforce water/mass clipping.
3. Compute the debris mass budget Mfr and water budget Wfr.
4. Interpolate SPH debris catalogues at the same query point to assemble a weighted debris sample.
5. In velocity space, cluster into Nfr fragments with mass-weighted centroids; apply the dust factor and renormalise
to match Mfr and Wfr exactly.
6. Map survivors and debris from the SPH frame to the simulation frame via (χ, ψ), then add them to the integrator
(survivors replacing the colliders; debris appended).
7. Run instantaneous survivor–survivor and debris–survivor reaccretion checks.

8. Set the activation split based on mact; label debris D1--DNfr.

maintaining tractable particle counts and strict conservation of mass and volatile inventories, modulo the deliberate
dust-removal parametrisation.
4.1.1. Reproducibility knobs and defaults
The main numerical and physical controls are:
• Integration: ∆t = 0.01 yr, hfac = 5, min dt = 10−4∆t, collision geometry direct, boundary none.
• Fragment resolution: MFM = 5.5 × 10−4 M⊕; activation threshold mact = 3 × 10−7 M⊙.
• Debris clustering: Nfr = ⌊Mfr/MFM⌋; KMeans in velocity space with sample weights = debris mass; post-clustering
dust factor = 1/2.
• Removal: unbound orbits; a > 15 AU; a < 0.1 AU or q < 0.01 AU.

• the immediate reaccretion tests are purely energetic and local to the collision timestep, leaving longer-term
gravitational focusing to the integrator.
• the radius–mass relation neglects composition and water-fraction dependencies at fixed mass.
• debris interpolation is restricted to the convex hull of the SPH grid; behaviour at more extreme impact parameters
awaits expanded SPH coverage.
• the adopted dust-removal factor and resolution thresholds (MFM, activation split) are explicit, tunable parameters
that trade physical fidelity against computational cost.

A. METHODS: SPH DATA
This section documents the SPH data used by the code and how it is prepared for consumption by the collision
resolver.
The implementation relies exclusively on a library of smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH) collision
outcomes sampled on a discrete grid in six control parameters, plus per-collision debris catalogues.
A.1. SPH outcome library: scope and provenance
Each datum corresponds to a single SPH impact between two non-rotating bodies and is indexed by the six-tuple

position and velocity vectors at contact (head-on ≈0◦, grazing ≈90◦), Mtot is the total colliding mass,
γ =min(m1/m2, m2/m1), and wf,t, wf,p are the bulk water mass fractions (%) of target and projectile. The library
samples these parameters on a fixed grid (Table 2).
SPH snapshots are recorded a short time after impact (order ∼10 hr in the source simulations) and then reduced into
the tabular formats described below; the code treats these as fixed inputs and does not depend on the hydrodynamic
solver internals. The adopted library is homogeneous in resolution (of order ∼2×104 SPH particles per collision), but
this resolution remains modest: convergence is expected to be poorest for the water partitioning of the second-largest
remnant and for the smallest clumps in the post-processed debris. Accordingly, we treat remnant- and debris-level
water fractions as approximate (potentially uncertain at the ≳tens-of-percent level for the second remnant, and larger
for smaller fragments), and we focus on conserving the total post-impact water mass budget when mapping outcomes
into the N-body code (Sect. 2.6). In particular, the most robust debris quantity is the mass-weighted mean water
fraction of the debris reservoir, whereas per-fragment (cluster) water fractions should be interpreted as bookkeeping
diagnostics rather than precise compositional predictions.

B. DIAGNOSTIC TESTS
B.1. Numerical diagnostics
In this section we show the results for a series of numerical diagnostic tests we ran for the same example shown in
section 3.1.
In Figs. 4, 5, and 6 we show the time evolution of the solid mass budget and particles number for our nominal
simulation.
In our code, the resolved solids inventory decreases through (i) dynamical excision of particles with e ≥1 or
a > 15 AU, (ii) boundary-driven mergers into the Sun (and perfect mergers with gas giants) for a < 0.1 AU or
q < 0.01 AU, and (iii) unresolved fragmentation when Mfr < MFM (so Nfr = 0). Therefore, as seen in Figs. 4, 5, and
6, the total solids mass is conserved as the missing mass in Fig. 4 is either transferred to the Sun/giants, removed as
ejected/out-of-bounds material, or relegated to an untracked sub-resolution dust component.
In Fig. 4, at t ∼25 Myr, we see a significant dip in the Embryos mass, compensated for by an increase in debris
mass.
In Fig. 7 we show the time evolution of the energy error relative to that at the start of the simulation ((Et−E0)/E0).
Ideally, for a perfectly energy-conserving integrator, this value would remain constant throughout the simulation. A
significant change indicates a systematic energy drift, which may arise from numerical integration errors, finite time-
stepping, close encounters, or the treatment of collisions and mergers.
Monitoring this relative error provides a
quantitative measure of the long-term stability and accuracy of the N-body integration scheme.
In our case, the
relative error exhibits negligible drift in the order of ∼10−3.
In Fig. 8 we show the collisions impact angle distribution for the same simulation. As expected, this follows a near
gaussian distribution with a mean of 45o.
