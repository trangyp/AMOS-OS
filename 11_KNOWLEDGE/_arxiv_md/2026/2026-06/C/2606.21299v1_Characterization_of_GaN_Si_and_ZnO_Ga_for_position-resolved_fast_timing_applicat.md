A B S T R A C T
We present the characterization of two fast, crystalline inorganic scintillators, silicon-doped gallium
nitride (GaN:Si) and gallium-doped zinc oxide (ZnO:Ga), and compare their performance with
cerium-doped yttrium aluminium perovskite (YAP:Ce) for in-vacuum alpha-detection applications
that require high-performance timing, position, and energy resolution, such as 3D elemental mapping,
medical imaging, and homeland security applications. In this paper, we propose ZnO:Ga and GaN:Si
as high-performance drop-in replacements for the alpha detector in Associated Particle Imaging
(API) systems. However, the results reported here also have wide applicability. Prior work has
reported on polycrystalline forms of ZnO:Ga, which suffer from self-absorption or non-spectroscopic
alpha energy spectra, and, to our knowledge, GaN:Si has not been proposed to be used in API
systems. We present room-temperature scintillation time constants obtained via X-ray-induced time-
correlated single-photon counting for both proposed materials. They both exhibit exceedingly fast
rise times of ≤15 ps, and high brightness ≥1000 ph/MeV𝛼with resolved alpha-peaks. Single-crystal
ZnO:Ga and single-crystal GaN:Si yield single-component decays of 805 ps and 32 ps respectively.
Using a plastic scintillator reference setup, coincidence timing resolution (CTR) and detector timing
resolution (DTR) measurements demonstrate a ≥3× improvement in timing resolution compared
to traditional YAP:Ce. GaN:Si and ZnO:Ga exhibit (35 ± 9) ps and (49 ± 5) ps DTR respectively,
compared to (144 ± 2) ps for conventional, single-crystal YAP:Ce. Finally, we evaluate their position
resolution in an experimental setup designed for API and measure better than 0.2 mm for YAP:Ce
and approximately 1 mm for GaN:Si. We obtain a position resolution of 0.3 mm for ZnO:Ga from
simulations. We also present alpha-induced ionoluminescence emission spectra that reveal direct,
red-shifted near-bandgap emission.
1. Introduction and Background
High-performance radiation detection systems, in par-
ticular those achieving fast timing, are of interest to many
application domains, from 3D elemental mapping in soils
or for planetary exploration to medical imaging, and the
detection and imaging of landmines. In medical imaging,
Positron-emission tomography (PET) and Computed To-
mography (CT) have aspirational timing-resolution targets
of ≤10 ps coincidence timing resolution (CTR) full width
at half maximum (FWHM) [1]. Reaching this benchmark
requires scintillators that are both bright and fast. In PET
and CT, improvements in scintillator materials for X-ray
and gamma-ray detection are key, and their properties, in a
well-engineered system, determine the fundamental limits
of performance. Enhancing the scintillator performance
would enable, for example, high-precision tumor detection
with lower radiation dose for the patient. For 3D elemental
mapping, techniques such as Associated Particle Imaging

1.1. Rise and Decay Times
In previous work, primary decay times were reported to
be less than 1 ns for GaN:Si (e.g., [6]) and ZnO:Ga (e.g.,
[7]). While unintentionally doped GaN was studied for its
relatively slow yellow luminescence scintillation compo-
nent, e.g., in Yanagida et al. [8], Pittet et al. [9] have shown
that for silicon concentrations on the order of 1 × 1019/cm3
the ultra-fast direct band-gap emission becomes dominant.
For GaN:Si at 1 × 1019/cm3, the decay constant of the fast
365 nm scintillation emission was reported to be 500 ps by
Schenk et al. [10]. For ZnO:Ga, Cates et al. [11] measured
the decay time of their polycrystalline ZnO:Ga sample
to be 235 ps and a rise time of 20 ps for alpha particle
induced time-correlated single-photon counting (TCSPC).
As ZnO:Ga has been known for some time for its potential
in fast timing applications, growth techniques, and different
dopants have been investigated, e.g., by Neal et al. in [12]
and by Bourret-Courchesne et al. [13].
1.2. Light Yield
Yanagida et al. [8] report a light yield of 5300 photons
for a 5.5 MeV alpha particle for the fast direct bandgap
emission of GaN:Si at ≈400 nm. For ZnO:Ga, Cates et al.
[11] measured less than 750 ph/MeV𝛼from a powder sam-
ple, where ph/MeV𝛼describes the light yield per alpha
particle energy. YAP:Ce is a scintillator with a well-known
light yield. Crytur, the manufacturer, reports a light yield of
(5630 ± 630) ph/MeV𝛼, where the error arises from the un-
certainty in the alpha-to-gamma ratio, ranging from 0.20 to
0.25.
1.3. Statistical Timing Performance Limits
Cates et al. [11] demonstrated sub-100 ps timing at
modest detected-photon counts for a ZnO:Ga powder sample
and benchmarked results against a straight-response model
and the statistical Cramèr-Rao lower bound (CRLB).

1.4. Position Resolution
Ayllon Unzueta et al. [2] characterized the position res-
olution capabilities of a YAP:Ce scintillator in an integrated
API system and reported an achievable alpha particle reso-
lution using a Hamamatsu H13700-03 SEL photomultiplier
tube (PMT) of less than 0.2 mm. For ZnO:Ga, Cates et al.
[16] report a position resolution of less than 1 mm for 80 %
of the detector area.

2.1. Scintillation Properties

GaN:Si is governed by a competition between near-band-

for example, Pittet et al. [9]). For heavily Si-doped material,
the radiative efficiency increases due to enhanced band-
to-band recombination rates [10]. In addition to band-
edge emission, a broad yellow luminescence band centered
around 540 nm is typically observed. This band is commonly
attributed to deep defect complexes such as Ga vacancies

ZnO:Ga is another wide-bandgap semiconductor with a
band edge around 3.37 eV. The dominant radiative mecha-
nism involves recombination of electrons from Ga3+ donor
levels with ionized holes trapped at shallow acceptors, most
effectively hydrogen ions bound to zinc vacancies. Bourret-
Courchesne et al. [13] have shown that processing ZnO with
Ga2O3 followed by hydrogen annealing minimizes defects,
resulting in high luminosity and stable sub-nanosecond de-
cay. This donor–acceptor channel produces bright emission
centered near 389 nm, slightly below the band edge by
about 0.18 eV, and explains the ultra-fast, spectrally pure
scintillation observed in an optimally prepared sample. In
this study, the emission peak of the ZnO:Ga sample is at
401 nm, which suggests strong self-absorption in our rela-
tively thick sample for the 389 nm emission peak reported by
Bourret-Courchesne et al. [13]. Therefore, unlike GaN:Si,
high-quality ZnO:Ga shows little contribution from deep-
level luminescence, and its performance is set by the balance
between Ga donor electrons and shallow acceptors created
during processing.

3.1. Time-Correlated Single-Photon Counting
Setup
To quantitatively characterize the scintillation kinetics
of the different samples and to calculate the CRLB ac-
cording to Eq. (5), time-correlated single-photon count-
ing measurements were performed. The experimental setup,
shown in Figure 2, employed an ultra-short pulsed 200 fs
Ti:Sapphire laser with frequency-doubled output (via second
harmonic generation), pumped by a Nd:YAG laser operating
at its second harmonic. The light from the Ti:Sapphire laser
served both as the start reference and as the driver for a
photo-excited X-ray tube (Hamamatsu N5084) operating at
40 kV. The emitted X-rays excited scintillation light in the
samples, which was collected by a Hamamatsu R3809U-50
microchannel plate PMT (MCP-PMT) with an intrinsic TTS
of 25 ps FWHM. Photon arrival times were digitized with a
PicoHarp 330, which uses constant fraction discrimination
(CFD) for accurate time-stamping of the start and stop sig-
nals and records their time differences. An older version of
this setup is described by Derenzo et al. [20]. The instrument
response function (IRF) of the system, assumed to be Gaus-
sian, has been improved since the original publication of the
design from 109 ps to about 68 ps FWHM. The IRF of the
system was measured using a PbF2 sample. While the IRF is
not perfectly Gaussian, a Gaussian fit was used to estimate its
FWHM. Since the original publication of the design, the IRF
has been improved from 109 ps to about 68 ps FWHM. The
timing resolution is predominantly limited by the response of
the pulsed X-ray tube, whereas contributions from the PMT
transit time spread (∼25 ps) and the excitation pulse duration
(∼200 fs) are comparatively small.

5. Conclusions
We characterized single-crystal ZnO:Ga and near-single-
crystal GaN:Si for in-vacuum alpha-particle detection, demon-
strating fast timing, energy-resolved alpha peaks, and high
position resolution relevant to API applications. To our
knowledge, the use of GaN:Si in API systems is novel.
We show that these crystals achieve a ≥5× improvement
in timing resolution over YAP:Ce, a commonly used high-
performance scintillator in such systems. Using TCSPC,
we extracted rise and decay components. We quantified
absolute and relative light yields, and measured the CTR
as well as the resulting DTR of our samples in an alpha
particle triple-coincidence setup. The main experimental
results are summarized in Table 5: (i) primary decay compo-
nents of (32.4 ± 1.0) ps for GaN:Si, (805 ± 1) ps for ZnO:Ga
and (24.3 ± 0.1) ns for YAP:Ce, (ii) absolute light yield
of (1060 ± 350) ph/MeV𝛼(GaN:Si), (2600 ± 860) ph/MeV𝛼
(ZnO:Ga), (6090 ± 2010) ph/MeV𝛼(YAP:Ce), (iii) DTR of
(35 ± 9) ps for GaN:Si and (49 ± 5) ps for ZnO:Ga compared
to (144 ± 2) ps for YAP:Ce, and (iv) position resolution
of at least 0.2 mm for YAP:Ce, about 1 mm for GaN:Si,
and expected 0.3 mm for ZnO:Ga, and down to 0.18 mm
with a reflective layer in a thinner geometry. Our CRLB
analysis indicates that with PMTs, MCP-PMTs or silicon-
photomultiplier achieving TTS FWHM < 100 ps and com-
parable QE, GaN:Si and ZnO:Ga could, in principle, support
DTR near or even below 10 ps.
From a systems perspective, faster decay and reduced
pile-up open a path to higher operational rates and finer
depth resolution in an integrated API system. While acciden-
tal coincidences remain a limiting factor at elevated neutron
rates, improved CTR can narrow the coincidence window for
certain applications where it is limited by the CTR and not
