Classifying Groups of Certain Orders
Which integers n have exactly one group of order n?
Shihan Kanungo
Euler Circle, Palo Alto, CA 94306
Abstract
We will first discuss the question of which integers n have exactly one group of
order n, namely the cyclic group Z/nZ. We will see that these are the integers
that are relatively prime to the Euler totient function ϕ(n). Then we discuss
how many groups there are of order p3 for each prime p. We end with a couple
of interesting results and conjectures pertaining to groups of squarefree order.
1
Introduction
One of the first things we learn in abstract algebra is the notion of a cyclic group. For every
positive integer n, we have the cyclic group Z/nZ, the group of integers modulo n. When
n is prime, a simple application of Lagrange’s theorem yields that this is the only group of
order n. We may ask ourselves: what other positive integers have this property? That is,
for which positive integers n is every group of order n cyclic?
This is not a new problem; the first solution is attributed to Burnside and has appeared in
numerous papers. Dickson [5] determined in 1905 those n for which all groups of order n
are abelian. The earliest proof focusing specifically on n for which all groups of order n are
cyclic (not just abelian) was given by Szele [7] in 1947.
Definition. For n ∈N let f(n) denote the number of (isomorphism classes of) groups of
order n.
Question. Is there a good characterization of n such that f(n) = 1?
Considering f(n) for small values of n we see that f(2) = f(3) = 1 because 2, 3 are primes.
However, for n = 4 already we have f(4) > 1.
Example 1. Z/4Z and Z/2Z×Z/2Z are non-isomorphic: they have different maximal orders
for their elements: 4, and 2 respectively. In general, f(p2) > 1 because Z/pZ × Z/pZ has p2
elements, has no element of order p2, and is therefore not cyclic.
More generally, if p2 | n for some prime p, and if m = n/p2 then f(n) > 1 because we have
at least two non-isomorphic groups of order n:
Z/p2Z × Z/mZ
and
Z/pZ × Z/pZ × Z/mZ.
Thus, at the very least, we need n to be squarefree.

Definition. Integers that are divisible by no perfect square other than 1 are called squarefree
integers.
For example, 10 = 2 · 5 is squarefree, but 18 = 2 · 32 is not.
Observation 1. If f(n) = 1 then n must be squarefree.
However, the converse is false, since f(6) = 2.
Example 2 (The dihedral group D3). D3 has 6 elements and is generated by the two
elements {ρ, τ} where ρ is counterclockwise rotation by 2π/3, and τ is the reflection across
the line y = 0. Moreover, we have the relation ρτ = τρ−1, so D3 is not abelian.
In general, for any integer n > 1, the dihedral group Dn has 2n elements, and is generated
by {ρ, τ} with ρτ = τρ−1. Thus Dn is not abelian, and f(2n) > 1. Thus, we get
Observation 2. If f(n) = 1, then either n = 2 or n must be a squarefree odd integer.
Once again, the converse is false, since f(21) = 2. This time, however, the reason is not so
obvious. It is not straightforward to come up with a group of order 21 that is not isomorphic
to Z/21Z. We need to introduce the notion of a semidirect product of groups.
2
The Semidirect Product
We start with a definition:
Definition. Let H and K be groups and let ψ : K →Aut(H) be a homomorphism. Let
G = {(h, k) : h ∈H and k ∈K}. Define multiplication on G by
(h1, k1)(h2, k2) = (h1ψ(k1)(h2), k1k2).
This multiplication makes G a group of order |G| = |K||H| where the identity of G is
(eH, eK), and (h, k)−1 = (ψ(k−1)(h−1), k−1) is the inverse of (h, k). The group G is called
the semidirect product of H and K with respect to ψ (denoted by H ⋊ψ K).
Remark (The semidirect product is the direct product if the homomorphism is trivial).
Suppose H and K are groups and ψ : K →Aut(H) is the trivial homomorphism, i.e.
ψ(k) = id for all k ∈K. Then
(h1, k1)(h2, k2) = (h1 · ψ(k1)(h2), k1k2) = (h1h2, k1k2).
Hence H ⋊ψ K ∼= H × K.
The set of automorphisms of a group plays a central role in the study of semidirect products.
The semidirect product is distinct from the direct product only if there is some non-trivial
homomorphism ψ : K →Aut(H), which only happens if |K| divides | Aut(H)|. Let us
remind ourselves how to compute | Aut(H)| when H is a cyclic group.
Proposition 3. Aut(Z/nZ) ∼= (Z/nZ)× and this is an abelian group of order ϕ(n).

We can now understand why f(21) = 2. This is because 21 = 3 · 7 and 3 | (7 −1).
Example 3 (Groups of order pq with p ≡1 (mod q)).
Let G be a group of order pq where p > q are distinct primes. Then there is only one
Sylow p-subgroup P, which is therefore normal. Let Q be a Sylow q-subgroup. Then Q is a
complement of P in G, so G is a semidirect product P ⋊ψ Q, for some homomorphism
ψ : Q →Aut(P).
Since q | (p −1) then Aut(P) ∼= Aut(Z/pZ) has a unique subgroup of order q, and ψ can be
an isomorphism from Z/qZ to this subgroup. We can choose a generator for Z/qZ to map
to a specified element of order q in Aut(Z/pZ). So there is, up to isomorphism, a unique
semidirect product which is not a direct product. In other words, the number of groups of
order pq (up to isomorphism) is 2 if q | (p −1) and 1 otherwise.
We can gather all these observations into one useful lemma.
Lemma 1. If n = pq where p | (q −1) then there exists a semidirect product of the cyclic
group of order p and the cyclic group of order q. In particular, f(n) = 2.
Proof. See Example above.
□

3
The Main Theorem
At the end of Section 1 we concluded that it was enough to restrict our attention to the odd,
squarefree integers. In Section 2 we discovered that if n is squarefree and p | (q −1) for some
primes p, q dividing n, then we have a semidirect product in addition to the cyclic group of
order n. This is the same as saying that if f(n) = 1 then gcd(n, ϕ(n)) = 1 where ϕ(n) is the
Euler totient function.

Lemma 2 allows us to restate Proposition 5 as
Proposition 6. If f(n) = 1 then gcd(n, ϕ(n)) = 1.
And this time the converse also holds! We thus obtain a tidy classification for integers n
such that there is exactly one group of order n.
Theorem 3.1. For a positive integer n, the only group of order n is the cyclic group Z/nZ
if and only if gcd(n, ϕ(n)) = 1, where ϕ denotes the Euler-ϕ function.
Proposition 6 proves that the condition is necessary. It will take us quite a bit more work
to prove that it is also sufficient. We will do so by first discovering that groups of squarefree
order satisfying the conditions of the theorem possess a couple of “nice” properties, and then
showing inductively that those properties force the group to be cyclic.
4
Groups of Squarefree Order
We will build our group inductively, out of its subgroups.
But what kind of subgroups
should we look for? We have seen that abelian groups are one class of groups that we largely
understand; in fact we have a precise classification of all finite abelian groups. Therefore
we will try to decompose our group into abelian subgroups. Groups that permit such a
subdivision are the solvable groups, so our first step is to show that any group of odd
squarefree order is solvable.

Proposition 7. Let G be a group of order p1p2 · · · pk, where p1, p2, . . . , pk are distinct primes.
Then G is solvable.
Proof. We proceed by induction on k, the number of prime factors.
If k = 1, then |G| = p1 is prime, so G is cyclic and hence abelian. Therefore G is solvable.
Now assume k ≥2 and that every group whose order is a product of fewer than k distinct
primes is solvable. Let |G| = p1p2 · · · pk, where the pi are distinct, and let pk be the largest
prime dividing |G|.
Consider the number npk of Sylow pk-subgroups of G.
By Sylow’s
theorems,
npk ≡1
(mod pk)
and
npk | p1p2 · · · pk−1.
Since npk divides the product of the primes p1, . . . , pk−1, every prime divisor of npk is strictly
smaller than pk.
Thus npk < pk.
But npk ≡1 (mod pk), and the only positive integer
less than pk that is congruent to 1 modulo pk is 1. Hence npk = 1. Therefore the Sylow
pk-subgroup P is unique and hence normal in G.
Since |P| = pk, the subgroup P is cyclic of prime order and therefore abelian. In particular,
P is solvable. Moreover,
|G/P| = p1p2 · · · pk−1,
which is a product of k −1 distinct primes. By the induction hypothesis, the quotient group
G/P is solvable. We now have a short exact sequence
1 −→P −→G −→G/P −→1,
with both P and G/P solvable. Since an extension of a solvable group by a solvable group
is solvable, it follows that G is solvable.
By induction, every group of order p1p2 · · · pk, where the pi are distinct primes, is solvable.
□
Proposition 7 guarantees that we have enough abelian subgroups inside G. Now we have
to find a way to take two of them of the right size and “glue” them together. The way we
imagine groups being built out of smaller pieces is that if G is a finite group and H ◁G then
G is built out of H and G/H. Thus, we can break down a group into smaller pieces if it has
a nontrivial normal subgroup of the right index.
Proposition 8. Let G be a group of order p1p2 · · · pk, where p1, p2, . . . , pk are distinct primes.
Then G has a normal subgroup of prime index.
Recall that the commutator subgroup [G, G] of G, is the subgroup generated by all elements
of the form [g, h] = ghg−1h−1. The quotient G/[G, G] is always abelian. We will be using
both these facts in the proof below.
Proof. By Proposition 7, G is solvable, so the commutator subgroup G′ = [G, G] is not equal
to G. Then G′ is either {e} or a proper subgroup of G. If G′ = {e}, then G is abelian.
Suppose G′ is a proper subgroup of G. Then (after reordering, if necessary) |G′| = p1p2 · · · pj
where 1 ≤j < k. So the quotient group G/G′ is an abelian group of order pj+1 · · · pk.
Therefore by Cauchy’s theorem G/G′ has a normal subgroup H/G′ of order pj+1 · · · pk−1.
Hence H is also a normal subgroup of G and |H| = p1p2 · · · pk−1, so [G : H] = pk.
□
Now we have everything we need to prove Theorem 3.1.

Proof of Theorem 3.1.
Suppose gcd(n, ϕ(n)) = 1. Then n = p1p2 · · · pk for distinct primes pi and pi ∤(pj −1) for
i ̸= j. We show that Z/nZ is the only group of order n. We use induction on k, the number
of prime factors of n.
If k = 1 then n = p1 i.e. n is a prime. Since every group of prime order is cyclic, Z/nZ is
the only group of order n.
Assume that the result is true for k = r i.e. for n = p1p2 · · · pr, Z/nZ is the only group of
order n. We will show that the result is true for k = r + 1 i.e. for n = p1p2 · · · pr+1.
Let G be a group of order n = p1p2 · · · pr+1. By Proposition 8, G has a normal subgroup, H,
of index pi for some i ∈{1, . . . , r + 1}. After reordering, if necessary, we can assume that
H = Z/mZ, where m = p1p2 · · · pr and gcd(m, ϕ(m)) = 1. Take K = Z/pr+1Z and consider
the semidirect product of H and K. It exists, because the semidirect product of any two
groups exists. Since H is a cyclic group, we have
| Aut(H)| = (p1 −1) · · · (pr −1).
Then any homomorphism from K to Aut(H) must be a trivial homomorphism, because
otherwise it would contradict gcd(n, ϕ(n)) = 1. Therefore the semidirect product of H and
K is actually just the direct product of H and K, which is a cyclic group of order p1p2 · · · pr+1.
Since H is the only group of order m, Z/nZ is the only group of order n = p1p2 · · · pr+1.
This establishes Theorem 3.1.

Keith Conrad [3] summarizes what is known about the count of groups of small p-power
order.
• There is one group of order p up to isomorphism (Z/pZ).
• There are two groups of order p2 up to isomorphism: Z/p2Z and Z/pZ × Z/pZ.
• There are five groups of order p3 up to isomorphism, but the explicit description of

Further Results and Conjectures
One of the first mathematicians to make advances in the enumeration of finite groups was
Otto H¨older. In 1893, he described groups of order p3 and p4. Shortly thereafter, he derived
a remarkable formula for the number of groups of order n when n is square-free.

n = p1p2 · · · pr and greater than 1, this last result implies that
f(n) ≤ϕ(n) = (p1 −1)(p2 −1) · · · (pr −1) < n.

We mention one final, curious conjecture in the enumeration of finite groups:
Conjecture 9. The group enumeration function is surjective.
That is, for every positive integer m, the conjecture asserts that there exists n such that
f(n) = m. This conjecture may well be resolved through consideration of squarefree n,
largely because of H¨older’s formula. Indeed, it has been verified that every m less than
10,000,000 is equal to f(n) for some squarefree n.
