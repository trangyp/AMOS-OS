Existing laboratory, astrophysical, and direct-detection searches constrain the kinetic-mixing portal ϵ of dark-
photon dark matter but do not determine the cosmological origin of the relic abundance. We show that cosmic
microwave background (CMB) isocurvature provides an independent probe of Higgsed dark-photon production
histories: two models with identical (mA′, ϵ) and identical present-day abundance can produce distinct cold-
dark-matter (CDM) isocurvature signatures if their hidden-scalar evolution differs. The relevant observable is
the logarithmic response of the final dark-photon abundance to the inflationary dark-Higgs displacement. We
develop a model-independent response formalism and demonstrate that any perturbative inheritance branch with
conserved comoving yield necessarily satisfies qeff ≥2. Consequently, a perturbative branch accounting for the
full dark-matter abundance through h →A′A′ requires an initial coherent displacement exceeding 3.5 × 104H∗,
while ordinary stochastic fluctuations over O(60) inflationary e-folds are exponentially unlikely to generate the
required field amplitude. Viable Higgsed dark-photon scenarios therefore require either subdominant abun-
dance, coherent initial conditions, suppression of the inherited scalar response prior to freeze-out, or sufficiently
cold momentum evolution of the produced vector population.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Understanding the microscopic origin of dark matter re-
mains one of the central open questions in modern particle
physics and cosmology.
While collider experiments, labo-
ratory searches, and direct-detection observations primarily
constrain the present-day interaction properties of dark mat-
ter candidates, the physics of the early Universe provides
an independent window into the mechanisms responsible for
their primordial production and cosmological evolution [1].
This interplay between particle phenomenology and inflation-
ary cosmology becomes particularly significant in scenarios
where the relic abundance of dark matter is directly connected
to scalar field dynamics during inflation.
Among the wide class of dark matter candidates, dark pho-
tons have attracted considerable attention as well-motivated
extensions of the Standard Model. In the conventional Stueck-
elberg realization, the dark photon is characterized at low en-
ergies by two effective parameters, namely its mass and ki-
netic mixing with the visible photon. Such scenarios naturally
accommodate several non-thermal production mechanisms,
including inflationary production and vacuum misalignment,
which can successfully generate a relic population of vector
dark matter [2–4].
A qualitatively different situation emerges when the dark
photon mass originates from a Higgs mechanism associated

In this Higgsed framework, the physical descrip-

including the structure of the symmetry-breaking potential,
the cosmological history of the hidden-sector phase transition,
and the radiative stability of the scalar potential itself [5–9].
As a consequence, the final dark-photon abundance no longer
depends solely on low-energy portal parameters, but also on
the detailed cosmological history governing the hidden scalar
sector. Various production channels such as inflationary vec-
tor production, axion-assisted amplification, resonant particle
generation, quenched energy transfer, cosmic-string induced
production, and coherent vector-field evolution therefore rep-
resent genuinely distinct physical origins rather than simple
reparameterizations leading to an equivalent relic abundance
[10–16].
A powerful probe of this production history arises through
cosmological isocurvature perturbations.
It is well known
from the classical axion misalignment mechanism that when
a light scalar field determines the final dark matter abun-
dance, quantum fluctuations generated during inflation induce
entropy perturbations that survive as observable isocurvature
signatures in the cosmic microwave background (CMB) [17–
22]. Similar theoretical arguments have subsequently been ex-
tended to a broad class of spectator-field, freeze-in, and non-
thermal hidden-sector dark matter scenarios [23–28].
The essential physical principle is straightforward. If the fi-
nal dark matter abundance depends on the amplitude of a light
scalar field displaced during inflation, then neighboring super-
horizon patches acquire slightly different dark matter densi-
ties due to inflationary fluctuations of that scalar field. Con-
sequently, the observable quantity is not determined directly
by the visible-sector portal interaction, but rather by the sen-

the subsequent post-inflationary transfer history become gen-

Higgsed dark-photon models provide a particularly timely

During inflation, a
sufficiently light dark Higgs field can acquire a stochastic
displacement, forming a coherent condensate whose subse-
quent evolution may transfer energy into stable dark pho-
tons through processes such as the perturbative decay chan-
nel h →A′A′ [32]. Although this specific production channel
serves as a useful benchmark example, the underlying physics
is considerably more general. From the cosmological perspec-
tive, the CMB effectively probes the functional relation con-
necting the inflationary displacement of the dark Higgs field
to the final dark-photon relic abundance.
In this work, we demonstrate that this scalar-origin imprint
imposes strong and highly nontrivial constraints on Higgsed
dark-photon dark matter. We show that the relevant cosmo-
logical observable is governed by the logarithmic response
parameter, defined by the sensitivity of the final vector abun-
dance to the initial scalar displacement. For perturbative in-
heritance mechanisms, we establish a lower bound qeff ≥2,
implying that scenarios in which dark photons constitute the
entirety of dark matter require an extremely large coherent
scalar displacement. We further show that the conventional
stochastic fluctuations generated during a finite inflationary
period are generically insufficient to naturally produce such
large field values. Consequently, viable cosmological realiza-
tions must either invoke coherently prepared condensates or
introduce dynamical processes capable of erasing the inher-
ited scalar dependence before the dark-photon number density
becomes fixed. The theoretical framework developed here is
intentionally local and largely independent of detailed model-
building assumptions. The first step consists of computing the
abundance-response parameter qeff = ∂ln ρA′/∂ln r, where r
denotes the inflationary scalar displacement associated with
the dark Higgs field. This response is then constrained by ap-
plying the CMB isocurvature bound to the inflationary fluc-
tuation amplitude H∗/(2πr), where H∗is the Hubble scale
when the CMB pivot mode exits the horizon. Only after this
cosmological consistency condition is imposed does one ex-
amine whether a specific hidden-sector realization—including
stochastic field evolution, coherent initial conditions, pertur-
bative decay channels, or non-equilibrium transfer dynam-
ics—can reproduce the required cosmological history.
A
central result of our analysis is that relic abundance alone
is insufficient to characterize the viability of Higgsed dark-
photon dark matter. Two models possessing identical low-
energy parameters (mA′, ϵ) and reproducing the same present-
day relic density can nevertheless generate fundamentally dif-
ferent isocurvature signatures if their hidden scalar sectors fol-
low distinct cosmological histories. The reason is that the
CMB probes the derivative structure of the abundance map
rather than simply its final normalization. This observation
leads to an important cosmological consistency criterion. A
viable Higgs-origin dark-photon relic must simultaneously
satisfy three independent physical requirements: it must re-
produce the observed dark matter abundance, remain com-
patible with inflation-induced scalar fluctuations constrained
by CMB isocurvature measurements, and ensure that the pro-
duced vector population redshifts sufficiently to become non-
relativistic before the onset of structure formation. The anal-
ysis presented in this work demonstrates that these three con-
ditions collectively provide a powerful and previously unex-
plored test of the cosmological origin of Higgsed dark-photon
dark matter.

Conclusion: The cosmological analysis of Higgsed-vector
dark matter remains fundamentally incomplete unless the
model specifies not only the relic abundance itself, but also
the abundance-response function, the local inflationary dis-

hidden-sector symmetry breaking, and the momentum evolu-
tion of the produced daughter particles.
Three independent consistency tests emerge naturally from
this framework: successful relic abundance generation, com-
patibility with CMB isocurvature constraints through the
scalar-response relation, and sufficiently cold momentum evo-
lution prior to structure formation. These conditions are logi-
cally independent, meaning that a model may satisfy any two
while failing the third. Altering the abundance normaliza-
tion merely shifts the location of an allowed parameter curve,
whereas the cosmological entropy constraint depends on the
logarithmic derivative governing scalar inheritance.
This has important phenomenological implications. Even
if a dark photon were discovered experimentally, its cosmo-
logical origin could not be reconstructed solely from the low-
energy parameters (mA′, ϵ). Conversely, future isocurvature
sensitivity from next-generation experiments such as CMB-
S4 or LiteBIRD reaching the range βiso ∼10−3–10−2 would
strongly disfavor perturbative all-dark-matter Higgsed-vector
inheritance unless the scalar response is dynamically sup-
pressed before freeze-out.
If the dark-photon relic abundance originates from a
stochastic dark-Higgs condensate displaced during inflation,
cosmological isocurvature measurements directly probe the
hidden scalar sector responsible for selecting the relic abun-
dance. The fundamental observable is therefore not simply the
visible portal interaction but the full cosmological history con-
necting spontaneous symmetry breaking, inflationary scalar
inheritance, and the final vector number density that survives
as dark matter in the present universe.

Appendix B: Inflationary potential and transition to the
Higgsed phase
During inflation, the relevant scalar dynamics are governed
by the effective potential experienced by the long-wavelength
Higgs field entering the stochastic Fokker–Planck descrip-
tion. This effective inflationary potential need not coincide
with the late-time vacuum potential responsible for sponta-
neous symmetry breaking. In general, curvature couplings,
Hubble-induced mass corrections, ultraviolet threshold ef-
fects, or reheating-induced thermal corrections can signifi-
cantly modify the scalar effective mass during inflation.
To capture this behavior, the stochastic inflationary phase
is parameterized by Eq. (2). A positive effective mass term
m2

values of m2

direction to remain light enough to develop large inflationary
fluctuations.
At later cosmological times, the hidden sector enters the
symmetry-breaking regime described by Eq. (3). If the tran-
sition connecting Eq. (2) to Eq. (3) proceeds adiabatically
with respect to the conserved comoving scalar number and
no hidden-sector number-changing interactions subsequently
erase the inherited abundance dependence, the perturbative
quadratic branch satisfies the universal relation qeff = 2.

must be computed directly from the complete abundance map.
This is precisely why the main analysis formulates the cosmo-
logical constraint in terms of the general quantity qeff, rather
than assuming a universal transition prescription.
