BAND BASES AS COMMON TRIANGULAR BASES IN CLUSTER ALGEBRAS
FROM SURFACES
FAN QIN AND CHAO SHEN
Abstract. We consider the skein algebra of an unpunctured marked surface. Thurston previously
constructed its band basis topologically. We show that this band basis coincides with the common
triangular basis, which is a Kazhdan-Lusztig type basis for quantum cluster algebras analogous to
the dual canonical basis of quantum groups. Our result confirms a conjecture of Thurston. It also
provides new cases for the existence of the common triangular basis. In addition, we discover a
phenomenon where certain unknots are arranged in configurations resembling beads on a necklace.

1. Introduction
1.1. Backgrounds. Cluster algebras, introduced by Fomin–Zelevinsky [FZ02], are commutative al-
gebras generated by cluster variables which are constructed recursively via a combinatorial algo-
rithm called mutation. They serve as an algebraic framework for studying the dual canonical basis
[Lus90, Lus91] [Kas90] and the theory of total positivity [Lus94]. Subsequently, Berenstein–Zelevinsky
[BZ05] introduced a natural quantization of this structure, giving rise to the theory of quantum cluster
algebras.
A primary objective in the study of (quantum) cluster algebras is the construction of “good” bases.
These bases are expected to share similar properties with the dual canonical bases of quantum groups.
Important families of these bases include:
• The generic basis in the sense of Dupont [Dup11] generalizes the dual semi-canonical basis
[Lus00] to the cluster algebra setting [GLS11]. For explicit computations on tame quivers, see
[DXX08] [Dup11]. Its general construction and existence can be found in [Pla13] [Qin21].
• The common triangular basis, introduced by the first author [Qin17], is a Kazhdan–Lusztig
type basis that extends the dual canonical basis to cluster algebras [Qin17, Qin20b].
In
acyclic cases, it coincides with the triangular basis of Berenstein–Zelevinsky [BZ14], see [Qin19]
[Qin20a, Remark 1.2.7].

• The theta basis, introduced by Gross–Hacking–Keel–Kontsevich [GHKK18], consists of theta

All three families of bases above are parameterized by tropical points, in accordance with the expecta-
tion of Fock–Goncharov [FG06, FG09]. It is believed that all known “good” bases for cluster algebras
in the literature belong to these families.
In the setting of cluster algebras from marked surfaces (via skein algebras), several bases are
constructed using topological objects, such as the bangle, bracelet, and band bases [MSW13] [Thu14].
We refer the reader to Section 2.1 for the background on the marked surface Σ and its skein algebra,
and to Sections 2.2 and 2.3 for the details of the bangle and band bases, respectively. These bases
have been compared with the general frameworks mentioned above.
For an unpunctured marked
surface, Geiß–Labardini-Fragoso–Wilson [GLFW23] showed that the generic basis coincides with the
bangle basis. Mandel–Qin [MQ23] established that the bracelet basis coincides with the theta basis
for general marked surfaces.
Although the bangle and bracelet bases fit into the general frameworks, the band basis remains less
understood. Thurston made the following bold conjecture [Thu14]:
The (quantized) band basis is an analogue of the dual canonical basis for the skein algebra.
Recall that the common triangular basis provides an analogue of the dual canonical basis [Qin17,
Qin20a]. Therefore, Thurston’s conjecture follows from the conjecture below.
Conjecture 1.1. The (quantized) band basis is the common triangular basis of the skein algebra after
localization at the frozen variables.
Remark 1.2. The only known evidence for this conjecture is the Kronecker case.
In this setting,
the common triangular basis is identified with the dual canonical basis. The dual canonical basis is
known to be the band basis because its elements (which correspond to imaginary roots) satisfy the
Chebyshev recursion [Lam11]. Therefore, the common triangular basis is realized as the band basis
in the Kronecker case.
1.2. Main results and our approach. Our main result is a proof of Conjecture 1.1 for unpunctured
surfaces.
Theorem 1.3. For any unpunctured surface Σ = (S, M), the band basis of the skein algebra Sk◦
q(Σ)
coincides with the common triangular basis.
Theorem 1.3 provides the existence of the common triangular basis for a new family of cluster
algebras, which was previously known only for cluster algebras arising from Lie theory [Qin24a]. It
also allows us to visualize Kazhdan–Lusztig type basis elements using topological objects.
Remark 1.4. The main result provides strong evidence for the existence of a monoidal category whose
simple objects categorify the band basis. Indeed, the common triangular basis of a cluster algebra is
typically categorified by the set of classes of simple objects in a suitable monoidal category, possibly
after mild modification of the cluster algebra (a change of the frozen part) [Qin17, Qin24a]. In this
light, the fact that the band basis coincides with the common triangular basis suggests that there
might exist a monoidal category where the band elements correspond to its simple objects.

2. Preliminaries
2.1. The skein algebra. In this section, we briefly review the fundamental concepts of skein algebras
on marked surfaces. For detailed definitions and proofs, we refer the reader to [Mul16].
Let S be a compact oriented surface with boundary, and M ⊂∂S a finite set of marked points.
The pair Σ = (S, M) is called a marked surface (unpunctured).
A curve x in S is an immersion x : C →S from a compact connected 1-manifold C to S such that
the boundary of C maps into M and the interior of C does not map to M or ∂S. Two curves are
homotopic if they can be related by a homotopy respecting M and orientation reversal. An arc is
a curve with endpoints in M, and a loop is a closed curve with no endpoints. An arc is a boundary
arc if it is homotopic to the closure of a component of ∂S\M. A multicurve X is an unordered finite
collection of (possibly homotopic) curves in S. Two multicurves are homotopic if there is a bijection
between their constituent curves which takes a curve to a homotopic one.
A strand in a multicurve X at a point p ∈S is a connected component of X ∩Dp, where Dp is a
sufficiently small disk neighborhood of p. A multicurve X is transverse if the strands have distinct
tangent directions at each intersection, and each interior intersection (a crossing) involves exactly
two strands. Moreover, every multicurve is homotopic to a transverse multicurve.
A transverse multicurve is simple if it has no crossings and no contractible curves.
Here, a
contractible curve is either a null-homotopic loop (an unknot) or an arc that bounds a disk in S
(a contractible arc).
A link X is a transverse multicurve X equipped with an ordering (over/under) of the two strands
at each crossing, as well as an equivalence relation and a total ordering on the resulting equivalence
classes of strands at each p ∈M. A simple multicurve X can be regarded as a link with a simultaneous
ordering at each endpoint, also denoted by X. Homotopies between links are performed within the
class of transverse multicurves, preserving crossing data.

Appendix A. Nested contractible sequence
We provide a refinement of Proposition 3.8 concerning contractible components. Although this
description is not required for the main results of this paper, it may be of independent interest.
Lemma A.1 (Existence of inner nested components). Let C be a strip-free unknot with at least one
outward angle. Then, in the interior of the disk D bounded by C, there exists another strip-free unknot
n′ which is a component of some Eε.
Proof. Since C contains no strips, the angle adjacent to its outward angle must be attached to another
component n′ of Eε.
Since D is contractible, the strand corresponding to this adjacent angle is
contained within D. As C ∩n′ = ∅, n′ must be contained in the interior of D.
Moreover, since n′ is also contractible and cannot end at a marked point, it must be a distinct
unknot component inside D. If n′ contains any strips, we can adjust the smoothing choice at those
crossings to obtain a strip-free unknot.
□
Lemma A.2 (Alternating signs). Let D be an embedded disk in Σ whose boundary n = ∂D intersects
the tubular neighborhood T of x transversely. If the portion of D outside T , denoted by D \ T , is
connected, then the smoothing signs must strictly alternate along n (i.e., . . . , +, −, +, −, . . . ).
Proof. We prove this using the induced orientation on the boundary and the intersection indices with
the tubular neighborhood.
Fix an orientation on D, inducing a tangent vector field V on n.
For each intersection point
p ∈n ∩∂T , define the intersection index ι(p) ∈{+1, −1}, corresponding to V pointing out of T
(exit point) or into T (entry point), respectively. On a fixed side of x, there is a geometric bijection
between the smoothing signs ε+ and ε−and the intersection indices ι. Thus, it suffices to prove that
the intersection indices alternate.
The boundary n is decomposed into two types of segments:
(1) Leaf component segments: These lie inside T . Since each leaf component d ⊂D ∩T
is a disk, by continuity of flow, its boundary segment must originate from an entry point
(ι = −1) and terminate at an exit point (ι = +1). Thus, each segment of the leaf component
contributes a pair of indices {−1, +1}.
(2) Outer segments: These lie outside T , connecting all leaf components in sequence.

Consider an outer segment connecting two consecutive leaf components di and di+1. This segment
must originate from the termination point of di (exit point, ι = +1) and connect to the starting point
of di+1 (entry point, ι = −1).
Contradiction: Suppose a segment connects two points with the same index (e.g., two exit points).
Together with:
• The two segments of the leaf components
• An interval connecting the remaining two points in the T -disjoint component,
they form a handle. Geometrically, this implies the tangent vectors at the connection are “opposing”
each other. To connect these vectors while maintaining the embeddedness of D (no M¨obius bands),
the handle would require a half-twist. This contradicts the fact that D is a two-sided embedded disk
(with trivial normal bundle).
Therefore, the outer segments must sequentially connect an exit point to an entry point. This
forces the intersection indices (and thus the corresponding smoothing signs) of all adjacent crossings
to alternate strictly.
□
Proposition A.3 (Nested contractible sequence). Let C be a strip-free contractible component of a
multicurve Eε. Consider all those Eε that contain C.
(1) If C is an unknot, then there exists a sequence of distinct strip-free unknots {ni}s
i=1 such that
ns = C, and the disks Di bounded by ni satisfy D1 ⊂D2 ⊂· · · ⊂Ds. Furthermore, the
smoothing signs of the innermost unknot n1 alternate strictly. For each unknot ni, i ≤s −1,
if we denote the number of negative angles by ki (where ki ≥2), then the total angle count is
2ki, and the sequence satisfies k1 ≤k2 ≤· · · ≤ks−1.
(2) If C is a contractible arc ending at a marked point p, then there exists a sequence of distinct
strip-free contractible curves (loops or arcs) {ci}s
i=1 such that cs = C, and the regions Di
(disks or monogons) bounded by ci satisfy D1 ⊂D2 ⊂· · · ⊂Ds. Furthermore, all angles of
the innermost curve c1 are inward. For each curve ci, if we denote the number of negative
angles by ki (where ki ≥2), then the total angle count is 2ki (if a loop) or 2ki −1 (if an arc),
and the sequence satisfies k1 ≤k2 ≤· · · ≤ks−1.
Proof. (1) Let D be the disk bounded by the unknot C.
Step 1: Construction of the nested sequence If C has an outward angle, by Lemma A.1, there
exists a smaller unknot contained in the interior of D. Since the components of Eε are finite, iterating
this process yields a finite sequence of strip-free unknots n1, . . . , ns = C, where D1 ⊂D2 ⊂. . . ⊂Ds.
Step 2: Properties of the innermost unknot n1 Consider the innermost loop n1. Since it
is innermost, it cannot contain any smaller components. Therefore, all angles of n1 must be inward
angles.
The fact that all angles are inward implies that the disk D1 bounded by n1 does not cross x locally.
Consequently, the portion of D1 outside the tubular neighborhood is connected, meaning n1 contains
exactly one unique T -disjoint component.
By Lemma A.2, this forces the smoothing signs (and corresponding angles) along n1 to alternate
strictly. Since n1 has 2k1 angles, these angles must exhibit an alternating pattern (+, −, +, −. . . ).
Note that k1 ≥2, otherwise the minimal intersection property of x and Y would be violated.
