ABSTRACT
Context. The elastic properties of the neutron-star crust are important for the calculations of crustal modes. In particular,
the ability of the crust to support shear stresses has been connected to observations of quasi-periodic oscillations and
to crust deformations potentially emitting gravitational waves.
Aims. In this work, we assess the uncertainties in the shear modulus and shear speed in the neutron-star outer and
inner crust.
Methods. To this aim, we performed a Bayesian analysis of the shear properties of the neutron-star crust at zero temper-
ature starting from both a non-informative and a nuclear-physics-informed prior. For the treatment of inhomogeneous
matter in the crust, we relied on the one-component plasma approximation, with a (semi-)classical treatment of the
ions.
Results. We show that the use of a nuclear-physics-informed prior has a non-negligible impact on the prediction of the
elastic properties of the crust. The frequency of the fundamental torsional crustal modes we obtain is compatible with
the low-frequency range of observed quasi-periodic oscillations, our estimates lying in the interval ≈20 −50 Hz.
Conclusions. Although the different considered priors lead to compatible results, the inclusion of nuclear-physics exper-
imental information in the prior considerably reduces the uncertainties in the prediction of the elastic properties of the
crust, potentially constraining the predicted frequency of the crustal modes.
Key words. stars: neutron – dense matter – stars: oscillations
1. Introduction
The structure of a cold non-accreting neutron star (NS) is
supposed to be composed of an outer crust (corresponding
to the first few hundred metres under the NS surface), made
of fully ionised atoms immersed in a charge-compensating
electron background, an inner crust (from the so-called neu-
tron drip at about 2 × 10−4 fm−3 up to the crust–core
transition at about 0.5nsat, the saturation density being
nsat ≈0.16 fm−3), where neutron-proton clusters co-exist
with a background of unbound neutrons and electrons, and
a core, made of homogeneous matter where non-nucleonic
degrees of freedom may also appear (see e.g. Haensel et al.
(2007);
Oertel et al.
(2017);
Burgio & Fantina
(2018);
Blaschke & Chamel (2018); Raduta (2022) for a review).
In this so-called ‘cold catalysed matter’ hypothesis, mean-
ing that the NS composition is determined in the ground
state at zero temperature, the crust is expected to be
stratified in pure layers, each of which consists of a one-
component Coulomb crystal (except possibly at the bound-
aries between adjacent layers; see Chamel & Fantina (2016)
for a discussion in the outer crust). Therefore, in this
scenario, and in general below the crystallization tem-
perature (Haensel et al. 2007; Medin & Cumming 2010;
Fantina et al. 2020; Carreau et al. 2020), nuclei in the crust
should be crystallized and form a body-centred-cubic (bcc)
lattice (although different geometries like a face-centred-
cubic lattice have been found to be thermodynamically
favoured in some cases; see e.g. Kozhberov & Potekhin
(2021)). The crust is thus expected to behave as a solid,
allowing for elasticity.
The computation of the elastic properties of the
NS crust has recently regained attention since the abil-
ity of the crust to support shear stresses has been
connected
to
observations.
Crustal
elasticity
has
in-
deed been suggested to explain quasi-periodic oscilla-
tions (QPOs) after giant flares of magnetars (see e.g.
McDermott et al. (1988); Gabler et al. (2018); Sotani et al.
(2018); Kozhberov & Yakovlev (2020); Sotani (2024)). In
addition, the shear (and bulk) modulus are relevant for
modelling crust deformations, such as the so-called ‘moun-
tains’ (asymmetric deformations in the crustal mass distri-
bution), that can lead to emission of gravitational waves
(see e.g. Haskell et al. (2006); Gearheart et al. (2011);
Gittins et al. (2020); Kerin & Melatos (2022)). In partic-
ular, it was pointed out that the overall scaling of deforma-
tion is set by the shear speed (Giliberti et al. 2020).
Several works in the literature, within different ap-
proaches and approximations, have been devoted to the

2. Model of the neutron-star crust
In this work we consider NSs at zero temperature in the
one-component plasma approximation (Haensel et al. 2007;
Chamel & Haensel 2008). According to the latter hypothe-
sis, each layer of the crust is composed of identical Wigner-
Seitz (WS) cell, each of volume VWS, with an ion with
proton and mass number Z and A, respectively, at the
centre, surrounded by a uniform electron gas (and, in
the inner crust, unbound neutrons too). In view of the
uncertainties pertaining to the calculation of the shear
modulus in the so-called ‘pasta phases’, expected to ap-
pear at the bottom of the inner crust, we consider here
only spherical clusters (see e.g. Pethick & Potekhin (1998);
Caplan et al.
(2018);
Pethick et al.
(2020);
Xia et al.
(2023); Zemlyakov & Chugunov (2023) for a calculation
of elastic properties in non-spherical configurations and
Dinh Thi et al. (2021a) for a discussion on pasta phases
within the same approach used here).

3.2. Uncertainties in the shear modulus and speed
We start our discussion by presenting the elastic proper-
ties of the outer crust in Fig. 1. The dark (light) yellow
shaded areas represent the 1σ (2σ) confidence intervals of
our posterior when all filters (1. to 7.) are included, start-
ing from a uniform prior for the parameter set X (point i in
Sect. 3.1), while the red dash-dotted lines identify the 2σ
confidence interval obtained starting from a non-uniform
prior (point ii in Sect. 3.1). For comparison, we also show
the CLDM results obtained using the BSk24 empirical pa-
rameters (green dash-dotted lines) and the results from the
outer-crust data in Pearson et al. (2018), for which the ion
masses have been calculated using experimental mass data
whenever available complemented with theoretical masses
from the HFB-24 mass model (Goriely et al. 2010) (black
dashed lines). We observe in all panels a similar trend of the
results for the shown crust properties, namely the WS ra-
dius rWS = (3VWS/(4π))1/3 (panel a), the ion proton num-
ber Z (panel b), the shear modulus (panel c), and the shear
speed (panel d). The results in Fig. 1 are obtained neglect-
ing the correction factor in the shear modulus, that is, using
Eq. (16). Indeed, we checked that the effect of this correc-
tion is negligible in the density range explored by the outer
crust, thus the two results would be indistinguishable in the
figure. The increase in the shear speed is mainly due to the
decrease of rWS with increasing density in the crust, com-
bined with the slight increase in Z; see Eq. (16). As for the
shear speed, its increase is mainly due to the increase in µ
(we recall that in the outer crust ρB,g = 0, thus ρB,d = ρB).
The CLDM results using the BSk24 empirical parameters
are in very good agreement with our posterior at 1σ, as pre-
viously noticed for other NS properties in Dinh Thi et al.
(2021a) and Fantina & Gulminelli (2023), while variations
are observed with respect to the more microscopic calcu-
lations of Pearson et al. (2018) based on the same func-
tional. This reflects the inclusion of nuclear-structure effects
in Pearson et al. (2018), which are absent in the CLDM
approach. The most striking feature observed in Fig. 1 is
the reduction of the 2σ uncertainty band when experimen-
tal nuclear data are included in the prior, as can be seen
by comparing the red dash-dotted lines to the light-yellow
shaded area. In any case, the uncertainties in the NS-crust
properties are relatively narrow, since the only nuclear in-
puts in their determination are nuclear masses (either ex-
perimentally measured or theoretically calculated) thus the
model dependence is relatively small and mainly affects the
deeper layers.
On the other hand, as expected, uncertainties are larger
in the inner crust, as shown in Figs. 2 and 3. As in Fig. 1,
the 1σ and 2σ confidence interval obtained with the CLDM
(yellow shaded areas) starting from a uniform prior (point
i in Sect. 3.1) are displayed together with the 2σ con-
fidence interval obtained starting from a nuclear-physics-
informed prior (point ii in Sect. 3.1; red dash-dotted lines).
For comparison, we also show the 2σ confidence interval re-
sulting from the posterior models of Klausner et al. (2026)
(blue lines)4, where the crust was treated in the Extended
Thomas-Fermi (ETF) approach, and the results obtained
using the inner-crust data of Pearson et al. (2018) com-
puted with the ETF plus Strutinski Integral (ETFSI) calcu-
lations based on the BSk24 functional (black dashed lines).
We note that the blue curves stop at nB ≈0.06 fm−3 be-
cause of convergence issues, as explained in Klausner et al.
(2026). From Fig. 2, we again observe in all approaches a
similar trend, that is, a decrease of the WS radius with den-
sity, while Z remains fairly constant for most of the densi-
ties explored in the inner crust, and only increases sensibly
at the bottom of the crust towards the crust–core transition
(see panels a and b, respectively), in agreement with pre-
vious works (see e.g. Carreau et al. (2020); Mondal et al.
(2020); Grams et al. (2022b)).
As for the elastic properties in the inner crust, from the
left panels of Fig. 3, we can note an increase of the shear
modulus while the shear speed is only slightly increasing
when the expression of the shear modulus for point-like nu-
clei, Eq. (16), is used. The behaviour of µ is mainly due
to the decreasing WS radius with density, while the trend
of vs can be attributed to the increasing importance of the
neutron-gas-density contribution in the dynamical density;
see Eq. (19). Also in the inner crust, the most remarkable
effect is the reduction of the 2σ uncertainty band when ex-
perimental nuclear data are included in the prior (blue solid
lines and red dash-dotted lines). As already observed in

3.3. Estimation of the frequency of the fundamental mode
Knowledge of the shear speed also allows us to estimate
the frequency of the fundamental n = 0 oscillation crust
mode (see Eq. (20)). In fact, QPOs discovered in the
afterglow after magnetar giant flares, whose frequencies
span the Hz to kHz range, have been considered to be
associated with NS oscillations. In particular, torsional
shear modes, that have a lower excitation energy com-
pared to other vibrational modes, have been invoked to
explain observed QPOs (Duncan 1998). We restrict our-
selves to the discussion of the fundamental lower order
(l = 2) mode, and we compare the estimated frequency
with the lowest observed QPO frequencies of SGR 1806−20
and SGR 1900+14 (Israel et al. 2005; Strohmayer & Watts
2005, 2006; Watts & Strohmayer 2006). Additional NS
properties, such as the equation of state and mass–radius
relation, computed with a nuclear-physics-informed prior
(point ii in Sect. 3.1) are shown and compared with those
obtained with a uniform prior (point i in Sect. 3.1; see also
Dinh Thi et al. (2021c)) in Klausner et al. (2026) (see e.g.
their Figs. 9-12).

would increase the uncertainties in its determination, as the

shown in Fig. 4. However, often previous works calcu-
lated the frequency considering full entrainment (that is,
ρB,d = ρB in Eq. (19)). This yields an underestimation of
the frequency with respect to the case where ρB,d = ρB−ρg,
that is, where entrainment is taken into account as es-
timated in recent works (Almirante & Urban 2025). For
example, using the SLy4 empirical parameters, we ob-
tain a frequency of about 18 Hz for a 1.4M⊙if we em-
ploy an averaged shear speed (either ⟨vs⟩r or ⟨vs⟩nB), in
the case of full entrainment and without finite-size correc-
tions (see Table 2). This is in good agreement with the
results shown in Fig. 3 of Steiner & Watts (2009), with
those in Tews (2017) (see their Fig. 8), and with the re-
cent ones in Parmar & Bombaci (2026) (see their Fig. 5 for
the case without pasta). Indeed, including uncertainties on
the composition, the surface parameters, and the entrain-
ment, Tews (2017) reported frequencies for the fundamental
l = 2 mode in the range ≈14 −52 Hz, while the Bayesian
study of Parmar & Bombaci (2026) indicates frequencies of
≈10−23 Hz for the same mode. As can be seen in Fig. 8 of
Tews (2017), the main factor contributing to increasing the
uncertainties and the overall values of frequencies appears
to be the treatment of the entrainment.
More recently, Sotani et al. (2024) presented computa-
tions of torsional ltn and shear modes, and couplings be-
tween axial and polar oscillation modes solving axisymmet-
ric perturbation equations. Employing the SLy4 interaction
and considering a 1.4M⊙, he predicted the frequency of the
2t0 mode at 23.4 Hz; see their Table I. For the same mode,
using Eq. (20) and the SLy4 empirical parameters, we ob-
tain a similar value, 30 Hz, when vs is calculated at the
crust–core transition in the case of full entrainment and
no finite-size corrections, which should better correspond
to the conditions of the calculation by Sotani et al. (2024)
(see Table 2). On the other hand, our estimate when we
include a more realistic treatment of entrainment is notice-
ably larger, due to the larger value of vs because of the
lower dynamical density. In both cases, the values are sen-
sibly reduced when the frequencies are estimated using an
average value of the shear speed with respect of taking vs
at the crust–core transition, as expected from Fig. 3. It is
interesting to see that overall the different approximations
considered (evaluation of vs at the crust–core transition,
full entrainment, and neglecting the finite-size corrections
in the calculation of µ) seem to compensate each other and
the resulting frequency, 30 Hz, is very close to our fiducial
value, 33.2 Hz, calculated with a density-weighted shear
speed, Eq. (23), using what we think to be the more realis-
tic expression for the entrainment, Eq. (19), and employing
Eq. (17) for µ. These values are also compatible with that
obtained in the more sophisticated approach of Sotani et al.
(2024), 23.9 Hz (last line in Table 2).
To check the impact of the different nuclear matter pa-
rameters on the frequency of the fundamental modes, we
also calculated the Pearson correlations coefficients. In par-
ticular, we observe an anti-correlation of the frequency with
the slope of the symmetry energy Lsym, as already reported
in previous works (see e.g. Gearheart et al. (2011), Sotani
(2024), and Parmar & Bombaci (2026)).
4. Conclusions
In this work, we performed a Bayesian analysis of shear
properties in the NS crust within a meta-model approach
for homogeneous nuclear matter complemented with a
CLDM for the ions. We find, in agreement with previ-
ous studies, that the properties of the outer crust are rel-
atively well constrained also for elastic shear properties,
while uncertainties increase with density in the inner crust.
In addition, we show that employing a nuclear-physics-
informed prior considerably reduce the spread in the pre-
diction for the crust, thus highlighting the importance of
nuclear-physics data for the physics of the crust. Interest-
ingly, the use of a more sophisticated ETF treatment of the
