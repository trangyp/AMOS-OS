Abstract
The widely known Yin-Yang symbol (Taijitu) is based on nested circles of different radii
whose areas are colored black and white such that the interface traces an S-shaped curve.
We address the question of how this symbol can be related to physical phenomena such as
daytime and nighttime duration and the annual seasons. Using a simple dynamic model of
daytime duration, we introduce the excess daytime fraction and reconstruct the symbol using
the latitude of Zurich. In particular, we explain how the black and white areas are linked
to the stability of Yin or Yang predominance. We further demonstrate that the Golden
and Silver Ratios found in the geometry of the symbol carry meaning with respect to the
Gregorian calendar. Finally, we construct an alternative Yin-Yang symbol using logarithmic
spirals with the Golden Ratio as the growth parameter. The didactical quantitative derivation
of the Yin-Yang symbol and its grounding in real-world observations can be regarded as a
novel perspective on this iconic pattern.

Introduction
In times of trouble, conflict, and polarization, there seems to be a renewed interest in states of
harmony, balance, and consensus. The Taijitu, commonly known as the classic Chinese symbol
for Yin and Yang (see Fig. 1), is an iconic expression of these desired states. It unites two
opposing “forces” or “principles”, represented as black and white, within a circle, the ultimate
symbol of wholeness and perfection. Black and white are balanced such that each covers half of
the circle’s area. Their dividing boundary, however, is not a straight line but an S-curve, giving
the symbol a dynamic impression. Two smaller circles of opposite color resemble “eyes”, which is
why some interpret the pattern as two embracing “fish”. In earlier times the Taijitu had a quite
different appearance1, one that was even more reminiscent of swirling fish than today’s version,
which is built on nested circles (see Fig. 2a).
The widespread popularity of the Taijitu contrasts with the limited knowledge about its origin
and meaning. To briefly summarize its history, the Taijitu became popular in China only during
the Song Dynasty, i.e. from the 11th century CE onward. The philosopher Zhou Dunyi (1017-
1073), who also wrote an insightful interpretation (Wang, 2005; Adler, 1999), used it to merge

A second notable feature regards the lengths of two prominent distances inside the YY.3 We
start from a point U on the perimeter of the unit circle that is equidistant from the center points
of the two smaller circles. Fig. 2(b) shows one of the two symmetric possibilities for locating U.
Then, drawing a line from U through each of the center points Z0, Z1, these lines cross the border
between the black and the white area at different distances. Because the outer circle is the unit
circle, one can verify that these two distances are related by the Golden Ratio, i.e. one of them
(red) has the length φ = 1.618 and the other one (blue) the length 1/φ = 0.618.
While these conclusions are straightforward, their interpretation remains unclear. We address
three questions in this paper:
1. What is the meaning of the outer circle, the two inner circles, and the two eyes? Are they
purely geometric shapes, or do they reflect something real?
2. What is the meaning of the black and white fillings? Does the prominent “fish-like” shape
have a foundation in reality?
3. How should the Golden Ratio within the YY be interpreted? Is it a purely geometric
artifact, or is it related to physical quantities?
In essence, these three questions ask whether the YY should be understood as an abstract
geometric representation of philosophical concepts or as a reflection of real observations. These
two perspectives are not mutually exclusive; both have their own validity.
In the following, I will demonstrate that the YY can indeed be linked to real observations.
Interpreting these observations aligns nicely with the philosophical perspective. Hence, these
investigations not only provide a didactic exercise in generating the YY from data, but also
shed new light on the scope of classical Chinese concepts.

3.3
Stability
The answer to our question: the YY does not simply reflect the actual values of the day-
time/nighttime duration, in terms of the excess fractions. Instead it indicates under what con-
ditions the subtle prevalence of Yin or Yang changes. In other words, this prevalence is not
identical to the daytime/nighttime relation. Instead it reflects the stability of the balance.

6
Discussion
Error estimates.
It was not the aim of this paper to obtain the highest accuracy in comparison
with empirical results. Nevertheless, because astronomical data exist, we can use them to estimate
the errors of our calculations. Before doing so, we summarize our simplifications in order to put
the results in the right perspective. In fact, the revolution of the Earth around the Sun and the
rotation of the Earth are not as regular as we assumed. The Earth’s orbit is an ellipse, not a
circle, the Earth itself is not a sphere but an ellipsoid, and its motion is influenced by the Moon
and the gravitational forces of other planets. Long-term dynamics such as the precession of the
Earth’s axis also play a role. In short, it is a rather complex motion, which we have captured
by the simplest possible rotational model based on only two parameters: the tilt of the Earth’s
axis, β, and the latitude of Zurich, λ.
Secondly, to simplify both the calculation and the visualization, we mapped the solar year, taken
as the round value 365, onto the circle of 360o. A quarter year was taken as a whole number
of days, 91, which introduces an error of 1 day; this discrepancy was assigned to the autumn
equinox (172+91=263, but 355-91=264). All calculated day numbers are rounded to whole days.
The dates of the cardinal points are uniformly set to the 21st day of the respective month, even
though it is well known that they shift over long time periods (e.g. from the 21st of December
to the 22nd of December).
With these simplifications in mind, let us take a look at the discrepancies in our calculations of
daytime and nighttime duration. According to timeanddate.de, the daytime duration for Zurich

Relations to the YY .
We have demonstrated that (i) the YY has a physical interpretation
in terms of the daytime/nighttime dynamics and (ii) the correct appearance of the smaller circles
and their coloring can be derived from a simple model whose only free parameter is the latitude
of Zurich.
Because Yin and Yang have always been associated with the sunlit and shadowed areas of, e.g.,
a valley, the connection to the daylight problem may seem obvious. Nevertheless, deriving such
a relation is an entirely different matter from offering a metaphysical interpretation. Key to our
derivations was the reduced variable ρ(γ), called the excess fraction of daytime/nighttime, which
yields an analytic expression for the S-curve dividing the black and white regions.
Relations to the Golden Ratio.
The physical interpretation of the Golden and Silver ratios
appearing in the geometry of the YY was an unexpected bonus. We were able to demonstrate
that these ratios divide the solar year correctly into days elapsed and days remaining, and can
thus be given a calendrical interpretation. At second glance, the correct proportions may seem
less surprising, since one can always arbitrarily divide a length L so as to obtain these ratios.
However, as we have verified, the angles γ defined with respect to the winter solstice would not
yield such relations. These relations emerge for the day numbers only after correcting the angles
by the appropriate offset of γ = 11o or t = 10 days. What is remarkable, therefore, is that this
offset coincides precisely with the start of the New Year according to the Gregorian calendar,
something that was by no means expected.
Relations to the “Book of Changes”.
The Yijing, the more than 2500-year-old classic
Book of Changes, already employs the concept of Yin and Yang to describe the fundamental
structure of reality as a “Supreme Polarity”. Yin is associated with even numbers, in particular
6 and 8, and Yang with odd numbers, in particular 7 and 9 (Wilhelm and Baynes, 1977).
The Yijing adds a dynamics to this structure. Yin and Yang each appear in two states, one
immutable and one changeable. Specifically, “old” Yin, characterized by the number 6, is able
to transform into “young” Yang, characterized by the number 7. Likewise, “old” Yang, char-
acterized by the number 9, is able to transform into “young” Yin, characterized by the number
8.

Our construction of the YY with its four quadrants (Fig. 6) connects these numbers to their
respective quadrants as follows: Quadrant I corresponds to young Yin, 8, because it has not yet
reached the critical threshold for change. Quadrant II corresponds to old Yin, 6, because we
observe its transformation into Yang. Quadrant III corresponds to young Yang, 7, which has
just appeared and has not yet grown to a critical level. Quadrant IV corresponds to old Yang,
9, because it is in the process of being transformed into young Yin.
We particularly highlight that this process of transformation follows a sequence of activity
(“change”) and tranquility (“rest”). The latter does not imply that nothing happens. In fact,
a continuous process unfolds “under the hood”, but the system “holds still” because its time
has not yet come. Thus, the dynamics we observe in our daytime model perfectly reflect the
intentions of the Yijing.
Relations to phase transitions.
It is possible to relate the Yin-Yang dynamics to phenom-
ena such as phase transitions in physics, bifurcations in nonlinear dynamics, or regime shifts in
ecology. The control parameter is the solar declination δ(t), the angle between the ecliptic and
the equator. It varies over time from δ(t) = −23.44o at the winter solstice to δ(t) = 23.44o at
the summer solstice (see also Table 1). This change is driven by an external process, the Earth’s
revolution around the Sun, which gives rise to the four seasons. When the control parameter
passes through a critical value, δ(t) = 0, a transition is induced because the stability of the
current state is lost. This critical value δ(t) = 0 occurs at the vernal and autumnal equinoxes.
Consequently, the transition takes place in the II. and IV. quarters of the year. In these two
quarters we observe the S-curve, i.e. the coexistence of black and white.
The new stable state is reached at the summer or winter solstice, respectively. Thus, during the
I. and III. quarters the dynamical system is stable. More precisely, it is metastable, since the
ratio between Yin and Yang is continuously changing. However, during the I. and III. quarters
this change has not yet reached a critical level. Therefore, the I. quarter is colored entirely black
and the III. entirely white in the YY, indicating stability.
To conclude, reconstructing the Yin-Yang symbol from a model of daytime change may not
add entirely new insights into the symbol itself. This was not to be expected, given the extensive
literature on this topic spanning hundreds of years. It is nevertheless surprising to observe natural
processes at work even in such a famous and iconic pattern. More importantly, the findings
presented here are perfectly aligned with the established concepts of Chinese philosophy, shedding
new light on the intricate connection between natural processes and systems of thought.
