ANCA M˘ACINIC∗
Abstract. We use recent results, among which modular inequalities for curves, to determine the
Alexander polynomials for some classes of pencil-type conic-line arrangements. For these classes of
curves we prove that the Alexander polynomial is (at least partially) combinatorial. To this end,
we exemplify new techniques that are suitable for broader use, lending themselves to more general
classes of curves.
1. Introduction
Seen as a natural generalization of arrangements of projective lines in the field of projective plane
curves, conic-line arrangements (or, equivalently, conic-line curves) are defined as curves whose
irreducible components are lines and smooth conics.
There are numerous recent articles that
explore algebraic, geometric, topological and combinatorial properties of conic-line arrangements,
see for instance [4, 19, 18, 30, 32, 40, 43].
Given a complex projective plane curve C defined by a homogeneous degree d polynomial fC ∈
C[x, y, z], we consider the Milnor fiber associated to C, i.e. the hypersurface in C3 defined by
F(C) : fC = 1.
The multiplication by a primitive root of unity of order d defines an action on F(C). This induces an
action on the homology group H1(F(C), Q), called algebraic monodromy, which makes H1(F(C), Q)
a Q[t±1]-module, where t acts by the algebraic monodromy. Since the monodromy is of finite order
d, H1(F(C), Q) is a torsion module. The Alexander polynomial of a curve, ∆C(t), is defined as
the characteristic polynomial of the monodromy action on H1(F(C), Q) and, in light of the above
discussion, it satisfies the formula

where ϕk is the k-th cyclotomic polynomial.
Computing the monodromy action on the homology of the Milnor fiber is a very difficult problem,
even in the case of degree 1 homology (meaning, computing the Alexander polynomial), and even
if we narrow down the problem to the case of arrangements of projective lines. To name just
a few papers on the subject, see [6, 8, 10, 15, 17, 14, 23, 26, 31].
In [11], based on a result
of Papadima-Suciu from [41], the authors introduce modular inequalities for (complements of)
complex projective plane curves, inequalities that give upper bounds for the multiplicities of roots
of the associated Alexander polynomial, provided that those roots are powers of primes. In the
same paper [11], lower bounds are given for the multiplicities of roots of the associated Alexander
polynomial, for quasi fiber-type curves. In this note we will use these results to compute Alexander
polynomials for pencil-type conic-line arrangements, but the techniques employed for this purpose
are likely to be of use in the computation of the Alexander polynomial of other general classes of
curves.
By pencil of conic-line arrangements, or, equivalently, pencil of conic-line curves, we mean a
pencil P of degree d ≥3 curves having at least three reducible and reduced members whose
irreducible components are lines and smooth conics. We will call these members conic-line fibers.

A pencil-type conic-line arrangement is a reduced curve that can be realized as an union of conic-line
fibers of a pencil of conic-line arrangements.
An intensely studied and still open problem concerning arrangements of hyperplanes, related
to the Milnor fiber monodromy problem, is whether the Alexander polynomial is determined by
the combinatorics of the intersection lattice of the arrangement, i.e.
if it is combinatorial.
A
comprehensive presentation of the problem and various approaches to solve it can be found in [38,
Subsection 1.8]. The general case of this problem can be reduced to the case of arrangements of
projective lines. On the other hand, we know that this conjecture does not hold for plane pro-
jective curves in general. In the context of curves, by combinatorial we mean determined by the
weak combinatorial type of the curve, see Section §2 for the definition. Counterexamples of the
combinatorial determination of the Alexander polynomial for curves can be found among Zariski
pairs (briefly, Zariski pairs are pairs of curves with the same weak combinatorial type but different
embedded in P2 topology, see for instance [2] for a detailed exposition on the subject). One may
consider for instance the pair of irreducible sextics that Zariski introduced in [47]. The two curves
are combinatorially equivalent but it turns out they have distinct Alexander polynomials. Ex-
amples involving reducible curves that are combinatorially equivalent but have distinct Alexander
polynomials have bee constructed as well, see for instance Artal Bartolo’s example from [3] of a
pair of degree 6 curves, each consisting of a union of a smooth cubic and three lines, which is also a
Zariski pair. Or, more generally, we refer to the Zariski pairs of sextics from Oka’s papers [34, 36],
emerging from sextics classification results.
However, we were able to detect a distinctive phenomenon that happens for a subclass of pencil-
type conic-line arrangements. Specifically, for this subclass, for which we give formulas for the
Alexander polynomials, in Theorems 2 and 3, we prove that the multiplicities of the roots of
the Alexander polynomials that we compute are combinatorial, see Theorem 1. These results are
reminiscent of similar results in the theory of arrangements of hyperplanes, see [29, 38].
In [9] Cogolludo-Libgober prove that in a pencil-type conic-line arrangement having only or-
dinary singular points as base points and which is realized as the union of m conic-line fibers of
a conic-line pencil of curves of degree d ≥3, necessarily m ≤6. We should point out that this
statement, recalled in Theorem 12, involves two cases different in nature, see [9, Proposition 4.2]
for the case d ≥4 and [9, Corollary 4.4] & [42] for the case d = 3. The bound on m in the case
d = 3 is sharp, as illustrated by an example of Ruppert (see [42]) of a a pencil-type conic-line
arrangement which is the union of 6 reducible conic-line fibers, each fiber being the union of a
line and a smooth conic. The bound on m in the case d = 4 is also known to be sharp, see [9,
Proposition 4.5].
We study here the class of pencil-type conic-line arrangements with transversal base points
coming from pencils of degree 3 curves.
Theorem 1. Let C be a pencil-type conic-line arrangement arising from a pencil of degree 3 curves
such that the singularities of C at the base points of the pencil are transversal.Then the multiplicity
of primitive roots of unity of odd order as roots of the Alexander polynomial ∆C(t) is combinatorial.
Moreover, if there are at most two non-ordinary singular points in Sing(C), then the multiplicity
of primitive roots of unity of order powers of 2 as roots of the Alexander polynomial ∆C(t) is
combinatorial as well.
Furthermore, we determine the Alexander polynomial of the curves C described in the above
theorem.

2. Preliminaries
Let C = C1 ∪· · · ∪Cr be the decomposition into irreducible components of the reduced plane
projective curve C and denote di := deg(Ci).
The data that consists of:
• Sing(C), the set of singularities of C;
• the set of labels r := {1, . . . , r} of the irreducible components;
• for each P ∈Sing(C) the set of local branches ∆P of C at P;
• the map ϕP that assigns to each branch at P the label of the irreducible component it
belongs to;
• the map that assigns to each pair of branches (from distinct components) at P the inter-
section number of the two;
is called the weak combinatorial type of the curve C, see [7]. We will denote it by WC. The weak
combinatorial type can be seen as playing the same role for curves as the notion of intersection
lattice for arrangements of hyperplanes. Furthermore, the notion of abstract curve combinatorics
was developed in [13] to capture formally the defining characteristics of a weak combinatorial
type, see [13] for a precise definition. Taking further the parallelism to the theory of hyperplane
arrangements, an abstract curve combinatorics is reminiscent of the notion of matroid. We will
get back to this in Subsection §4.1.
Keeping in mind the analogy to arrangements of hyperplanes, we will call an invariant or prop-
erty of a curve C combinatorial if it depends only on the weak combinatorial type of C. For instance,
Cogolludo-Matei prove that the cohomology algebra of the complement of a plane curve is com-
binatorial, building on a similar result by Cogolludo for rational curves, see [7, 12]. This mirrors
Orlik-Solomon’s emblematic result on the combinatoriality of the cohomology of the complement
of an arrangement of hyperplanes in a complex vector space.

Finally, we state a classic result on the Alexander polynomial of a curve. To this end, recall that
the local Alexander polynomial of a singularity P of a curve C is the characteristic polynomial of

Theorem 9. ([25, 16]) The Alexander polynomial of a curve C divides the product of the local

any irreducible component C of C, the Alexander polynomial of C divides the product of the local
Alexander polynomials ∆(C,P)(t), product taken over all singularities P ∈Sing(C) situated on the
component C.

3. Pencils of conic-line curves
The next classification result on pencils of conic-line curves with transversal intersections at the
base points is central to the proofs of our main theorems.
Theorem 12. ([9]) A primitive pencil P of degree d ≥3 curves with transversal intersections at
the base points has at most m = 6 fibers which are conic-line arrangements.
Moreover, as a direct consequence of the results from [9] and their proofs (that involve estimating
the Euler characteristic of the surface induced by the pencil by blowing up at the base points, in
terms of the Euler characteristics of the fibers; see for instance the proofs of [9, Prop 3.2] and [9,
Thm 1.4]), one has, in such pencils, certain restrictions on the number of conic-line fibers which
are pencils of lines. This is stated explicitly in [44], as an outcome of a particular instance of the
general techniques used in [9].
Proposition 13. In the hypothesis of Theorem 12, if n denotes the number of those fibers in the
pencil P that are pencils of d lines, then n ≤3 and n = 1 implies m ≤5, n = 2 implies m ≤4,
respectively n = 3 implies m = 3.

4.1. An abstract weak combinatorial type. Notice that Proposition 19 treats the case |N| = 4,
minus the situation when all base point type singularities involve an even number of lines. In this
situation, it can happen (at least theoretically) that β2(C) > 2. More precisely, we show next
that there exists an ’abstract’ weak combinatorial type W such that β2(W) = 3. This will be an
instance of the concept of abstract curve combinatorics, introduced by Cogolludo-Agust´ın & Marco-
Buzun´ariz in [13]. This notion, in relation to curves, can be seen through analogy to the notion of
matroid in relation to arrangements of hyperplanes. The intersection lattice of an arrangement of
hyperplanes is in particular a matroid, but the class of matroids is strictly larger than the class of
intersection lattices of hyperplane arrangements. A matroid that is isomorphic to the intersection
lattice of a hyperplane arrangement is called realizable. Likewise, to a curve one associates the
weak combinatorial type, which is in particular an abstract curve combinatorics.
Recall that the generalized Aomoto complex defined in Section 2 is combinatorial, in the sense
that it depends only on the weak combinatorial type of the curve C. So one can extend seamlessly
the notion of generalized Aomoto complex to an abstract weak combinatorial type.
