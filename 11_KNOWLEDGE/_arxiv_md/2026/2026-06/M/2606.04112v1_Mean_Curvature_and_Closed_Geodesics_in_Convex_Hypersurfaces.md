MEAN CURVATURE AND CLOSED GEODESICS IN CONVEX
HYPERSURFACES
JAMES DIBBLE AND JOSEPH ANSEL HOISINGTON
Abstract. We give a sharp lower bound for the total mean curvature of a convex hypersurface in
Euclidean space in terms of the length of a shortest nontrivial closed geodesic, generalizing a result
of Paiva [Pai97] for convex surfaces. This result is based on a sharp lower bound for the mean width
of a convex hypersurface in terms of its Birkhoff invariant, which also gives sharp lower bounds for
a broader array of total curvature functionals. We also characterize spheres as the unique convex
hypersurfaces whose planar sections containing chords of maximal length are all as long as possible.

Theorem 1.3. Let M n be a closed, convex hypersurface in Rn+1, n ≥2, and w+(M) its extrinsic
diameter (as in Definition 3.1). Suppose every affine 2-plane containing points x, y ∈M with |y−x| =
w+(M) intersects M in a curve of length πw+(M). Then, M is a round sphere.
Theorem 1.3 complements rigidity results for convex hypersurfaces discussed in [MMO19, Chapter 9].
For example, Montejano [Mon91] proved that every convex hypersurface whose k-sections through a
point all have constant width must be round, and S¨uss [S¨us47] showed that, for n = 2, only the sphere
has congruent planar sections in each direction.
Paiva proved the inequality in Theorem 1.1 in the case n = 2 in [Pai97]. Paiva’s proof of this
inequality can be adapted to prove the inequality in Theorem 1.2 in all dimensions. A non-sharp
version of the inequality in Theorem 1.1 for n = 2 also follows from Minkowski’s inequality between
the mean curvature of a convex surface and its area [Min03] and results of Treibergs and Croke
bounding the area of a convex surface below in terms of the length of its shortest closed geodesic
[Tre85, Cro88].
None of these arguments or results, however, gives a description of the equality
conditions in Theorems 1.1 and 1.2, even for smooth convex surfaces in R3. The equality conditions
in Theorems 1.1 and 1.2 admit alternate proofs under some additional assumptions, which we discuss
in Remarks 3.17 and 5.2, but these draw on results such as the proof of the Blaschke conjecture
[Bes78]. It therefore seems that the characterization of equality in Paiva’s result, as in Theorems 1.1
and 1.2, requires an argument like the one given here.
Theorem 1.1 is similar in spirit to results of Willmore and Chen [Wil65, Che71a, Che71b] that give
lower bounds for the integral of the n-th power of the mean curvature of a hypersurface in Rn+1.
For hypersurfaces of dimension n ≥3, however, the authors do not know of general results bounding
the integral of the mean curvature itself from below in terms of the length of the shortest closed
geodesic or similar geometric invariants, with the exception of a result for convex 3-spheres discussed
in Remark 5.2. Theorem 1.2 gives a lower bound for the mean width of a smooth convex hypersurface
in terms of the length of the shortest closed geodesic, recorded in (15), because of Birkhoff’s result
cited above, but the Birkhoff invariant of a general convex hypersurface does not seem to admit a
geometrically natural representative—generic convex polyhedra, for example, have no simple closed
geodesics [Gru91, Gal03]. In this sense, Theorem 1.2 seems to be the simplest geometrically natural
statement of its type.

A geodesic in M is a constant-speed path γ : [a, b] →M that is locally length-minimizing and,
therefore, realizes the distance between nearby points. It is minimal if its length is the distance
between its endpoints. Lytchak showed that, in embedded C1,α submanifolds of smooth manifolds,
geodesics are as regular as the submanifold itself.
Theorem 2.2 ([Lyt05, Theorem 1.2]). Let 0 < α ≤1. If M is an isometrically embedded C1,α
submanifold of a smooth Riemannian manifold, then geodesics in M are C1,α.
Remark 2.3. Geodesics in manifolds with C0,1 Riemannian metrics are C1,1 [LY06], but without a
sufficiently regular immersion into an ambient smooth manifold, many important properties are lost.
For example, with C1 metrics, the initial-value problem for the geodesic equation might fail to have
unique solutions [Har50] and solutions to the geodesic equation might not realize the distance between
nearby points [HW51].

3. Convex hypersurfaces with long diametrical slices
Any 2-plane containing a pair of antipodal points in a sphere of radius r slices the sphere in a circle
of length 2πr. It follows from Crofton’s formula (Theorem 3.2 below) that this is the longest possible
plane section of a convex hypersurface of extrinsic diameter 2r. The primary aim of this section is to
prove Theorem 1.3, which says that this property characterizes the sphere.

used in the proof of Theorem 1.3 and, in turn, Theorems 1.1 and 1.2.

Definition 3.8. A diametrical segment of a closed, convex hypersurface M n in Rn+1 is a chord
of M of length w+(M). Two points of M are diametrically opposed if they are connected by a
diametrical segment. If a 2-plane Π contains a diametrical segment of M, its intersection with M is a
diametrical slice. If all diametrical slices of M have length πw+(M), then M has long diametrical
slices.
Note that every planar convex curve has long diametrical slices, according to the definition above,
and that the conclusion of Theorem 1.3 is not valid in this case.
