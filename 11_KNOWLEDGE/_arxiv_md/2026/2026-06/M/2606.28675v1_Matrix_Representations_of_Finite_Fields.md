Abstract
Finite fields are important algebraic structures that have a wide range
of applications in fields such as coding theory and cryptography.
But
the standard construction of finite field extensions through polynomial
quotients is computationally opaque, especially when we want to identify
a degree-2 extension of F8 and a degree-3 extension of F4.
In this short note, we present a coherent family of representations by
matrices ρn

These maps are chosen so that concatenating ρm

subfield chains F64/F8/F2 and F64/F4/F2 at the same time. A variant ϱ

a cyclic shift of rows and columns.
From an educational point of view, these rhos give explicit and self-

polynomial, and Frobenius all become visible through matrix algebra ac-
cessible to most students.
From a theoretical point of view, the con-

normal basis theorem.

Introduction
Finite fields have many practical applications in coding theory, cryptography,
randomized algorithms, combinatorial design, and other related fields [MM07,
LNC09, MMPS10, MP13]. They are particularly useful because their elements
can be represented by a finite amount of memory, and they have all the algebraic
operations we like, especially division.
There are, however, some subtleties when it comes to implementing them
on a computer. Take F64 as an example. The standard approach is to find a
degree-6 irreducible polynomial f 6
2 (x) = e0 + e1x + · · · + e6x6 ∈F2[x] to form
F64 := F2[ϵ]/⟨f 6
2 (ϵ)⟩. Each element of F64 is then encoded by 6 bits, understood
as the coefficients of a polynomial. The problem with this implementation is
that it is not possible to compute the product of “111000” and “010101” without
looking up f 6
2 or the multiplication table. Another common implementation of
F64 is to represent each element as a power of ϵ.
This way, multiplication
becomes addition of the exponents, but addition requires table lookups again.
To sum up, standard implementations of finite fields tend to favor either the
additive structure or the multiplicative structure, leaving the other opaque and
dependent on a nontrivial lookup.

Preliminaries
A field is a set with addition and multiplication that have additive inverses, mul-
tiplicative inverses for nonzero elements, associativity for both operators, com-
mutativity for both operators, and distributivity of multiplication over addition.
A finite field (or a Galois field) is a finite set equipped with field operations.
The following well-known result classifies all finite fields.
Fact 2 (finite field classification). There exists a finite field F of size q if and
only if q = pk for some prime p and positive exponent k. Moreover, F is unique
up to isomorphism for each such q. This unique field is usually denoted by Fq
or GF(q). See [LNC09, Theorem 2.5] for a proof.

The theoretical reason that Fpk is unique is that it is the splitting field of
the polynomial xpk −x over Fp, and the splitting field of a polynomial is unique
up to isomorphism. However, implementing finite fields in a computer algebra
system (CAS)34567 involves making choices and breaking the symmetry. More
precisely, we need to choose polynomials f k
p to construct Fpk as the quotient ring
Fp[κ]/⟨f k
p (κ)⟩. Afterwards there are two equally popular options: One option
is to represent an element of Fpk as a polynomial in κ of degree less than k. To
do so, we record a k-tuple of Fp-elements, which are essentially k integers in the
range [0, p −1]. The other option is to represent a nonzero element of Fpk as a
power of κ, and so we record an integer in the range [1, pk −1], and the integer
0 is reserved for the additive unit of Fpk. The latter option turns multiplication
into addition of the exponents and addition into looking up a table [LNC09,
Exercise 2.8]8 of size pk. When pk integers fit nicely into a computer’s memory,
this is preferred over multiplying polynomials modulo fk
p . The only caveat is
that the elements need to be powers of κ, so not every κ works.
Fact 3 (multiplicative group). The multiplicative group of a finite field F,
denoted by F ∗or F ×, is cyclic. An element that generates the whole group is
said to be primitive. If one root of an irreducible polynomial is primitive, then
all roots are, and the polynomial is called a primitive polynomial. See [LNC09,
Theorem 2.8] for a proof.
The first obstacle we encounter along these implementation approaches is
when we need to identify subfields in a large finite field. When is a finite field
a subfield of another finite field? How do we derive the embedding map using
the two f’s? To answer these, recall the following results.
Fact 4 (subfield criteria). Fix a prime p. Let d and k be positive exponents.
The following are equivalent.
• d divides k.
• pd −1 divides pk −1.
• Fpd is a subfield of Fpk.
• F∗
pd is a subgroup of F∗
pk.
• Fpk is a vector space over Fpd.
This is a combination of [LNC09, Lemma 2.1, Theorem 2.6, and Exercise 2.9].

Concluding Remarks
The two constructions above emphasize the same principle from different direc-
tions. Conway-compatible primitive elements organize inclusions among finite
fields, while normal bases organize the Frobenius action. In both cases, the
visible matrix patterns come from making the degree factorization visible at the
level of bases. However, there does not seem to be a perfect way to add the
Frobenius action to the picture of Theorem 1. It is therefore natural to ask
what the next best possibilities are: how much of the block structure can be
retained while also making the Frobenius action visible?
