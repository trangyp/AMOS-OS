Modification of jet-energy flow in heavy-ion collisions
ALICE Collaboration*
Abstract
The ALICE Collaboration presents the first measurements of the jet-energy flow (∆pT) observable
in proton-proton and heavy-ion collisions. Jets are excellent probes for the quark–gluon plasma, a
deconfined state of matter produced in heavy-ion collisions. The jet-energy flow observable charac-
terizes the radial distribution of energy from the jet axis in an infrared and collinear-safe way and
is sensitive to medium-induced parton-shower modifications. Inclusive charged jets are measured in
Pb–Pb collisions at √sNN = 5.02 TeV for the transverse-momentum interval 60–80 GeV/c. For pp
collisions at √s = 13 TeV, measurements include the 40–60 and 60–80 GeV/c intervals, where the
latter serves as the reference for investigating medium-induced modifications. Results show that most
parton energy is concentrated in the jet core, with a clear suppression of energy flow in heavy-ion
collisions at larger radii (significance 3.5–4.5σ) indicating a narrowing of the energy flow. While all
models – PYTHIA 8, HERWIG, JEWEL, and JETSCAPE – reproduce the pp results with only small
deviations in the tails, the relative modification in Pb–Pb collisions is well described by JEWEL
without recoil. Conversely, JEWEL with recoil (medium response) and JETSCAPE show significant
deviations, exhibiting increasing or more constant trends with radius that are disfavored by the data.

Introduction
High-energy collisions at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) provide an ideal environment to study
strongly interacting matter and to test the theory of quantum chromodynamics (QCD). In particular,
heavy-ion collisions create extreme conditions of temperature and energy density, under which ordi-
nary hadronic matter undergoes a transition to a deconfined state of quarks and gluons, known as the
quark–gluon plasma (QGP) [1, 2]. Jets, which are collimated sprays of particles, serve as multi-scale
probes of the QGP. Originating from hard scatterings of the initially interacting partons, their production
cross sections can be calculated within perturbative QCD, making them quantitatively well-controlled
observables.
As these partons propagate through the QGP, they lose energy to the medium and their internal structure
is modified, an effect known as jet quenching [3–7]. The aforementioned modifications to the jet and
its substructure arise from the interplay between medium-induced emissions, which redistribute energy
to larger angles, combined with the stronger suppression of wide jets due to incoherent energy loss of
resolved subjets (color decoherence) [8–12]. By investigating the mechanisms of jet energy loss, one
gains direct insight into these parton–medium interactions and the transport properties of the QGP [13–
15].
A number of jet and jet-substructure measurements have been previously reported by ALICE [14–16],
ATLAS [7, 17, 18] and CMS [19–22] to study these parton-medium interactions. In this paper, we
explore a new observable, known as the jet-energy flow (JEF) ∆pT, which provides an event-by-event
measure of these modifications. Defined in Eq. 1, it is an infrared- and collinear-safe observable that
characterizes how the energy inside a reconstructed jet is distributed as a function of the angular opening
(radius) from the jet axis. Consequently, ∆pT is directly sensitive to medium-induced modifications of
parton-shower evolution.
∆pT = pT,Ri+1 −pT,Ri,
(1)
where pT,Ri denotes the transverse momentum of the jet reconstructed with the smaller radius and pT,Ri+1
that of the matched jet reconstructed with the larger radius within a similar η–ϕ region in the same
event. Here, η and ϕ are the pseudorapidity and azimuthal angle, respectively; further details regarding
the matching procedure are provided in the section 3.1. The difference between these two values probes
the radial distribution of jet energy relative to the jet axis.
The differential jet shape observable which measures the radial energy-density profile around the jet axis
in annular regions is closely related to the ∆pT observable [23]. The main difference between the two
observables is that the axes of the jets with different radii in the energy flow measure are not necessarily
precisely aligned, while the radial profile is measured with respect to a fixed axis. In addition, the energy-
flow measurement provides an event-by-event characterization of parton-shower modification induced by
the QGP.
In this paper, the ALICE Collaboration presents the first measurements of the JEF ∆pT in proton-proton
(pp) and heavy-ion collisions. The measurement is performed for inclusive (a mixture of gluon- and
quark-initiated jets) charged jets in Pb–Pb collisions at √sNN = 5.02 TeV in the transverse-momentum
interval 60–80 GeV/c. In pp collisions, measurements are carried out in the intervals 40–60 GeV/c and
60–80 GeV/c at √s = 13 TeV, with the latter used as the reference for investigating medium-induced
modifications. To maximize the available statistics, pp collisions at √s = 13 TeV (integrated luminosity
Lint = 25.81±0.43 nb−1 [16, 24]) are used instead of √s = 5.02 TeV (Lint = 18.0±0.4 nb−1 [16, 24])
for the reference measurement. The JEF modification, defined as the ratio of the measured JEF in Pb–Pb
collisions at 5.02 TeV to that in pp collisions at 13 TeV, is compared to a PYTHIA-based ratio of pp
collisions at 5 TeV to pp collisions at 13 TeV, which accounts for the beam-energy dependence of the
pp baseline. The PYTHIA-based baseline is used for comparison because it provides the best descrip-
tion of the pp measurements, compared to other models. The results are also compared to predictions
from Monte-Carlo (MC) event generators and theoretical models, including the JETSCAPE and JEWEL
model, providing insight into QCD dynamics in pp and Pb–Pb collisions. These measurements place

Experimental setup and data sets
This analysis uses charged-particle tracks reconstructed with the ALICE central-barrel tracking sys-
tem, covering |η| < 0.9 inside a solenoidal magnet. The detectors used are the Inner Tracking System
(ITS) [25] and the Time Projection Chamber (TPC) [26]. This system provides high tracking efficiency
in the range 0.15 < pT < 100 GeV/c, with uniform acceptance in |η| < 0.9 and 0 < ϕ < 2π. The ITS
is the innermost tracking detector of ALICE, designed for high-resolution tracking and vertexing. It
consists of six cylindrical layers of silicon detectors: two layers each of Silicon Pixel Detectors (SPD),
Silicon Drift Detectors, and Silicon Strip Detectors. The ITS enables precise reconstruction of primary
and secondary vertices and particle tracking at low transverse momentum. The TPC is the main tracking
detector in ALICE and it plays a crucial role in particle identification. It is a large cylindrical gas-filled
chamber with readout planes at both ends. Charged particles traversing the TPC ionize the gas, and the
resulting electrons drift toward the readout planes under an applied electric field. A detailed description
of the ALICE detector and its performance is provided in Refs. [27, 28].
The results presented in this paper are based on pp collisions at a centre-of-mass energy of √s = 13 TeV
recorded by ALICE during 2016–2018 and Pb–Pb collisions at √sNN = 5.02 TeV recorded in 2018. The
MC samples used for the corrections described in Section 3.2 are generated using the PYTHIA 8.2 event
generator [29] with the Monash 2013 tune [30], and with the GEANT 3.21.11 [31] transport model. The
ALICE detector layout, along with variations in the data-taking conditions during the run, are reproduced
in the simulation.
3
Analysis method
Events used in this analysis are selected using the minimum bias (MB) trigger, defined by coincident
hits on the V0 scintillator detectors [32] and a time selection on the coincidence of the signals in the
Zero-Degree Calorimeters (ZDCs) [33] on both sides of the experiment. The V0 detector consists of two
scintillator arrays placed on either side of the interaction point along the beam axis, covering the pseu-
dorapidity ranges 2.8 < η < 5.1 (V0A) and −3.7 < η < −1.7 (V0C). The MB trigger is activated by a
simultaneous signal in V0A and V0C, coinciding with a bunch crossing. When ZDC information is avail-
able, a further selection is made on the timing of the ZDC coincidence compared to the bunch-crossing
time to reject out-of-time background. For Pb–Pb data, in addition to the MB trigger, an online centrality
trigger is applied to select the most central events. This analysis focuses on the 0–10% centrality class,
where jet quenching effects are expected to be strongest. To ensure uniform detector acceptance, events
are required to have a primary vertex located within ±10 cm of the nominal interaction point along the
beam direction. Events with more than one reconstructed vertex are excluded to avoid pileup effects.
ALICE achieves high reconstruction efficiency for charged particles within the transverse-momentum
range of 0.15 < pT < 100 GeV/c.
The single track reconstruction efficiency rises from ∼60% at
0.15 GeV/c, plateauing at ∼80% for tracks with pT > 0.4 GeV/c. The track transverse-momentum
resolution, σpT/pT, is approximately 1% at pT = 1 GeV/c, increasing to about 4% at pT = 50 GeV/c.
To ensure uniform coverage in the η–ϕ plane, the analysis utilizes a “hybrid track” sample consisting
of three categories. First, global tracks must include at least one hit in the SPD and satisfy quality
criteria [34], including ≥70 TPC space points (out of a maximum 159) and ≥80% of the geometrically
findable clusters. Second, complementary tracks lack SPD hits but meet all other standard criteria. Third,
the sample includes tracks with a poor fit, characterized by a high χ2 value or missing expected hits in the
ITS layers. To ensure momentum accuracy and reduce contamination, both complementary and poor-fit
tracks are refitted with a constraint to the primary vertex of the event. This analysis selects tracks with
pT ≥0.15 GeV/c within |η| ≤0.9 over the full azimuth (0 < ϕ < 2π).

3.1
Jet reconstruction and jet-energy flow calculation
The selected tracks serve as input for the jet-clustering algorithms. In this analysis, jets with varying
radii from R = 0.05 to 0.4, in steps of ∆Rstep = 0.05 (i.e., R = 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25, 0.3, 0.35,
and 0.4), are reconstructed in the same event to examine the pT difference between jets. Apart from
the jet-resolution parameter, the same configuration is used for the reconstruction of all jets. Jets are
reconstructed using the anti-kT clustering algorithm [35], as implemented in FastJet [36]. Charged jets
are considered and clustered using the E-recombination scheme, in which the four-momenta of the jet
constituents are combined. The reconstructed jets are required to satisfy |ηjet| ≤0.5 and a minimum jet
pT of 10 GeV/c. A ghost area of 0.005 is used for the jet-area determination, where low-momentum
ghost particles are added uniformly in the event to estimate the active jet area. In Pb–Pb collisions, jets
are additionally required to contain a leading hadron with pT ≥8 GeV/c.
Jets are then paired into “[narrower, wider]” combinations to ensure sufficient area overlap, using adja-
cent radius pairs: [Rjet1,Rjet2] = [0.05, 0.1], [0.1, 0.15], [0.15, 0.2], [0.2, 0.25], [0.25, 0.3], [0.3, 0.35],
[0.35, 0.4]. To select true [Rjet1,Rjet2] matched pairs, bijective matching is performed within ∆R < 0.2.
In the bijective matching procedure, each Rjet1 jet is matched to its closest Rjet2 jet, and vice versa. If
two jets identify each other as their respective closest match, they form a valid jet pair. The matching
criterion is based solely on the jet-axis positions and does not require a shared pT fraction between the
jets. In practice, the axes of matched jets with different radii are found to be strongly aligned, typically
with ∆R < 0.05. For each valid jet pair, the energy flow is probed by calculating the transverse momen-
tum difference ∆pT as ∆pT = pT,2 −pT,1, where pT,1 and pT,2 denote the transverse momenta of jet1 and
jet2, respectively, with jet2 having the larger radius (Rjet2 > Rjet1). This procedure is repeated until all
jets in each radius pair are exhausted. A schematic illustration of the matching procedure between jets
of different radii is shown in Fig. 1. The ∆pT observable is evaluated in the 40 < pch jet

3.2
Corrections
3.2.1
Background subtraction
In pp collisions, background contributions from the underlying event (UE) are negligible in the studied
jet-pT interval. Furthermore, any small residual contribution is further reduced since we examine the
difference in pT between jets that lie in close proximity within the η–ϕ plane. In Pb–Pb collisions, a
substantial background of uncorrelated soft particles originating mostly from soft production processes
is present. This background can significantly inflate the measured jet energy and distort its internal struc-
ture. The thermal background is subtracted using the event-by-event constituent-subtraction method [37].
This subtraction method introduces "ghost particles" to the event with transverse momentum pg
T = ρ ·Ag,
where ρ is the median background density and Ag is the small area occupied by a single ghost. The

method iteratively identifies the closest pair of a real particle and a ghost particle in the η −ϕ plane. For
each identified real-ghost particle pair, provided their separation is within a maximum distance, ∆Rmax
(a key free parameter of the method), the momentum of the softer of the two particles is subtracted
from the harder one. The four-momentum of the softer particle is then set to zero. This step is repeated
for all matched pairs. After the iteration is complete, all particles (real or ghost) with zero momentum
are discarded from the event. The jet-finding algorithm is then applied to the resulting collection of
background-subtracted particles to reconstruct the background-corrected jets. It should also be noted
that due to the definition of the energy flow observable as ∆pT, the contributions of shared background
fluctuations in the matched jets will cancel.
The free parameter ∆Rmax controls the locality of the subtraction. In this analysis, its value is tuned
based on the jet radius being studied to avoid over- or under-subtraction. For jets with R ≤0.2, a value
of ∆Rmax = 0.1 is used, while for larger jets, ∆Rmax = 0.25 is employed. The sensitivity to this choice is
evaluated as a systematic uncertainty.

optimal value is found to be four for both pp and Pb–Pb collisions. Closure tests are also performed to
ensure the robustness and reliability of the unfolding procedure. Two types of closure tests are carried
out. A refolding test is performed to verify that the detector-level spectrum can be recovered by reversing
the unfolding procedure. In this test, the unfolded result is multiplied by the response matrix, and the
resulting spectrum is compared with the detector-level spectrum. In addition, a closure test is performed
in which simulated data at the detector level are unfolded and compared with the corresponding truth-
level distributions. For the test, the simulated data are divided into two statistically independent subsets:
one is used to determine the response matrix, while the other serves as a test sample. For both closure
tests, good agreement is observed, with only small bin-by-bin fluctuations.
4
Systematic uncertainties
The kinematic selections and background correction methods have inherent limitations and associated
uncertainties. To assess the resilience of the final measurement, the relevant analysis parameters are
varied within the limits of their known uncertainties, and the resulting impact on the final result is evalu-
ated. The total uncertainty is calculated as the quadratic sum of the individual contributions listed below
assuming that these contributions are uncorrelated.

1. Tracking Efficiency: This is a dominant source of uncertainty, arising from the imperfect knowl-
edge of the detector’s efficiency in reconstructing charged-particle tracks. The tracking efficiency
uncertainty is estimated to be 3% in pp collisions and 3–5% in Pb–Pb collisions, depending on
track pT, based on variations of the track selection criteria and the uncertainty in the ITS–TPC
track-matching efficiency in the MC simulation [15]. The associated systematic uncertainty is
evaluated by creating a modified response matrix where a fraction of tracks equal to the corre-
sponding uncertainty are randomly rejected. The final unfolded data using this modified response
is compared to the default result to determine the systematic uncertainty.
2. Unfolding Procedure This addresses uncertainties inherent in the statistical unfolding method
itself. It includes three variations:
– Regularization parameter (Niter): The number of iterations in the Bayesian unfolding al-
gorithm is varied by ±2 (number based on the performance of the closure tests) around the
nominal value to test the stability of the result. The average difference between the results
obtained with these two variations is selected as the relative systematic uncertainty.
– Truncation: The lower limit of the jet-pT range used at the detector level is varied by ±5
GeV/c to check for sensitivity to the chosen kinematic boundaries.
– Prior variation: The dependence on the shape of the true distribution from MC (the prior) is
tested. In order to evaluate this systematic, we fit the prior ∆pT distribution with an exponen-
tial function. The slope of this function is varied by ±5% and a new response is constructed
for each variation by scaling the prior with ∆pT-dependent weight. The raw distribution is
then unfolded using the modified response matrices. The average deviation of the result-
ing ratios with respect to the default result is assigned as the relative systematic uncertainty
associated with this source.
These three sources probe the same source of uncertainty, so the total unfolding uncertainty is
estimated here as the standard deviation of all three contributions from zero.
3. Model Dependence: This uncertainty accounts for the choice of the MC event generator used to
compute the corrections and the response matrix.
– pp: To investigate the sensitivity of the final results to the choice of event generator used
for deriving the correction factors, a comparison of unfolded results obtained with different
generators is performed. To reduce the computing cost for this test, the study uses a fast
simulation with parametrized response of the ALICE detector instead of a full simulation us-
ing GEANT. Simulation samples were generated with both PYTHIA and HERWIG [40] and
passed through this parametrized detector response. The corresponding response matrices
were then constructed and used to unfold the raw-data distribution. The relative difference
between the results unfolded with HERWIG and those unfolded with PYTHIA is taken as
the systematic uncertainty due to model dependence.
– Pb–Pb: The same procedure is applied for Pb–Pb collisions, using the JEWEL [41] generator
without the inclusion of medium partons recoiling after interactions with the jet to evaluate
the model-dependence systematic uncertainty.
4. Background Subtraction Method (Pb–Pb only): This uncertainty is specific to the heavy-ion
analysis and quantifies the sensitivity to the parameters of the constituent subtraction method. The
free parameter ∆Rmax is varied to explore potential over- or under-subtraction of the background.
To evaluate the systematic uncertainty, ∆Rmax is varied to 0.025 for R ≤0.2 and to 0.3 for larger
jet radii. The full analysis is repeated for data and embedded MC with the varied parameter, and
the difference in the final unfolded result is taken as the systematic uncertainty.

These models incorporate different implementations of the microscopic properties of the medium, its
evolution, and the jet–medium interaction. In JETSCAPE [46], a medium-modified parton shower is
described by MATTER and LBT. The bulk medium initial conditions are setup; using the TRENTO
model [47] followed by (2+1)-dimensional free-streaming pre-equilibrium evolution [48] and the QGP
medium evolution is described by the VISHNU (2+1)-dimensional hydrodynamics [49]. In the JEWEL
event generator [41, 42], the hard-scattering event is generated by PYTHIA 6, followed by a medium-
modified shower which takes into account elastic scattering and splittings, including formation time. The
medium is modeled as a boost-invariant, longitudinally expanding ideal quark–gluon gas and hadroniza-
tion is handled by PYTHIA 6 [42]. The parameter set from Ref. [50], which is appropriate for the
kinematic range of this measurement, is used. The medium partons recoiling after interactions with the
jet constituents can either be discarded from the event (recoils off) or allowed to hadronize together with
the jet (recoils on). For the JEWEL prediction with recoil on, the contribution from thermal partons
is subtracted using the momenta of the medium partons stored in the JEWEL output and a subtraction
algorithm that is based on the constituent-subtraction algorithm [51].
The models show overall agreement with the measured Pb–Pb/pp ratios. For the JETSCAPE model, the
agreement is better in pp collisions (as shown in Fig. 3) than in Pb–Pb collisions, while the Pb–Pb/pp
ratio is still reasonably well reproduced. In contrast, JEWEL in pp shows overall good agreement, with
some deviations in the tails of the distributions for jet pairs with small radii. For Pb–Pb/pp ratios, JEWEL
with recoil on and off performs similarly for jet pairs with small radii. However, for jet pairs with larger
radii (Rjet ≥0.20), larger deviations are observed for the recoil-on case, while the recoil-off case shows
better agreement. This behavior is also observed in other observables, such as the RAA double ratio [14].

6
Conclusions
The JEF is measured in pp collisions at √s = 13 TeV and in central Pb–Pb collisions (0–10% centrality
class) at √sNN = 5.02 TeV with ALICE. In pp collisions, the JEF exhibits a clear ordering with jet radius,
with the energy concentrated in the jet core and decreasing smoothly toward larger radii, consistent with
collimated vacuum parton showers and well described by several MC generators, with some deviations
observed in the tails of the distributions. This establishes a robust baseline for the Pb–Pb measurement.
In Pb–Pb collisions, the JEF distributions are significantly steeper than in pp, and a suppression at large
∆pT is observed, which increases with jet radius. This behavior indicates a narrowing of the jet energy

profile due to medium-induced modifications. Comparisons to theoretical models show that while the
overall trends are qualitatively reproduced, the description depends sensitively on the treatment of the
medium response. Extending future measurements to larger jet radii will enable the investigation of a
possible redistribution of radiation at large angles. Furthermore, extending the measurement to lower
and higher jet momenta will allow the study of the pT dependence of the jet-energy flow.
