MCS SPACES ARE CS
MOHAMMAD ALATTAR AND LEWIS TADMAN
Abstract. In this paper we further develop the theory of MCS spaces. Our main result
shows that MCS spaces, as defined by Perelman, are CS sets with respect to their MCS
stratification, and that in fact, the intrinsic stratification agrees with the MCS stratification.
As a consequence, we improve on Perelman’s result and answer affirmatively a question by
Fujioka.
1. Introduction
In 1972, Siebenmann [35] introduced two classes of stratified spaces, CS sets (see Definition
2.6) and WCS sets. These are, respectively, locally and weakly cone-like spaces that can be
stratified by manifolds. The main difference, roughly speaking, between CS and WCS sets
is that CS sets are stratified spaces where the local cone charts are witnessed by homeomor-
phisms that preserve the strata with respect to some stratification on the link of the cone
factor. On the other hand, WCS sets are stratified spaces where the local cone charts pre-
serve only the bottom stratum (strictly speaking, the definition of WCS set allows for more
general spaces than cones. These spaces are called mock open cones and will not concern
us). CS sets are WCS, and examples of CS sets abound. For instance, all topological mani-
folds, all locally finite simplicial complexes and polyhedra are examples of CS sets. In [25],
Perelman introduced a general class of stratified spaces termed MCS (see Definition 2.9).
These spaces are, by definition, spaces with multiple conical singularities. Perelman showed
that these spaces are examples of WCS sets. Then using the structure of finite-dimensional
Alexandrov spaces with curvature bounded below, he showed that Alexandrov spaces are
MCS. Combining the geometric structure with the WCS structure, Perelman established his
stability theorem [25]. Recently, Fujioka improved on Perelman’s result, and among other
interesting results, showed that Alexandrov spaces with the extremal set stratification, as
defined by Perelman–Petrunin [26], are CS sets [9]. Fujioka inquired whether the same is
true for Alexandrov spaces with respect to their MCS stratification, and whether the MCS
stratification agrees with the intrinsic one [9, Question 3.3]. Note that, as observed in [9,
Example 2.26], the extremal set stratification does not coincide with the MCS stratification,
as the double suspension of the Poincaré homology sphere readily shows.
Our main theorem states that all MCS spaces are CS with their MCS stratification, and
in fact, the intrinsic stratification agrees with the MCS stratification, thereby improving on
Perelman’s result, and answering affirmatively Fujioka’s question for the more general class
of MCS spaces (for clarity, we reiterate that every Alexandrov space is an MCS space).
Theorem A. Every MCS space with the MCS stratification is a CS set. In fact, the MCS
stratification coincides with the intrinsic stratification.

The next corollary shows that Alexandrov spaces have a special tangent microbundle struc-
ture along their MCS strata.
Corollary B. Let X be a MCS space, C be any component of any MCS stratum, and y ∈C,
and L a link of y. Then the canonical diagram C →C × X →C yields a c(Sn−1 ∗L)
microbundle, where n is the dimension of the stratum. In particular, the map C →C × X is
the diagonal map and the map C × X →C is the projection map.
The proof to the corollary follows immediately from the main theorem in conjunction with
[1, Proposition 3.1]. In particular, the preceding corollary applies to Alexandrov spaces. Note
that the theory of microbundles is an important component of smoothing/PL theory (see for
example [19, 31]), and more recently, understanding PL structures on Alexandrov spaces has
garnered some interest (see for example [9]).
Finally, we mention that Theorem A, combined with [17, Theorem 2, Lemma 1], gives new
(and streamlined) proofs, and allows us to recover several (well known) results originally

Corollary C. If X is an MCS space that is a homology manifold, then
(1) X × R is a manifold.
(2) The set of non-manifold points of X is discrete.
(3) If X is 3 dimensional, then X is a manifold.

Note that establishing that certain spaces appearing in metric geometry (that are not neces-
sarily Alexandrov) satisfy the properties above is of interest. For example, recently Fujioka-
Gu [10] showed that locally BNPC spaces that are homology manifolds satisfy property (2),
generalizing earlier work due to Lytchak and Nagano in the CAT(0) setting [20, Theorem
1.2]. Furthermore, 3-dimensional locally BNPC spaces that are homology manifolds satisfy
property (3) above. This can be derived using the work of Thurston [37, Theorem 3.3] and
also the work of Fujioka-Gu [10, Theorem 3.9]. More is true. In fact, if X is a locally BNPC
space that is a homology manifold, then X × R is indeed a manifold and thus (1) results.
This follows from the proof of the topological regularity theorem due to Lytchak and Nagano
[20]. Namely, every 1-strained point in a locally BNPC homology manifold X is a manifold
point (see also [10, Theorem 7.1]). Since every point of X × R is obviously 1-strained in the
line direction, X × R must be a manifold (this was pointed out to us by Tadashi Fujioka,
whom we thank gratefully). In fact, one can be content with a topological argument in higher
dimensions, and in the ENR setting by appealing to the following result due to Bryant and
Lacher [5, Theorem 1.2]: For m ≥5, every generalized m-manifold X (an ENR that is a
homology manifold) with a discrete singular set is a codimension 1 manifold factor (note the
dimension restriction).
We now shall provide some additional context and mention related work. The structure of
MCS spaces has found several important consequences to the theory of Alexandrov spaces.
For example, the notion of orientability for Alexandrov spaces was developed in detail by
considering their MCS structure (see for example the work by Harvey–Searle [16] and also
see the work by Mitsuishi [22]). The Bonnet–Myers theorem for Alexandrov spaces can be
shown to follow from the fact that MCS spaces have universal covers as shown by Harvey
and Searle [16, Theorem 2.10]. Similarly, Harvey and Searle showed that the existence of a
ramified double cover for an Alexandrov space follows also from the corresponding existence
result for non-branching MCS spaces [16, Theorem 3.4].
In [23, Appendix], Yamaguchi
and Mituishi obtained refinements of Perelman’s fibration theorem by considering the MCS
structure of regular fibers of admissible functions. In general, understanding the structure
and properties of the strata of a given stratification on an Alexandrov spaces have yielded
fruitful consequences. For example, Harvey [15, Section 5] showed that extremal sets (which
stratify an Alexandrov space) satisfy a tameness property. This property allowed for the
application of a generalization of Palais’ covering homotopy theorem due to Harvey [14].
Note that stratified spaces, and CS sets in particular, play an important role in topology.
For example, Goresky and MacPherson [11] developed the celebrated theory of intersection
homology on CS sets, which has several applications. For example, Schütz [32, 33] used
intersection homology and stratified spaces to study moduli spaces of a closed linkage. In
high dimensions, these spaces turn out to be pseudomanifolds as observed by Schütz (see
[32, section 3] for more details). Additionally, Habegger and Saper generalized intersection
homology [12], and established several foundational results. We refer the reader to the books
by Banagl [3] and Friedman [8] for a modern treatment on the subject. As another exam-
ple, Steinberger and West [36] proved an equivariant version of Siebenmann’s CE-mapping
theorem ([34]) using the theory of CS sets.
Thus, our results may be viewed as forming a new bridge between metric geometry and the
theory of stratified spaces.

2. Preliminaries and Definitions
Before we begin, we denote the vertices of cones by o, and recall that the cone over the
empty set is simply a point. First, we give the definition of a filtration, and then define both
CS sets and MCS spaces, and mention some of their elementary properties that we shall use.
Finally, we introduce an invariant for MCS spaces, that captures, in some sense, how singular
a point is relative to the MCS structure. This will be our main tool. We provide examples
throughout and devote the majority of this section to studying this object.
Definition 2.1. A stratified set X is a metrizable space equipped with a filtration ∅=
X(−1) ⊆· · · ⊆X(n−1) ⊆X(n) ⊆... ⊆X such that each X(i) is closed in X, and the
components of the strata, X[i] = X(i) −X(i−1) are open in X[i].
We will mainly be interested in the case where X(n) = X for some n.
Remark 2.2. We shall often abuse language and merely denote a stratified set by X.
For example, a compact manifold Mn with boundary can be stratified by setting M(i) = ∅
for i < n −1, M (n−1) = ∂M and M (k) = M for all k ≥n. If M is a manifold without
boundary, then the stratification is given by M(n) = M and M (k) = ∅for k < n.
Example 2.3. We mention a few other examples that we shall need.
(1) (Product Stratification) If X and Y are stratified sets, then X × Y is a stratified set,
by declaring (X × Y )(i) = S
a+b=i X(a) × Y (b).
(2) (Cone Stratification) If X is a compact stratified set, then cX admits a stratification
by defining (cX)(−1) = ∅, (cX)(0) = vertex, and (cX)(i) = c(X(i−1)) for i ≥1.
Remark 2.4. Note that the strata in a stratified set are not required to be of the same
dimension as their index. In general, these two notions do not coincide.
Before beginning with the notion of a CS set, a few more additional preliminaries are in
order.
Definition 2.5. Let X and Y be stratified sets. An isomorphism h: X →Y is a homeo-
morphism such that h(X(n)) = Y (n) for all n ≥0.
Now we define the notion of a CS set.
Definition 2.6. A stratified set X is a CS set if
(1) The strata X[i] are topological manifolds of dimension i without boundary.
(2) X is locally cone-like: For each p ∈X, say in X[i], there exists an open neighborhood
U of p in X[i], and a compact stratified set L and an isomorphism U × cL →X onto
a neighborhood of p. The space L is called a link of x in X.
Remark 2.7. Strictly speaking, in the definition of a CS set, one requires the link L to have
finite formal dimension. Though since we will be dealing with MCS spaces, this will not
concern us.

Remark 2.8. One should note that the stratification on L may not be a CS stratification
itself. In particular, Siebenmann intentionally did not assume that the stratification of the
link L in the definition of a CS set is stratified by manifolds (see [35, p. 128]).
Definition 2.9. A metrizable space X is MCS of dimension n if for every point p ∈X, there
exists a neighborhood Up of p, a compact MCS space L of dimension n −1 and a pointed
homeomorphism (Up, p) ≈(cL, o). The unique MCS space of dimension −1 is the empty set.
Recall, the cone over the empty set is a point. A 0-dimensional compact MCS space is a
finite set of points.
The MCS property is closed under natural operations, as the next example illustrates (cf.
[25, p. 6])
Example 2.10. MCS spaces can be constructed via the following means:
(1) (Joins) The join of two compact MCS spaces is MCS.
(2) (Products) If X and Y are MCS then X × Y is MCS.
(3) (Cones) The cone over a compact MCS space is MCS.
(4) (Open Subsets) If X is MCS and U is an open subset of X, then U is MCS.
We mention the following elementary property.
Example 2.11 (MCS is preserved under homeomorphisms). If X is an MCS space and
f : X →Y is a homeomorphism, then Y is an MCS space too.
We now introduce the main tool of this paper.
Definition 2.12. Let X be a locally cone-like finite dimensional metric space. Let x ∈X.
Define I(X, x) to be the supremum of all m ∈N such that there exists a neighborhood Ux of
x, a compact MCS space L, and a pointed homeomorphism θx : (Ux, x) →(Rm × cL, o). We
shall often refer to θx as the associated homeomorphism.
Remark 2.13. If no such m exists, then I(X, x) = −∞.
The quantity I serves as an invariant for MCS spaces, which also detects to some extent
the MCS singularities of locally cone-like spaces. This quantity is analogous to Armstrong’s
notion of an intrinsic dimension at a point [2] in the PL category. Handel used a suitable
generalization of the intrinsic dimension [13] to define the notion of an associated (intrinsic)
filtration for a locally cone-like space. The salient difference between the other invariants
and ours is that in ours (at least apriori), the links are required to be MCS. Further, I turns
out to be the defining element for the MCS strata. In general, for a locally cone-like finite
dimensional metrizable space X, I does not coincide with Handel’s (see Example 3.9). Since
I will be our main tool, we will take the opportunity to study it. As a starting point, let us
look at the following example.
Example 2.14. Let X = RP 2. Consider cX, the open cone over X. Let o be the vertex
of cX. Then, I(cX, o) = 0. However for z ̸= o, I(cX, z) ≥1. Hence I is not continuous in
general. By continuous we mean that if xi →x then I(X, xi) →I(X, x).
The preceding example is useful to keep in mind when going over the proof of the main
Theorem.

such that li →l for some l ∈L. To demonstrate closedness, we must show that l ∈L(n).
By definition of L(n), for each i, there exists ki ≤n such that li ∈L[ki]. Hence there exists
ri ∈Rm and ti > 0 so that I(Rm × cL, (ri, li, ti)) = m + ki + 1. By (1), k = max(ki) is well
defined. Note, I(Rm × cL, (ri, li, ti)) = I(Rm × cL, (0, li, r0)) ≤m + k + 1, where r0 > 0.
Hence h(0, li, r0) →h(0, l, r0). Thus, h(0, l, r0) ∈X(m+k+1). This follows from Claim 1, which
asserts that each X(i) is closed in X. Now note, I(X, h(0, l, r0)) = I(Rm × cL, (0, l, r0)) > m.
To summarize, m < I(Rm × cL, (0, l, r0) ≤m + k + 1, where k ≤n. Thus, l ∈L[j], for some
j ≤n. (3) is now routine.
This shows that X is a CS set.
Claim 4. The intrinsic stratification coincides with the MCS stratification.
Since X is MCS, for any x ∈X, consider the intrinsic dimension at x, I(X, x). We recall
I(X, x) is the supremum of all m such that there exists a neighborhood U of x, a compactum
L and an open embedding h: Rm × cL →X onto a neighborhood of x, for which h(o) = x.
Note that in this case, L is not assumed to be MCS. Thus, I(X, x) ≤I(X, x). We shall
establish equality. Before doing so, we highlight that by what we have established above, X
is a CS set with the MCS stratification. Now we shall verify I(X, x) ≤I(X, x). To that end,
let m = I(X, x) and i = I(X, x) (m is for MCS, and i is for intrinsic). So, x lives in the
m-th MCS stratum. Let θ: (Ux, x) →(Ri × cL, o) be a pointed homeomorphism, where L is
a compactum (we are not assuming L is MCS). Note that although L is not assumed to be
MCS, Ux is and therefore Ri × cL is MCS. This observation combined with Corollary 2.19
shows that θ−1(Ri × o) is contained in the m-th MCS stratum. Since we have shown that X
is a CS set with the MCS stratification, we obtain i ≤m. Hence i = m and this concludes
the proof.
□
Remark 3.3. The strata L[k] are always resolvable homology manifolds, provided the di-
mension of L[k] is at least 4. To show that L[k] are resolvable homology manifolds, first
observe that L[k] is a manifold factor (a space X is a manifold factor if X × Rk is a manifold

Before proceeding, the following lemma is in order, for it is instructive. Namely, it illustrates
the types of pathologies that can occur. Before stating the lemma, we mention that a space
X is said to be stably MCS if X × Rk is MCS for some k ≥1.
Lemma 3.4.
(1) A compactum can be stably MCS but not MCS.
(2) There exists a compact resolvable homology manifold L that does not admit a CS
stratification but Ri × cL is MCS for each i (hence L cannot be an MCS space).
Proof. For both cases, the construction of the compactum is the same. Indeed, let α be the
(non-cellular) Fox-Artin arc in S3 (see [24, Corollary 2.10]). In particular, S3−α is not simply
connected. Then S3/α × R ≈S3 × R. The space S3/α is the desired example. Indeed, the
suspension of S3/α is S4 (see [6, p. 91] or [7, Corollary 2.6.4]). Thus c(S3/α) is homeomorphic
to R4. Hence, for each i, Ri × c(S3/α) ≈Ri+4 is MCS, however, S3/α cannot admit a CS
structure by the results of Henderson [17].
□
Remark 3.5. For another reference regarding spaces appearing in the proof above and their
properties, we refer the reader to [7]. Though note, in that reference, what they call “Fox-
Artin arc” is an arc α in S3 such that S3−α is simply connected, and so it is not the arc we are
using above. In particular, in their example, α is cellular and hence by Bing-Shrinking, their
resulting quotient space is homeomorphic to S3. For a further reference on Bing-Shrinking,
we refer the reader to [4].
Example 3.6. Note that spaces as in Lemma 3.4, (2), appear naturally as Gromov–Hausdorff
limits. For example, since we have a cell-like map S3 →S3/α, the proof of [24, Corollary 2.10]
shows that there exists a sequence of metrics di on S3 admitting a uniform contractibility
function, and a metric d on S3/α, such that (S3, di) →GH (S3/α, d).
Note that Lemma 3.4 shows that although Ri × cL is MCS, L may not be. However, the
proof of the next lemma ensures that in our case, L can always be replaced by an MCS space
L′. More precisely, we have the following Lemma.
Lemma 3.7 (Link Replacement lemma). Let X be an MCS space, and let x be a point in
X. Assume i is the maximal dimension for which there exists a neighborhood U of x, and a
compactum K and a pointed homeomorphism θ: (Ux, x) →(Ri × cK, o).
Then there exist a compact MCS space L and a pointed homeomorphism (Ux, x) →(Ri ×
cL, o).
Remark 3.8. The space K is not assumed to be an MCS space.
Proof. As the proof of the main theorem demonstrates, since X is an MCS space, I(X, x) = i.
Hence there exists a neighborhood Nx of x and a compact MCS space L and a pointed
homeomorphism ψ: (Nx, x) →(Ri × cL, o).
Now by uniqueness of cone neighborhoods
[18] (see also Mazur’s elegant generalization [21]), there exists a pointed homeomorphism
(Ux, x) →(Nx, x). Thus the result follows by composing.
□

is locally cone-like and is not the underlying space of a CS set. Then there exists an x ∈X
so that I(X, x) ̸= I(X, x). Indeed, otherwise X would be an MCS space.
