ABSTRACT
We present our search for little red dots (LRDs) in the ’J1030 field’, a region of the sky around the z ∼6.3 quasar SDSS J1030+0524, observed
by the JWST EIGER program. Over 154 point-like sources selected in a JWST-based photometric catalog, we find five broad line emitters (with
FWHM ≳1000 km s−1) that are red (F200W −F356W > 0) and are undetected in the X-rays. We use these sources to derive the bolometric
luminosity function (LF) of LRDs at z = 2.4 and z = 4.5. At z = 2.4, the space density of LRDs is only a factor of ∼2 lower than that of all
pre-JWST active galactic nuclei (AGNs) with bolometric luminosity Lbol ≳3 × 1044 erg s−1. At z = 4.5, our estimate is consistent with those
derived for LRDs based on larger areas of the sky. A similar behaviour is observed in the black hole mass function. More importantly, we study
the number density of LRDs from cosmic dawn to cosmic noon. We find that there is no significant evolution in the abundance of LRDs with
Lbol ≳3 × 1044 erg s−1 at z > 2. We speculate that the drop at z < 4 seen by other studies is due to their sampling of only the bright-end of the
LRDs LF. At cosmic noon, the abundance of LRDs is n = 3.4+5.6
−2.4 × 10−5 Mpc−3, which is a factor of ∼350 larger than recent model predictions
and is comparable with that of X-ray selected AGNs with similar bolometric luminosity. Our result may imply that, if LRDs are the early, rapid
stages of supermassive black hole growth, as suggested by some models, then the formation of black hole seeds can be efficient down to epochs as
recent as cosmic noon. Alternatively, LRDs may simply be a high-accretion phase in already mature black holes.
Key words. quasars: supermassive black holes — quasars: emission lines — galaxies: high-redshift — galaxies: active
1. Introduction
One of the most intriguing results of the James Webb Space
Telescope (JWST) has been the discovery of a previously un-
known population of extragalactic objects called "little red dots"
(LRDs; Matthee et al. 2024). These sources are mostly unre-
solved in JWST/NIRCam images, have red near-infrared (NIR)
colors, and show a ’v-shaped’ UV-optical spectral energy distri-
bution (SED), with the inflection around the Balmer break wave-
length (3645 Å). When looking at their spectra, most LRDs (up
to ∼60% according to Greene et al. 2024, or even more, see
Hviding et al. 2025) show broad emission lines with full-width
at half-maximum (FWHM) of 1000 −2000 km s−1. This fact
suggests that LRDs can be powered by active galactic nuclei
(AGNs) with black hole masses MBH ∼107 −108 M⊙.
However, some of their properties are at odds with those
of classical AGNs. For example, a significant fraction (up to
10 −20% in JWST-discovered broad line AGNs; BLAGNs Lin
et al. 2024) shows prominent blueshifted/redshifted absorptions
in Balmer lines (e.g., Matthee et al. 2024; Maiolino et al. 2024;
Lin et al. 2024; Kocevski et al. 2025), which are extremely rare
in low-redshift AGNs (∼0.1% incidence; Inayoshi & Maiolino
2025). LRDs seem to have lower hot dust emission, typical of
the obscuring torus, than normal AGNs, although most of them
do show near-IR and mid-IR excess emission, both individually
and in stacks (Delvecchio et al. 2025; Brazzini et al. 2026; Ji
⋆e-mail: federica.loiacono1@inaf.it
et al. 2026; Lin et al. 2026; Pérez-González et al. 2026). Com-
pared to local supermassive black holes, the black holes in LRDs
tend to be overmassive with respect to their host galaxies (e.g.,
Harikane et al. 2023; Übler et al. 2023; Maiolino et al. 2024;
Juodžbalis et al. 2024b; Jones et al. 2025; Juodžbalis et al. 2025).
More intriguingly, LRDs do not produce either significant X-
ray (Ananna et al. 2024; Mazzolari et al. 2025; Yue et al. 2024;
Maiolino et al. 2025) or radio emissions (Mazzolari et al. 2024).
To explain the observed, and in some way exotic, proper-
ties of LRDs, Inayoshi & Maiolino (2025) propose a scenario
in which the Balmer absorption and the strong Balmer break are
both produced by the absorption from extremely dense gas along
the line of sight (with hydrogen density nH ≳109 cm−3). Absorp-
tion from dense, dust-poor clouds in the broad line region (BLR)
might also explain the X-ray weakness in LRDs, provided that
the covering factor is significantly larger than in classical AGNs
(Maiolino et al. 2025). Another scenario proposed to interpret
the X-ray weakness invokes super-Eddington accretion onto the
black hole (Madau & Haardt 2024; Pacucci & Narayan 2024;
King 2025; Madau 2025). Based on detailed analysis of individ-
ual LRDs rest-frame optical spectra, some studies interpret their
observational features as a result of a "black hole star" (Begel-
man et al. 2008), i.e, a supermassive black hole surrounded by
a turbulent, highly dense, and dust-free atmosphere (de Graaff
et al. 2025; Naidu et al. 2025).
Some of the scenarios described above put into question the
presence of highly massive black holes at the center of LRDs.

For example, some studies suggest that the exponential profiles
observed in the Broad lines of a fraction of LRDs (less than 50%
according to Scholtz et al. 2026) may not be the effect of virial
gas motions around the black hole but could be instead produced
by resonant Balmer or electron scattering, implying that black
hole masses estimated through standard calibrations may be bi-
ased high, up to a factor of ∼100× (Naidu et al. 2025; Rusakov
et al. 2025). However, other works illustrate that exponential pro-
files can simply arise from virial motions in a stratified BLR
(Madau et al. 2026; Scholtz et al. 2026), and that black hole
masses seem consistent with those derived from the single epoch
virial estimators (Juodžbalis et al. 2026). In addition, rather than
being explained by absorption from dense gas around the black
hole, Labbe et al. (2024) suggested that the strong Balmer break
may be produced by evolved stellar populations. Other scenar-
ios finally explain the spectral properties of LRDs as the sum of
UV radiation from very young stars and optical emission from
a short-lived supermassive star (with a mass M > 104 M⊙). In
this framework, LRDs would be globular clusters in formation,
rather than accreting supermassive black holes, and the broad
line profiles would be produced by winds from the supermas-
sive stars at their center (Chisholm et al. 2026). These examples
show how, three years after their discovery, the nature of LRDs
remains hazy and necessitates further investigation.
Besides, LRDs are only a part of a vaster population of X-
ray silent, broad-line AGNs discovered by JWST. According to
some studies, only a small fraction (10−30%) of these sources is
represented by LRDs (Hainline et al. 2025; Brazzini et al. 2026;
Taylor et al. 2025; Geris et al. 2026). The remaining sources lack
the ’v-shaped’ SED, have blue optical continuum (optical slope
βopt < 0), and they are referred to as little blue dots (LBDs). Ac-
cording to recent models, LRDs may represent the dust-reddened
version, viewed at high-inclination angles of LBDs (Madau &
Maiolino 2026). However, the fraction of LRDs found by other
studies can be as high as 60 −80% (Hviding et al. 2025; Zhuang
et al. 2025), suggesting that selection effects may play a major
role in determining the relative abundance of sub-populations of
JWST-discovered BLAGNs.
Regarding their demographics, several studies attempted to
estimate the abundance of LRDs and compare it with that of
other AGN populations. At z ∼5 −7, LRDs are 1 dex more
abundant than X-ray and UV selected AGNs with similar UV
absolute magnitude MUV (Kocevski et al. 2025). A similar re-
sult was found by Kokorev et al. (2024), who reported an abun-
dance of LRDs a factor of ∼100× larger than that of UV-selected
quasars at 6.5 < z < 8.5 and with −22 < MUV < −17. Concern-
ing the number density of LRDs as a function of time, Kocevski
et al. (2025) find that their redshift distribution sharply declines
at z < 4.5. The drop in the number density of LRDs at those red-
shifts was confirmed by Ma et al. (2025) using ground-based
photometric data. On the other hand, Loiacono et al. (2025),
based on a small sample of spectroscopically confirmed LRDs,
find that their abundance at cosmic noon is only a factor of ∼2−3
lower than that of UV-selected quasars with comparable bolo-
metric luminosities, meaning that this new population is still rel-
evant at z ∼2−3. Similarly, Euclid Collaboration: Bisigello et al.
(2025) find no drop in the abundance of LRDs between z ∼4 and
z ∼1.5, and a decrease only at lower redshifts. This reconciles
with recent studies that find a very low number density of LRDs
in the local universe n ∼(1.6−5)×10−9 Mpc−3 (Park et al. 2026;
Lin et al. 2026). The contradictory results reported at z < 4 mean
that the demographics of LRDs needs to be further addressed at
cosmic noon, which is a crucial epoch in the history of galaxies
and their active nuclei (Madau & Dickinson 2014).
In this work we derive the bolometric luminosity function
of LRDs at z ∼2.4 and z ∼4.5. Our sample consists of five
compact, red in NIRCam colors, and X-ray silent BLAGNs in
the ’J1030 field’, a region of the sky around the z ∼6.3 quasar
SDSS J1030+0524 (hereafter, J1030). This area is covered by
several multi-wavelength data, including deep radio (D’Amato
et al. 2022) and X-ray (Nanni et al. 2020) observations. At opti-
cal and near-infrared wavelengths, it was imaged by LBT/LBC,
CFHT/WIRCam, and Spitzer, with both IRAC (Annunziatella
et al. 2018) and MIPS, while in the millimeter domain, the field
was covered by AzTEC (Zeballos et al. 2018). The central 1′×1′
region was observed with HST/ACS, HST/WCF3, VLT/MUSE,
and ALMA (Stiavelli et al. 2005; D’Amato et al. 2020; Mignoli
et al. 2020). The field is also part of the MUSYC survey (Ga-
wiser et al. 2006). Finally, the J1030 field is one of the targeted
regions by the EIGER (Emission-line galaxies and Intergalac-
tic Gas in the Epoch of Reionization; Kashino et al. 2023) pro-
gram, which observed it with JWST/NIRCam, both in imaging
and slitless spectroscopy. We discovered the sources analyzed in
this work in this JWST dataset, and we use them to derive the
number density of LRDs at cosmic noon.
This work is organized as follows. In Sect. 2 we detail the
data reduction and in Sect. 3 present the sample selection. We
analyze the spectra in Sect. 4 and present the main results in
Sect. 5. Finally, we discuss our findings in Sect. 6 and summarize
this work in Sect. 7.
In this paper we adopt a ΛCDM cosmology with ΩΛ = 0.7,
ΩM = 0.3 and an Hubble constant H0 = 70 km s−1 Mpc−1. The
reported errors correspond to the statistical uncertainty of 68%
(1σ), if not specified differently.
2. Data reduction
2.1. The J1030 field seen by EIGER
We used the archival data of the 1243 GTO program EIGER (PI:
S. J. Lilly). Briefly, this program targets the fields of six 5.9 <
z < 7 quasars (J0100+2802, J0148+0600, J1030+0524, J159-
02, J1120+0641, and J1148+5251) with NIRCam imaging (in
the F115W, F200W, and F356W filters) and wide-field slitless
spectroscopy (WFSS; F356W filter and R grism); see Kashino
et al. (2023) and Eilers et al. (2024) for further details. In our
analysis, we focused on the J1030 field. The images and spectra
were acquired in two periods, between May 30 and June 2, 2023,
and on May 25, 2024. The field was observed in five telescope
visits (visit-1 to visit-5), covering four sky regions that include
the quasar, and which are combined into a mosaic.
The NIRCam images form a mosaic of ∼6.8′ × 3.6′ around
the quasar. The exposure time varies across the field and reaches
in the central part the value of ∼17.5 ks, ∼25.8 ks, and ∼7.3 ks for
the F115W, F200W, and F356W filters, respectively (Figure 1).
The WFSS data of the J1030 field cover an area ∼17 arcmin2
at 3.1 µm and ∼25 arcmin2 at 3.95 µm (see Kashino et al. 2023).
The observed spectral range is 3.1 −4.0 µm, and the resolving
power R = λ/∆λ is ∼1500 at the central wavelength. The total
obseerving time for the WFSS data is ∼36.9 ks, which also cor-
responds to the maximum exposure reached in the central region
of the field.

the rows of the contaminant that did not overlap with the target

clean, 1D spectrum of the main target. Once the decontaminated

spectro-photometric calibration. Thanks to the accuracy of the
decontamination process and the choice of the extraction aper-
ture size that fit the point-like nature of the targets, the required
photometric correction was always below 15%.

The final photometric catalog comprises 11,838 sources. Be-
cause the J1030 field was not homogeneously covered by the
NIRCam observations, we estimated the 3σ limiting magnitude
per pixel for each available band using the noise maps gener-
ated during the reduction process. For the forced photometry
performed in the F200W and F115W images at the positions
of objects detected in the F356W band, the measured aperture
magnitudes were compared with the corresponding detection
limits. Whenever a source magnitude was fainter than the cor-
responding 3σ limit, the latter was adopted as an upper-limit
value. Following this procedure, 87 objects in F200W (< 1%)
and 506 objects in F115W (4%) were classified as flux upper
limits among the sources detected in F356W. Due to dithering,
the mosaic exhibits non-uniform exposure coverage, leading to
spatial variations in the photometric limiting magnitude. Never-
theless, across more than 80% of the image area, the data reach
a 5σ-limiting magnitude of F356W = 28.5. Finally, to perform a
robust morphological classification of the objects in the catalog,
we adopted a two-step process aimed at maximizing the quality
of the JWST imaging. The first technique relies on the ratio of
an object’s total flux to the flux measured within a fixed aperture
of D=0.18′′. This method efficiently selects point-like sources
down to a flux level at which the distribution of faint, compact
galaxies overlaps with that of stellar sources. We performed this
analysis in both the detection band (F356W) and the ancillary
F200W band, the latter providing the dual advantage of being
deeper for blue objects and offering a higher spatial resolution.
We did not extend this analysis to the F115W image because
the PSF is significantly undersampled in this filter. Lastly, the
list of candidate point-like sources selected through this method
was analyzed using the IRAF task psfmeasure, which estimates
the spatial extent of the targets by calculating the enclosed flux
with increasing aperture radius. For the final classification, we
consistently adopted the band in which each target exhibited the
highest signal-to-noise ratio (S/N). This two-step morphological
analysis yielded a robust list of 154 objects classified as point-
like down to a magnitude of F356W∼28.
3.2. Color-color and color-magnitude diagrams
We plot the F200W−F356W color vs the F115W−F200W color
of the 154 point-like sources in the field (Fig. 3, left panel). We
fill in green the symbols corresponding to sources with at least
one emission line in the WFSS spectrum extracted at the source
position. We also show the color (F200W −F356W) - mag-
nitude (F356W) diagram, which highlights the F356W magni-
tudes of the objects (Fig. 3, right panel). In Fig. 3 (left), the point-
like sources are distributed across two distinct regions, charac-
terized by F200W −F356W colors roughly below and above
0, respectively. The former population corresponds to "blue"
sources, which likely represent stars (forming the “star locus”).
To interpret the nature of the second group ("red" sources; 21
in total), we show the color-color track of some LRDs from
the literature ("Rosetta stone", Juodžbalis et al. 2024a; stack-
ing of LRDs, Delvecchio et al. 2025; "BiRD", Loiacono et al.
2025; "The Egg" or "The Lord", Lin et al. 2026; Ji et al. 2026)
computed from their SED as a function of redshift. We can see
that the tracks overlap with most of the point-like sources with
F200W −F356W > 0, suggesting that at least part of them are
likely LRDs at various redshifts.
Two of the authors (FL, MM) independently analyzed the
WFSS spectra of all the point-like sources shown in Fig. 3, us-
ing both the original and decontaminated 1D and 2D spectra. For
the red sources with more than one line in the grism spectrum,
we unambiguously attributed a redshift (see Tab. 1). Based on
He i and Paγ, we found four emitters at 2.3 < z < 2.5, while,
based on the [O iii] λλ4959, 5007 and Hβ lines, we identified
three emitters at 5.7 < z < 6.4. For the sources with one line
only, when possible, we used the tracks and the inflection in
the spectral energy distribution (see Fig. A.1 in the appendix)
to infer the most reasonable nature of the line. In this way, we
found three possible Hα emitters at z ∼4.4 −4.5. The nature of
the remaining red, point-like sources remains ambiguous. The
lack of prominent emission lines, combined with the faintness
of the sources themselves and their non-detection in non-JWST
photometric bands (both from HST and ground-based facilities),
also precludes a search for the potential Balmer break. Conse-
quently, this prevents both the determination of their redshifts
and the characterization of their intrinsic nature (e.g., whether or
not they harbor an AGN).
In addition, we found four sources (circles marked with a
cross in Fig. 3) that are known X-ray AGNs in the Marchesi
et al. (2021) catalog of Chandra J1030 identifications4. These
objects have −0.3 < F200W −F356W < 0.5, and are therefore
located between the stellar locus and the cloud of red sources.
Also, they are at the bright end of the F356W magnitude distri-
bution (Fig. 3, right). The three X-ray AGNs with bluer colors
(XID 10, 21, 30 in Marchesi et al. 2021) are standard, broad line
AGNs with spectroscopic redshifts in the range of z = 1.2 −6.3
(XID 21 is the z = 6.3 J1030+0524 quasar itself). The fourth X-
ray source (i.e., the one with the reddest F200W −F356W color)
is a narrow line AGN at zspec = 2.4 (XID 31).
We also identified an i-band dropout (5.4 < z < 6.6) just
outside the LRD tracks. This source, ID 5247, was discovered
by Kim et al. (2009) through HST/ACS imaging (see Object A8
in their Table), and the narrow line in the WFSS data is likely
[O iii] λ5007 at z = 5.61, with the [O iii] λ4959 and Hβ lines
possibly too faint to be detected.
Finally, we found five single-line emitters in the star locus.
For these sources, we carefully examined the SED and the spec-
tral trace to exclude the possibility that the line is associated
with a contaminant object. Due to the detection of a single line
only and the inconclusive photometric information, the nature of
these sources remains unclear.

4. Analysis
4.1. Spectral line fitting of X-ray silent BLAGNs at z ∼2.4
We fit the He i emission using both a narrow and a broad Gaus-
sian component. We assessed the significance of the He i broad
component using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), de-
fined as
BIC = χ2 + k ln N
(1)
where χ2 is the Chi-square, k is the number of fitted parameters,
and N is the number of fitted data points. We estimated ∆BIC =
BICN −BICN+B, which is the difference between the BIC that
we obtained using a narrow component only (BICN) and both a
narrow and a broad component (BICN+B). According to Liddle
(2007), a ∆BIC = 5 means a ’strong’ improvement of the model.
The evidence in favor of the model with the lowest BIC becomes
’decisive’ if ∆BIC = 10. We obtain ∆BIC = 11 and ∆BIC =
5620 for ID 1017 and ID 3646, respectively, which ensures the
reliability of the He i broad component in both sources.
We used the Python package emcee (Foreman-Mackey et al.
2013) based on a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method
to perform the line fit. In addition to the He i line, we fit the con-
tinuum emission with a linear function. We used uniform priors
for all the fitted parameters, with the FWHM of the narrow and
broad components being lower than 1000 and 10000 km s−1, re-
spectively. We then fit the Paγ emission (and the underlying con-
tinuum) separately using a narrow and a broad Gaussian compo-
nent. In this case, we fixed the FWHM of the two components
to the He i values because of the low S/N of the Paγ line.

5.1. Bolometric luminosity, X-ray and radio properties
We derive the bolometric luminosity from the broad Hα emis-
sion of the sources. For ID 1017 and ID 3646, i.e., the sources
where this line is not covered by our data, we inferred the Hα
luminosity from the measured broad Paγ line assuming Case B
recombination. We note that Case B may not apply in the high-
density regime of BLR (Ili´c et al. 2012). However, we verified
that this scenario is valid for the Rosetta Stone, a z ∼2.26 LRD
where both Paγ and Hα lines were observed (Juodžbalis et al.
2024a; see Loiacono et al. 2025 for more details). Assuming a
flux ratio ∼31.7 between Hα and Paγ (Osterbrock 1989), we es-
timate an intrinsic Hα luminosity of L(Hα) = 2+3
−1.7 ×1042 erg s−1
and L(Hα) = 5+3

spectively. On the other hand, the Hα luminosity was directly
derived from the observed broad line for the z ∼4.5 sources.
We find L(Hα) = 2.1+0.3

Fig.5). This can be due either to strong obscuration or an intrinsi-
cally weak X-ray emission (Maiolino et al. 2025; Comastri et al.
2026).
We also stacked the X-ray images at the source’s location
separately for the z ∼2.4 and z ∼4.5 samples. In this way we
obtain more stringent upper limits to their median X-ray lumi-
nosity, which is L2−10 keV,rest < 3.0 × 1042 erg s−1 at z ∼2.4
and L2−10 keV,rest < 1.5 × 1043 erg s−1 at z ∼4.5. Assuming
the median bolometric luminosity of the sources at z ∼2.5 and
z ∼4.5, we find lower limits to the X-ray bolometric correction
of Lbol/L2−10 keV,rest > 234 and Lbol/L2−10 keV,rest > 25, respec-
tively. While the limit for the z = 4.5 stack does not provide
stringent information about the obscuration level, if the three
objects at z = 2.4 were standard AGN, it would take a column
density of NH ≈1024 cm−2, i.e. Compton thick or close to it, to
bring the bolometric correction of their stacked emission at least
a factor of 10 above the average value expected by Duras et al.
(2020) for normal quasars.
None of the BLAGNs is detected in the radio image of the
J1030 field (D’Amato et al. 2022), which reaches an rms of
2.5 µJy at 1.4 GHz. Such non-detections imply upper limits in
radio luminosity (1.4 GHz rest frame) of ∼3.4 × 1039 erg s−1 at
z = 2.4 and ∼1.3 × 1040 erg s−1 at z = 4.5. Based on the typ-
ical emission level of radio-quiet AGN, these limits are not as
stringent as the X-ray ones. Deeper radio observations are thus
needed to verify if these sources are in fact radio weak or even
silent, as shown by Mazzolari et al. (2024) for JWST-discovered
BLAGNs.

6. Discussion
6.1. Little red dots and little blue dots
As shown in Fig.6, our selection criteria - i) point-like source;
ii) presence of broad emission lines; iii) absence of X-ray de-
tection (see Sect. 3.2) - have returned three LRDs, one LBD,
and one object (ID 1017) at the border between the two classes.
We note that ID 8511 has βUV > −0.37 (i.e., the upper bound-
ary of Kocevski et al. (2025) to define LRDs). This slope cut
minimizes the contamination from dusty systems (Brazzini et al.
2026; Geris et al. 2026). As our sample is not affected by these
contaminants, we relaxed this criterion and included ID 8511 in
our sample.
It is now interesting to compare the prevalence of LRDs in
our sample with what is found in other JWST BLAGN sam-
ples, as the fractions of LRDs reported in different works scat-
ter significantly. For example, taking the reported numbers at
Article number, page 10 of 19


## Page 11


Loiacono F. et al.: No evolution in the number density of little red dots from cosmic dawn to cosmic noon
face value, some works indicate that LRDs may constitute only
a minority, ∼30%, of the whole new population of BLAGNs
discovered by JWST (Taylor et al. 2025; Brazzini et al. 2026),
whereas other works find they are instead the vast majority (up
to ∼60 −80% of the population; Hviding et al. 2025; Zhuang
et al. 2025, and, albeit with large uncertainties, even this work,
where they are ∼60 −80%). What are the reasons for the re-
ported differences, and, in turn, what is the true incidence of
LRDs among JWST BLAGNs? As already noted in different
works (Hainline et al. 2025; Taylor et al. 2025; Zhuang et al.
2025), the fraction of LRDs increases towards larger Hα lumi-
nosities. On average, JWST BLAGN samples selected through
NIRSpec spectroscopy reach lower luminosities (e.g. ∼4 × 1041
erg s−1 at z = 4; Taylor et al. 2025) than those selected through
NIRCam/WFSS (∼2×1042 erg s−1; Matthee et al. 2024; Zhuang
et al. 2025, this work), and is therefore reasonable to expect
lower fractions in NIRSpec-selected samples. In addition, we
verified that a stronger effect is related to the velocity threshold
adopted to define a broad line. For example, when increasing the
line FWHM threshold from 700 to 1000 and 1500 km s−1, the
LRD fraction in the sample of Taylor et al. (2025) progressively
increases from 34 ± 6% to 39 ± 7% and 50 ± 8%. Also, it is
worth noting that Taylor et al. (2025) only apply a compactness
criterion to define LRDs, but not LBDs. As shown by Hviding
et al. (2025), whereas the vast majority (∼90%) of LRDs are
compact, only ∼60% of LBDs are compact. When summing
compact and non-compact LBDs to compact LRDs, the fraction
of LRDs with FWHM > 1500 km s−1 in Hviding et al. (2025)
turns out to be 59 ± 6%, very similar to the 50 ± 8% in the Tay-
lor et al. (2025) sample. In the WFSS sample by Matthee et al.
(2024), the LRD fraction goes from 55 ± 11% to 59 ± 12% when
cutting at FWHM > 1000 and 1500 km s−1, respectively, which
is consistent with the results above.
To summarize, the LRD fraction increases towards higher
line luminosities and width. The dependence on line width may
be related to geometric effects consistent with recent modeling
(Madau 2025), suggesting that JWST BLAGNs are powered by
Super Eddington flows and feature a highly asymmetric geom-
etry. In these models, a geometrically thick accretion flow pro-
ducing strongly anisotropic radiation is surrounded by a copla-
nar, equatorially concentrated BLR and, on larger scales, by a
flattened dusty cloud distribution, both sharing a similar, mod-
est (∼10 −20%) covering factor. In this scenario, LRDs would
be the edge-on analogs of face-on LBDs, and hence would be
preferentially selected at large line widths. Instead, it would be
difficult to reconcile the prevalence of LRDs at large Hα lumi-
nosities if the covering factor is low. In fact, in (edge-on) LRDs
the Hα luminosity would be more suppressed by dust than in
(face-on) LBDs, and only a large dust covering factor would be
able to reproduce the observed LRDs dominance, e.g. ∼70% for
L(Hα) > 1043 erg s−1 in the samples of Taylor et al. (2025) and
Matthee et al. (2024). This argument could suggest that LRDs
are intrinsically more abundant than LBDs at a given bolometric
(line) luminosity, and that the smaller fraction of LRDs observed
at low luminosities is primarily the result of selection effects, as
extinction in edge-on systems can suppress faint emission lines
below the detection threshold.

than our field. We thus expect to sample only the more abundant
low-luminosity end of their LF because of the smaller area of
our field.
6.3. Active black hole mass function
We study the space density of LRDs as a function of the black
hole mass. We note that the active black hole mass functions
(BHMFs) are, in general, difficult to derive due to selection bi-
ases (Matthee et al. 2024; Taylor et al. 2025). Therefore, we con-
sider our measurement as tentative.
At z ∼2.4, the black hole masses of ID 1017, ID 3646,
and BiRD span between ∼1.6 × 107 −2.4 × 108 M⊙and thus
we split the sources over two mass bins of 1 dex centered at
log(MBH/M⊙) = (7.5, 8.5), populated with two and one sources,
respectively. We divide the space density determined in Sect. 5.3
by a factor of 2 to take into account the 1 dex bin size. On
the other hand, at z ∼4.5, we use a 0.5 dex bin centered at
log(MBH/M⊙) = 7. The BHMFs are reported in Table 6 and
shown in Fig. 8.
We note that the lowest mass bin at z ∼2.4 and the mass
bin at z ∼4.5 possibly suffer from incompleteness because of
the FWHM threshold that we adopt to define a broad line emit-
ter (see Sect. 3.3). Indeed, we are possibly missing sources with
MBH ∼107.5 M⊙and MBH ∼107 M⊙, depending on the redshift
bin, but with FWHM < 1000 km s−1.
Compared to quasars, at z ∼2.4 the abundance of LRDs
(plum pentagons in Fig. 8, left) is consistent with the number
density of BLQSOs inferred by Shen & Kelly (2012).
At z ∼4.5 LRDs with MBH ∼107 M⊙(red pentagon, Fig. 8,
right) are significantly more abundant than quasars, according to
the predicted values by Shen & Kelly (2012). We note however
that Shen & Kelly (2012) sample black holes with MBH ≳6×108
M⊙, which makes the extrapolated abundance at MBH ∼107 M⊙
very uncertain. We also compare our estimate with the best-fit
BHMF of He et al. (2024) (corrected for incompleteness), which
includes measurements from BLAGNs with black hole masses
down to MBH ∼3×107 M⊙at slightly lower redshift (3.50 < z <
4.25), based on SDSS and the Subaru Hyper-Supreme Cam data.
Compared to He et al. (2024), our estimate of the BHMF samples
lower black hole masses and appears a factor of ∼1.8× larger
than a possible extrapolation of their best-fit at lower masses (yet
consistent within the 1σ uncertainties).
We compare our estimates with the BHMFs of LRDs from
other studies at z ∼2.5 (Loiacono et al. 2025) and z ∼4.5
(Matthee et al. 2024; Taylor et al. 2025). We do not apply any
scaling factor to their mass bins, as these works use the calibra-
tion of Reines & Volonteri (2015) to derive black hole masses.
We note that, as for our measurement at z ∼4.5, neither Matthee
et al. (2024) nor Taylor et al. (2025) apply the extinction correc-
tion to the Hα luminosity.
At z ∼2.5, our BHMF in the largest mass bin is fully con-
sistent with the value of Loiacono et al. (2025), which samples
black hole masses that are larger on average. However, we note
that the two estimates are not independent as BiRD is included
in the BHMF of Loiacono et al. (2025) as well.
At z ∼4.5, our estimate is nicely consistent with the low-
est mass bin of Matthee et al. (2024), which also suffers from
incompleteness (empty diamond in Fig. 8, right). On the other
hand, the values of Taylor et al. (2025) at 6 × 106 −2 × 107 M⊙
are a factor of ∼3 −7 larger than our point. This is likely be-
cause Taylor et al. (2025) do not apply any FWHM cut to the Hα
emitters in their sample, which mitigates incompleteness effects.
Besides, as discussed in Sect. 6.1, at low FWHM, the selection
of Taylor et al. (2025) is more sensitive to LBDs than LRDs,
compared to our sample. When considering LRDs only, as for
our z ∼4.5 bin, their points are only a factor of ∼2 larger than
our estimate.

clouds, which prevent them from directly collapsing into black

formation of black hole seeds can still be efficient at epochs as
recent as cosmic noon. Alternatively, our result may simply hint
that LRDs, rather than being the very first accretion episodes of
growing seeds, are more likely a high-accretion phase in already
mature black holes. Additional studies will be necessary to dis-
criminate between the two scenarios.
6.5. LRDs and other AGN populations at cosmic noon
We compare the abundance of LRDs at cosmic noon with that of
AGNs based on multi-wavelength (bolometric) selection (light
blue area in Fig. 9; Shen et al. 2020) or X-ray selection (gray
shaded area; Peca et al. 2023). We obtained the curves by inte-
grating the AGN LFs at various redshifts for the same integra-
tion limit of LRDs (Lbol ≳3 × 1044 erg s−1) and considering the
1σ errors. For the X-ray LF by Peca et al. (2023), we converted
from 2 −10 keV to bolometric luminosities using the relation of
Duras et al. (2020). The difference between the AGN space den-
sity derived using the bolometric LF of Shen et al. (2020) and
the X-ray LF of Peca et al. (2023) is about a factor of 2−3 going
from z = 0 to z = 4. This is broadly consistent with the expecta-
tions of synthesis models of the cosmic X-ray background (Gilli
et al. 2007; Ananna et al. 2019) suggesting that the abundance
of heavily obscured, Compton-thick AGNs (NH > 1024 cm−2)
that are largely missed by X-ray surveys, is comparable to that
of less obscured AGNs whose abundance is instead recorded in
the X-ray luminosity functions.
At z ∼2.4 the abundance of LRDs with Lbol
≳3 ×
1044 erg s−1 is a factor ∼3.6 lower than that of all AGNs. On
the other hand, the number density of LRDs is within the errors
of X-ray selected AGNs with comparable bolometric luminosity.
It is possible that LRDs are part of the population of
Compton-thick AGNs, whose contribution to the total AGN cen-
sus is still poorly understood. For example, if the large equivalent
widths that are measured in the broad Hα lines of LRDs are due
to a large covering factor of the clouds in the BLR (significantly
larger than in standard AGN), then the same clouds would be suf-
ficiently dense to produce Compton-thick absorption (Maiolino
et al. 2025).
However, absorption by Compton-thick gas perhaps is not
the only reason for the lack of X-ray emission in LRDs, which
might be instead intrinsically X-ray weak (Tortosa et al. 2026).
Several LRDs have line widths < 2000 km s−1 and can thus be
classified as narrow-line Seyfert 1 galaxies, which are known to
accrete at high rates and to have very steep X-ray spectra (Iwa-
sawa et al. 2024). Such steep spectra, would indeed imply low
rates of X-ray photons at rest-frame energies above a few keV,
i.e. the ones sampled in high-z LRDs, explaining in turn why
these objects may have escaped X-ray detection (Maiolino et al.
2025).
Recently, Sneppen et al. (2026) proposed a scenario in which
both obscuration by thick gas (NH ∼1025 cm−2) and high ac-
cretion rates are needed simultaneously to explain the observed
X-ray weakness of LRDs. From the picture portrayed above, it
is thus difficult to quantify to what extent LRDs contribute to
the population of Compton-thick AGNs, with the latter possibly
having a substantial role in the accretion history of supermassive
black holes (Peca et al. 2023).
6.6. The fate of LRDs in the local universe
According to some studies, the number density of LRDs in the
local universe becomes as low as n ∼(1.6−5)×10−9 Mpc−3 (Lin
et al. 2026; Park et al. 2026). This implies a drop by a factor of
∼104 compared to our estimate at cosmic noon.
A fair comparison between our measurement and those in
the local universe is not obvious due to the different sample se-
lections. Lin et al. (2026) selected their sample from the SDSS
DR17 spectroscopic database based on their ’v-shape’ (i.e., they
include LRDs only). They removed sources with strong [N ii]
and Mg ii emission, as possible type-I AGN contaminants, and
required an [O iii] equivalent width above 10Å to reduce the con-
tamination from galaxies. The objects are compact on a scale of
1.8′′, and their final sample includes three objects at z ∼1 se-
lected as the most representative LRDs. Only one out of the three
sources is undetected in X-ray, while one has a 2σ detection, and
the third one was not observed in the X-ray. Park et al. (2026)
considered a sample of eight LRDs at 0.2 < z < 0.5 that were
selected from the DESI DR1 spectroscopic catalog. The sample
includes both LRDs and LBDs and only two out of eight sources
have an Hα FWHM > 1000 km s−1 (i.e., our width cut). The
sources are compact on a scale of 1′′ and their X-ray properties
are not considered in the selection.
More importantly, the samples of Lin et al. (2026) and Park
et al. (2026) are incomplete. As noted by Lin et al. (2026), the
SDSS LRDs were disadvantaged in being selected for spectro-
scopic follow-ups due to their compact morphology. In addition,
the adopted cut in [O iii] equivalent width may prevent some
LRDs from being selected and that are similar to high-redshift
LRDs reported in the literature (e.g., Ji et al. 2025; Naidu et al.
2025; de Graaff et al. 2025). According to Park et al. (2026), it
is very difficult to quantify the incompleteness of their sample,
as their LRDs span a variety of selections and show up to the
faintest magnitudes in the DESI catalog.
As discussed in Lin et al. (2026) and Park et al. (2026), the
measurements in the local universe should be regarded as lower
limits to the number density of LRDs. Future studies, based on
blind spectroscopic samples of LRDs at z ∼0, will be crucial to
improve these estimates.
If the number density of LRDs is confirmed to decline
sharply from cosmic noon to the present epoch, this may still
be interpreted in the model framework proposed by Inayoshi
(2025). Indeed, assuming that LRDs represent the first one or
two super-Eddington accretion episodes of newly-born black
hole seeds, high accretion rates may become unsustainable once
the black hole has grown considerably in mass and the gas sup-
ply from its host has decreased, possibly because of both the pro-
gressive consumption of the gas reservoir and AGN feedback.
Therefore, objects that were shining as LRDs at cosmic noon
would be observed as normal AGNs in the local universe.

Appendix A: SEDs of the X-ray silent BLAGNs
We report the SEDs and the NIRCam composite images
(F115W + F200W + F356W) of the compact, X-ray silent
BLAGNs studied in this work. The SED and cutouts of BiRD
are shown in Loiacono et al. (2025). The photometry includes
ground-based measurements, JWST, and, when available, HST
data-points (F775W, F850LP, and F160W). The 3σ upper lim-
its are also shown. For the ground-based data points of ID 3646,
we used aperture-corrected magnitudes to derive the correspond-
ing flux densities. This approach was necessary because con-
tamination from a stellar spike makes the total magnitudes in
the Y, J, and K bands reported in the J1030 catalog (Mazzolari
et al. 2026) unreliable. Since the source is point-like, aperture-
corrected magnitudes are perfectly suited to derive the total
fluxes of the target.
