WILD AUTOMORPHISMS AND COMPOUND ISOTRIVIALITY
JASON BELL AND RAHIM MOOSA
Abstract. Inspired by the model theory of difference fields in characteristic
zero, a class of automorphisms of an algebraic variety, here called compound
fundamental isotrivial, is introduced. These are algebraic dynamical systems
that are built up via a finite sequence of equivariant fibrations from (possi-
bly nonautonomous) algebraic dynamics which trivialise after base extension
over themselves. Every wild automorphism of an abelian variety is compound
fundamental isotrivial. Conversely, it is shown that the only irreducible projec-
tive varieties admitting a wild automorphism that is compound fundamental
isotrivial are the abelian varieties. That is, the wild automorphism conjecture
of Reichstein, Rogalski, and Zhang is here proven for compound fundamental
isotrivial dynamics. Along the way, a counterexample to the naive generalisa-
tion of the conjecture to the nonautonomous setting of σ-varieties is provided.

1. Introduction
A central problem in algebraic dynamics is to understand automorphisms of pro-
jective varieties with no proper nonempty invariant closed subvarieties. Such maps
are called wild automorphisms, a notion introduced by Reichstein, Rogalski, and
Zhang [13]. Working over an algebraically closed field k of characteristic zero, the
wild automorphism conjecture asserts that if a projective variety admits a wild
automorphism then it is an abelian variety. This conjecture was motivated by con-
nections with the structure theory of projectively simple graded algebras, which are
a natural ring-theoretic analogue of just infinite groups. Reichstein, Rogalski, and
Zhang [13] established the conjecture in dimension at most two and conjectured

should admit a description via equivariant fibrations, which ultimately reduce to

In this paper we will settle the conjecture for a special class of automorphisms
that we call compound fundamental isotrivial. While this will be a strong condition
to impose on automorphisms generally, it is one that is implied by the conjecture
itself. We will first discuss independent model-theoretic motivation for considering
this class, before giving a more precise definition later in this Introduction, and
then a careful treatment in Sections 5 and 6 below.
The wild automorphism conjecture is a group existence statement; it concludes
that the projective variety X admits the structure of an algebraic group. In model
theory there are several important contexts in which the existence of a definable
group is deduced from geometric information. One of these is the binding group
theorem, which in the setting of algebraic dynamics was established recently in [8].
It applies to dynamics that are “quantifier-free internal to the fixed field”, in the
terminology of model theory, which corresponds to the geometric property of be-
ing isotrivial; these are dynamics which become trivial after difference field base
extension – we will give a more precise definition shortly. For isotrivial dynamics,
the truth of the wild automorphism conjecture follows from combining the bind-
ing group theorem with cases of the conjecture already appearing in [13]. But the
class we are interested in here is much wider, it is what a model theorist might
call “quantifier-free analysable in the fixed field”, a notion that is so far only im-
plicit in the literature, but for which the condition of compound isotriviality that
we introduce here is a geometric articulation. These are dynamics that are built
up via finitely many equivariant fibrations from isotrivial dynamics – again, more
details to come. The relevance of this class comes from the manifestation of the Zil-
ber dichotomy in algebraic dynamics, a deep model-theoretic result of Chatzidakis
and Hrushovski [4] which, along with its refinements, has played a role in most
applications of model theory to algebraic dynamics, including work of Chatzidakis-
Hrushovski [4, 5, 6], Hrushovski [7], Medvedev-Scanlon [11], and more recently
Kamensky-Moosa [8, 9]. The Zilber dichotomy plays no explicit role here except
to suggest that the compound isotrivial case is worth considering. Indeed, a conse-
quence of the dichotomy, roughly speaking, is that if an algebraic dynamics is not
compound isotrivial then it will admit a positive dimensional equivariant image
that is sparse in the sense that its cartesian powers do not admit rich invariant
families of subvarieties. See [9, Section 4] for a precise geometric articulation of the
Zilber dichotomy.
What we propose here is a strategy, motivated by model theory and powered
by the binding group theorem, for attacking the wild automorphism conjecture
in the compound isotrivial case.
What we actually accomplish, however, is to
carry out this strategy in a more restricted class of automorphisms, those that are
compound fundamental isotrivial. This restriction is at least partially justified by

Let us now try to be a bit more precise so that we can state our main theorem.
First of all, our point of view will involve working in the more robust category
of σ-varieties where the dynamics may be twisted by an automorphism of the
field of definition. That is, one fixes a field K, and an automorphism σ : K →
K, and then considers pairs (X, ϕ) where X is a variety defined over K and ϕ :
X →Xσ is an isomorphism to the transform of X with respect to the action
of σ on K. An invariant subvariety is then a subvariety Y such that ϕ(Y ) ⊆Y σ,
and wildness in this setting makes sense too. While we are primarily interested
in the autonomous case, when X and ϕ are defined over k ⊆Fix(K, σ), so that ϕ
is an automorphism of X, we are forced to consider nonautonomous σ-varieties
because they arise naturally as the generic fibres of equivariant maps. Indeed, if
f : (X, ϕ) →(Y, ψ) is an equivariant map of autonomous σ-varieties over k, then
the generic fibre is a (usually nonautonomous) σ-variety over the difference field
(k(Y ), σψ) where σψ is the automorphism of the function field of Y induced by ψ.
And studying the generic fibres of equivariant maps is central to the approach that
we are here proposing.

2. Preliminaries on σ-varieties
We begin by recalling and fixing terminology around possibly nonautonomous alge-
braic dynamics. Further details can be found in [8, Section 3]. We work throughout
over a given inversive difference field of characteristic zero, namely a field K of char-
acteristic zero equipped with an automorphism σ.
Definition 2.1. By an inversive σ-variety, (X, ϕ) over (K, σ), we will mean an
absolutely irreducible algebraic variety X over K equipped with an isomorphism
ϕ : X →Xσ, where Xσ is the transform of X with respect to the action of σ on K.
An invariant subvariety of (X, ϕ) is a (not necessarily irreducible) subvariety
Y ⊆X over K such that ϕ(Y ) ⊆Y σ. Note that if Y is absolutely irreducible, then
(Y, ϕ|Y ) is an inversive σ-variety in its own right.
An equivariant rational map, f : (X, ϕ) 99K (Y, ψ), between inversive σ-varieties
over (K, σ), is a dominant rational map f : X 99K Y such that ψ ◦f = fσ ◦ϕ as
rational maps from X to Y σ.
Remark 2.2.
(a) In the literature it is often only required that ϕ be birational,
but we restrict our attention to isomorphisms in this paper.
(b) As all our σ-varieties will be inversive, we will often drop this adjective.
(c) These notions really depend on the base difference field (K, σ). For exam-
ple, if Xσ = X then ϕ is an automorphism of X and we can view (X, ϕ)
as a σ-variety over (K, σ) or over (K, id), and this choice effects what one
means by “invariant” subvariety or “equivariant” map.
It is convenient to embed (K, σ) in a sufficiently saturated difference-closed field
extension (U, σ). This means that every (possibly infinite) system of σ-polynomial
equations and inequations over a difference subfield of cardinality strictly smaller
than |U| (called small), with the property that all finite subsystems have solutions
in some difference field extension of (U, σ), has a solution in (U, σ). The existence
of such universal domains is at the very beginning of the model theory of difference
fields, as carried out in [4]. We follow the standard convention that all difference
fields which arise are implicitly assumed to be small difference subfields of (U, σ),
unless explicitly stated otherwise.
A key advantage of working in a universal domain is that, given a σ-variety
(X, ϕ) over (K, σ), the set
(X, ϕ)♯:= {a ∈X(U) : σ(a) = ϕ(a)}
is Zariski dense in X.

Definition 2.6. A σ-variety (X, ϕ) over (K, σ) is isotrivial if there is a difference

3. Nonautonomous wildness
Our goal in this section is to extend the notion of wild automorphism to the twisted
case of σ-varieties, to prove some basic lemmas about wild σ-varieties that we will
need later, and to observe by a counterexample that the analogue of the wild
automorphism conjecture fails in this nonautonomous setting.

Definition 3.1. An inversive σ-variety (X, ϕ) over (K, σ) is wild if it has no proper

5. Fundamental isotriviality
The gap between homogeneity and actually being an abelian variety, for a projective
σ-variety, is bridged if the group action is not only transitive but free. To ensure
freeness of the action, we need a stronger condition than isotriviality.
Fix an algebraically closed inversive difference field (K, σ) of characteristic 0.
Definition 5.1. A σ-variety (X, ϕ) over (K, σ) is fundamental isotrivial if there

components of Y , and hence some iterate of it, say (σ−1◦ϕ)n preserves an irreducible
component, say Y◦. Now, (σ−1 ◦ϕ)n = σ−n ◦ϕ(n). So ϕ(n) maps Y◦to (Y◦)σn. That
is, Y◦is invariant for the σn-variety (Xb, ϕ(n)). By irreducibility, and existential
closedness of (U, σ), there is a Zariski generic point e ∈Y◦(U) over K such that
σn(e) = ϕ(n)(e). Now, let Z := loc(e/k). Since Y ̸= Xb, and b is Zariski generic
in X1 over k, we have that Z ̸= X. On the other hand, ϕ(n)(e) = σn(e) implies
that Z is invariant for (X, ϕ(n)). But wildness of ϕ implies wildness of ϕ(n), by
Lemma 3.4, which precludes the existence of such a Z.
✠
By assumption, (Xb, ϕ) is fundamental isotrivial. Hence wildness implies Xb is
an abelian variety, by Proposition 5.4. Since X is an algebraic fibre space over
an abelian variety, with general fibre an abelian variety, we have, by a result of
Cao and Pˇaun [3], that the Kodaira dimension of X is nonnegative. But wildness
rules out positive Kodaira dimension (this is [13, Corollary 4.2]), and in the case of
Kodaira dimension zero it implies that X is of the form A×Y where A is an abelian
variety and Y has trivial Albanese (this is [10, Theorem 1.2]). It follows that A
is the Albanese of X and hence π : X →X1 must factor through the co-ordinate
projection X →A. That is, Y embeds in every fibre of π, and so in particular in
the abelian variety Xb. This forces dim Y = 0, and hence X = A is abelian.
□
Theorem 6.6 reduces the wild automorphism conjecture to showing that every
wild automorphism of a projective variety is compound fundamental isotrivial.
