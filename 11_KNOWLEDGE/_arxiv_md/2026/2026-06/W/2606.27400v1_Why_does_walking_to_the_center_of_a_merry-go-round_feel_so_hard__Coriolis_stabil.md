Why does walking to the center of a merry-go-round feel so hard?
Coriolis stabilization and the metabolic cost of staying on track
Mario J. Pinheiro∗
Department of Physics, Instituto Superior T´ecnico,
Universidade de Lisboa, 1049-001 Lisboa, Portugal
(Dated: June 29, 2026)
A standard undergraduate problem has a student walk radially inward on a rotating, frictionless
merry-go-round. The textbook analysis—angular momentum is conserved, so the kinetic energy
rises and the student does work—is exactly correct for a point mass. Yet anyone who has tried it
knows the effort is dominated by something the point-mass model never mentions: the muscular
work of not being thrown sideways. We make that effort quantitative with a deliberately minimal
model. Treating the student as an active controller that cancels the Coriolis force at a metabolic
cost P ∝F n
C , we show that the cost scales as r−2n as the axis is approached. The widely used
quadratic cost (n = 2) gives a steep r−4 rise; a linear cost (n = 1) gives r−2. We argue that this
sensitivity of the prediction to the cost model is the most useful thing in the problem: it forces
students to see how a modeling assumption, not just an algebraic step, drives a physical conclusion.
We give an order-of-magnitude estimate (explicitly flagged as such), a one-line entropy-production /
efficiency argument that connects the exercise to non-equilibrium thermodynamics, a feedback (PD-
controller) reformulation that reproduces the same scaling, and a back-of-the-envelope experiment
students can do on a playground with a phone and a heart-rate strap. The material is aimed at an
intermediate-mechanics or biophysics elective and is designed to teach model validity, assumption
sensitivity, and the idea that staying alive and on-course has a thermodynamic price.

II.
EFFORT IS NOT WORK: THE
CONCEPTUAL CORE
The pivot of the whole exercise is the distinction be-
tween mechanical work and metabolic effort. The clean-
est demonstration needs no rotation at all.
Hold a heavy book at arm’s length, stationary. Me-
chanically dr = 0, so W =
R
F · dr = 0: the book gains
no energy and you do no work on it.
Physiologically
you are nonetheless consuming ATP at a steady rate, be-
cause muscle fibers hold tension by cycling actin–myosin
cross-bridges, each cycle hydrolyzing ATP even with no
macroscopic displacement [1]. This isometric cost is real,
measurable as elevated oxygen uptake, and completely
outside Newtonian work accounting.
The merry-go-round is the same lesson in motion.
Even if the student’s center of mass tracks a perfect radial
line, the muscles must continuously supply a tangential
force to cancel the Coriolis force and prevent lateral drift.
That cancelling force is perpendicular to the radial ve-
locity, so it does no work on the center of mass—yet it
must be generated, and generating force costs metabolic
energy. The rest of the paper quantifies how that cost
grows as r →0.
The standard global energy statement
Etotal = K + Eint + Q
(3)
is of course never violated: Eint (chemical, elastic) and
heat Q absorb everything the mechanical balance omits.
The pedagogical point is not that energy goes missing
but that the mechanical work on the center of mass can
be a small part of the true energy budget, and that the
rest is structured and calculable.

METABOLIC COST OF CORIOLIS
STABILIZATION
A.
A family of cost models
To turn “effort” into a number we need a cost function
relating metabolic power Pmet to the stabilizing force the
student must produce. Rather than commit to one form,
we keep a family,
Pmet = αn F n
C ,

(a) Linear, n = 1. The simplest assumption is that
metabolic power is proportional to the force generated,
as in some lumped models of muscle cost [6]. It is the
cheapest to defend and gives the mildest scaling.
(b) Quadratic, n = 2.
A quadratic running cost is
the canonical choice in linear-quadratic optimal control
[3]: it penalizes large forces, yields smooth controls, and
admits closed-form solutions.
It is also formally iden-
tical to Joule heating (P = I2R), a comparison every
physics student already owns. Kuo [5] showed that for
a limb stabilizing against a stochastic disturbance the
time-averaged cost is proportional to the variance of the
required force, i.e. to ⟨F 2⟩, which is the setting closest to
ours. This is the “default” choice in much of the motor-
control literature.
(c) Higher order, n > 2. Muscle-model-derived costs
can rise faster still near the limit of available force [6]; we
mention this only to show the trend, not to advocate it.
The central derivation below is done once, for general
n, precisely so that the dependence of the conclusion on
this choice is visible rather than hidden.

IV.
A THERMODYNAMIC READING:
ENTROPY AND EFFICIENCY
The phrase “thermodynamics of staying on course”
should be cashed out, not merely invoked. Here is the
one-line version suitable for class.
Skeletal muscle converts chemical free energy to me-
chanical output with an efficiency η ∼0.25 [1]; the re-
maining (1−η) ≈0.75 is released as heat. In our problem
the mechanical output associated with the stabilization
channel is essentially zero—the Coriolis-cancelling force
does no work—so to leading order the entire metabolic
power spent on stabilization is dissipated as heat at body
temperature T ≈310 K. The corresponding rate of en-
tropy production is simply

VI.
A BACK-OF-THE-ENVELOPE
EXPERIMENT
A fair objection to everything above is “is this just
theory?” It need not be. A playground roundabout, a
smartphone, and a heart-rate strap suffice for a quali-
tative test that fits in a lab afternoon, and we include
it precisely so the exercise can carry classroom evidence
rather than assertion.
The phone, placed at a known radius, gives ω(t)
from its gyroscope and lateral acceleration from its ac-
celerometer; the heart-rate strap gives a rough proxy for
metabolic power above baseline (heart-rate reserve cor-
relates with ˙VO2 over moderate ranges). Two predictions
are accessible:
1. Scaling of effort. Have a participant move slowly
from the rim toward the center (with a spotter) at
as steady a pace as safety allows, logging heart rate
vs. radius over several runs. The model predicts a
steep, faster-than-linear rise in effort as r falls; even
distinguishing “faster than r−2” from “slower” is a
meaningful qualitative test of the cost model and a
vivid lesson in fitting noisy data.
2. Sway frequency.
From the lateral-acceleration
trace, extract the dominant frequency of corrective
sway as a function of r and test the ωn ∝r−1/2
prediction of Sec. V A.
Neither measurement is publication-grade, and that is
the point:
students confront real, messy data, decide
what the model robustly predicts (escalation; rising sway
frequency) versus what it does not (a precise exponent),

VII.
CLASSROOM USE
The material is designed for the second half of an
intermediate-mechanics course (after rotating frames and
the Coriolis force) or for a biophysics or active-matter
elective. A natural sequence is: (1) assign the standard
point-mass problem and have students compute ∆K; (2)
pose the question “where does the fatigue come from, if
not from this work?”; (3) develop the cost-model family
of Sec. III A and derive the general r−2n scaling, empha-
sizing that the exponent is a consequence of an assump-
tion; (4) do the order-of-magnitude estimate and inter-
pret the divergence as a domain boundary; (5) add the
entropy/efficiency line of Sec. IV; and, time permitting,
(6) run the experiment.
The recurring meta-lesson—and the reason the prob-
lem is worth a week rather than a single problem set—
is assumption sensitivity: students see one cost-function
choice change a prediction from r−2 to r−4, and they
learn to ask of any model “which of my conclusions are
robust, and which are artifacts of a convenient assump-
tion?” That is a skill the standard version of the problem
cannot teach, because it has no contested assumption in
it. The Appendix gives a graded problem set keyed to
the learning objectives of Sec. I.
VIII.
CONCLUSION
The student on the merry-go-round is not a point
mass; she is an active, sensing, dissipating controller [7].
The point-mass solution is correct within its domain and
silent outside it, and that silence is the opportunity. By
layering a transparent metabolic cost model, an explicit
entropy-production line, and a feedback reformulation
onto the standard problem, we obtain a teaching unit
whose central lesson is not a particular exponent but
the contingency of exponents on assumptions. The ro-
bust physics—effort must escalate toward the center, and
holding course costs entropy—survives every reasonable
modeling choice; the dramatic r−4 does not, and saying
so out loud is the most useful thing the problem does.
We hope instructors find that the question “where does
the fatigue come from?” opens more doors than the tidy
original closes.
