PRESERVATION OF PRIMARINESS UNDER ℓ1-, c0-, AND
ℓ∞-SUMS OF BANACH SPACES
ANTONIO ACUAVIVA
Abstract. We prove transfer principles for the uniform primary fac-
torisation property (UPFP) from a Banach space X to the vector-valued
sequence spaces ℓ1(X), c0(X) and ℓ∞(X). The hypotheses are either
finite-cotype assumptions on X or X∗, or natural self-similarity as-
sumptions on X. Consequently, under these conditions, the resulting
vector-valued sequence spaces are primary. As applications, we recover
the primariness of ℓ∞(Lp) for 1 ≤p < ∞without using Bourgain’s lo-
calisation method, and obtain the primariness of c0(L1). We also show
that ℓ1(Γ, L1[0, 1]) has the UPFP for every set Γ, and consequently that
C[0, 1]∗has the UPFP and is primary.

1. Introduction and organisation
A Banach space is called primary if every decomposition of it as a direct
sum has one summand already isomorphic to the whole space. Equivalently,
whenever P : X →X is a projection, at least one of the spaces PX and
(IX −P)X is isomorphic to X. The primariness problem for classical Banach
spaces has played an important role in the isomorphic theory of Banach
spaces, and many of its solutions rely on factorisation methods.
A typical feature of such proofs is that they establish a stronger factori-
sation dichotomy for arbitrary operators. This is captured by the (uniform)
primary factorisation property, abbreviated (U)PFP; see Definition 2.2.
Roughly speaking, the PFP asks that, for every operator T : X →X, the
identity on X factors through either T or IX −T, while the UPFP asks for
uniform control of the corresponding factorisation constants. Thus (U)PFP
is a natural property to explore in the study of primariness, and is often
the more flexible property in permanence arguments. This point of view
was used explicitly in the recent work of the present author and Kania on
primariness of uncountable ℓp-sums [3]; we refer to that article for a more
detailed discussion of primariness and the PFP.
In this paper, we prove transfer principles showing that, under finite-
cotype assumptions on X or X∗, or under natural self-similarity assumptions
on X, the UPFP passes from X to the vector-valued sequence spaces ℓ1(X),
c0(X) and ℓ∞(X). The proofs are surprisingly elementary and recover, by
a unified method, several known primariness results for spaces of the form
ℓ∞(X). Our first main results is the following.
Theorem 1.1. Let X be a Banach space with the UPFP. Suppose that at
least one of the following holds:
(i) The space X is self-similar, in the sense that X ≃ℓp(X) for some
1 ≤p ≤∞, or X ≃c0(X).
(ii) The dual space X∗has finite cotype.
Then ℓ1(X) has the UPFP. In particular, ℓ1(X) is primary.
We also obtain a similar conclusion in the case of c0 and ℓ∞-sums.
Theorem 1.2. Let X be a Banach space with the UPFP. Suppose that at
least one of the following holds:
(i) The space X is self-similar, in the sense that X ≃ℓp(X) for some
1 ≤p < ∞.
(ii) The space X has finite cotype.
Then both c0(X) and ℓ∞(X) have the UPFP. In particular, c0(X) and
ℓ∞(X) are primary.
As an immediate consequence, we recover the primariness of ℓ∞(Lp) for
1 ≤p < ∞by elementary methods. The original proofs of Wark [16] for
1 < p < ∞and Müller [14] for the full range, rely on substantially more
technical methods, in particular Bourgain’s localisation method. We also

1.1. Organisation and proof strategy. In Section 2, we introduce the
notation and basic facts used throughout the paper, including the matrix
representation of operators on vector-valued sequence spaces.
Once this
framework is in place, the proofs of Theorems 1.1 and 1.2 are fairly straight-
forward, we present them in Section 5. The main idea is to reduce, up to
a small perturbation, an operator T : E(X) →E(X) to diagonal form. We
then use the UPFP of X to factor the identity through either the resulting
diagonal operator D or through IE(X) −D; see Proposition 5.5. Finally,
we use the fact that factorisation of the identity is stable under sufficiently
small perturbations; see Lemma 5.6.
The diagonal reduction is obtained in two steps: an upper-triangular re-
duction, proved in Section 3, and a lower-triangular reduction, proved in
Section 4. There are two types of hypotheses. In the finite-cotype case,
the relevant finite-dimensional geometry is sufficiently far from that of the
scalar sequence spaces involved: finite cotype of X∗is used for ℓ1(X), while
finite cotype of X is used for c0(X) and ℓ∞(X). In the self-similar case, the
reductions are obtained by contrasting incompatible sequence-space geome-
tries: for ℓ1-sums, the internal ℓp, 1 < p ≤∞, or c0 structure is contrasted
with the external ℓ1 structure, while for c0- and ℓ∞-sums, the internal ℓp
structure is contrasted with the external ℓ∞norm structure.
In Section 6, we apply a variant of the same diagonal method to un-
countable ℓ1-sums with summand L1[0, 1]. The additional ingredients are a
finite-coordinate localisation lemma for operators into ℓ1(Γ, L1[0, 1]) and a
finite free-set theorem of Hajnal. This gives the UPFP for ℓ1(Γ, L1[0, 1]) for
every set Γ, and hence the UPFP and primariness of C[0, 1]∗.
Finally, Section 7 contains some additional remarks concerning the rela-
tionship between the PFP and UPFP of X and those of ℓp(X), c0(X) and
ℓ∞(X), together with some open questions.

3. Upper triangular reduction
In this section we prove the upper-triangular reductions needed later for
the diagonal reductions. The general mechanism is the same throughout:
first one proves a forward reduction lemma, which allows us, after passing to
suitable infinite subsets, to choose a coordinate whose image has arbitrarily
small projection onto future coordinates; then a standard recursive selection
turns these one-step estimates into an upper-triangular form.
We begin with the case of ℓ1(X). The finite dual-cotype case is recorded
in Proposition 3.1, while the self-similar case is obtained from the forward re-
duction in Lemma 3.2 and the inductive construction in Proposition 3.3. We
then treat the c0(X) and ℓ∞(X) cases: finite cotype is handled in Lemma 3.4
and Proposition 3.6, and self-similarity in Lemma 3.7 and Proposition 3.8.

6. An uncountable ℓ1-sum
Although the preceding transfer principles are stated for countable vector-
valued sequence spaces, the diagonal-reduction arguments are not inher-
ently countable. With the evident modifications, the same methods apply
to spaces indexed by an arbitrary set Γ, such as ℓ1(Γ, X), c0(Γ, X) and
ℓ∞(Γ, X). The only caveat is that for X ≃ℓ1(X), the UPFP of X does not
automatically transfer to the UPFP of ℓ1(Γ, X) when Γ is uncountable.
The uncountable setting allows an additional diagonal-reduction mecha-
nism. In the countable arguments above, both upper- and lower-triangular
reductions are needed in order to reach a diagonal operator. For uncountable
index sets, one can sometimes replace this two-sided selection by a one-sided
finite-interference estimate, followed by a free-set argument which removes
the finitely many exceptional coordinates on a large subset of the index set.
We now apply this mechanism to the summand L1[0, 1]. Although L1[0, 1]
is not covered by the finite-dual-cotype hypothesis in Theorem 1.1, it has
enough additional measure-theoretic structure to recover the required diag-

while Kalton’s representation theorem gives the finite-interference estimate
for operators from L1[0, 1] into ℓ1(Γ, L1[0, 1]). Combining this estimate with
Hajnal’s free set theorem yields the diagonal reduction for every uncount-
able Γ. Together with the countable case, this proves Theorem 1.6. The
application to C[0, 1]∗then follows from its classical representation as an
ℓ1-sum of copies of L1[0, 1].

7. Final discussion and open questions
The spaces ℓ1, c0 and ℓ∞, especially the latter, have historically been
among the more delicate settings for factorisation and primariness argu-
ments, often requiring arguments different from those used for ℓp, 1 < p <
∞, or even for sums with respect to arbitrary symmetric bases.
Some-
what surprisingly, the methods presented here can only be directly applied
in these cases. The reason is that the geometry of ℓ1 and of the supremum
norm gives enough control over coordinate restrictions to obtain both upper-
and lower-triangular reductions.
For ℓp-sums with 1 < p < ∞, the situation is less clear. One can often
obtain one-sided reductions, either upper or lower triangular, under natural
hypotheses, but obtaining both simultaneously seems to require substan-
tially stronger assumptions. This obstruction is not absolute: in [2], a two-
sided reduction was obtained for ℓp-sums of certain C(K) spaces. There,
the upper-triangular reduction used the c0-structure of the spaces, while the
lower-triangular reduction relied on the special structure of C(K), in par-
ticular on reproducibility properties of K which force an ℓ1-type behaviour.
It is natural to ask whether there are general hypotheses under which
analogous two-sided reductions can be proved for ℓp(X), 1 < p < ∞.
Question 7.1. Let 1 < p < ∞. Are there natural and sufficiently broad
conditions on a Banach space X under which the UPFP of X passes to
ℓp(X)?
Remark 7.2. In the uncountable setting, the need for a full two-sided reduc-
tion can sometimes be avoided. Indeed, once a one-sided finite-interference
estimate is available, Hajnal’s free set theorem can remove the finitely many
exceptional coordinates on a large subset of the index set. Thus the cotype
and self-similarity mechanisms used in the countable reductions above are

expected to give corresponding UPFP results for uncountable sums ℓp(Γ, X),
1 < p < ∞, under the analogous hypotheses.
The PFP and UPFP originate in the study of primariness, so it is natu-
ral to ask whether these properties, or primariness itself, are preserved by
vector-valued sequence-space constructions.
Question 7.3. Is there a Banach space X with the PFP, respectively the
UPFP, such that ℓp(X), for some 1 ≤p ≤∞, or c0(X) fails the PFP,
respectively the UPFP?
Question 7.4. Is there a primary Banach space X such that ℓp(X), for
some 1 ≤p ≤∞, or c0(X) is not primary?
A counterexample to either question would likely have to be rather patho-
logical, while a positive answer seems far beyond the reach of the present
methods.
