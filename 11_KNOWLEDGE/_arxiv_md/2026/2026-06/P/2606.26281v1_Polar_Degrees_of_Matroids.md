Polar Degrees of Matroids
Clara Briand

June 26, 2026
We show that the polar degrees of the coordinate-wise inverse of a linear
subspace L ⊆Pn are given by the coefficients of a substitution of the re-
duced characteristic polynomial of the associated matroid M(L). Our proof
connects the geometry of conormal varieties of reciprocal linear spaces to
the combinatorial conormal fan of M(L). As a corollary, we settle two open
conjectures regarding matroid discriminants.

The closure of the image of L under this map is denoted L−1 and is called the reciprocal
linear space associated to L. This a projective variety whose geometric properties are
closely related to the realisable matroid M represented by L. Reciprocal linear spaces
have been widely studied both from the viewpoint of maximum likelihood estimation
in algebraic statistics (see e.g. [HS14]) and from the viewpoint of matroid theory (see
e.g. [OT92; HK12; PS06]). In this article we study L−1 through the lens of projective
duality. The central object in our work is the conormal variety Con(L−1) ⊆Pn × (Pn)∗
whose general points are pairs (p, H) where p ∈L−1 is a smooth point and H ∈(Pn)∗is
a hyperplanes tangent to L−1 at p. The projectively dual variety (L−1)∨of L−1 is the
image of Con(L−1) under the projection to the second factor (Pn)∗.
Our initial motivation for studying these varieties comes from a collection of recent
conjectures by Matsubara-Heo and Telen in their work on principal matroid determi-
nants [MT26]. In their paper they introduce an analogue of the theory of principal
A-determinants for toric varieties by Gelfand, Kapranov and Zelevinsky [GKZ94] to the
setting of reciprocal linear spaces, thereby defining the principal matroid determinant
EL. Their idea is that the stratification of toric varieties into orbits is replaced by the
stratification of reciprocal linear spaces into strata corresponding to flats of the under-
lying matroid M := M(L). The main theorem [MT26, Theorem 1.2] shows how the
principal matroid determinant decomposes into a union of dual varieties of reciprocal
linear spaces of minors of M. The following three conjectures about these factors and
their multiplicities in EL were left open.

defective, i.e. dim((L−1)∨) < n −1, if and only if the matroid M is not connected.

(L−1)∨is a hypersurface of degree 2dβ(M), where β(M) is the beta invariant of M.

this we derive a first combinatorial formula which sums over all decreasing flags of
flats of M (Section 3.2).
• Finally we extract a recursion for the complicated combinatorial formula obtained
in the last step to relate the polar degrees to a substitution of the reduced char-
acteristic polynomial χM (Section 3.3).

2 Preliminaries and dual degree
Throughout this article we work over an algebraically closed field K of characteristic 0.
2.1 Matroids
In this section we collect the required combinatorial preliminaries. Namely, we recall
how to associate a matroid to a linear space, as well as some important properties of
matroids.
This construction is what allows us to study the geometric properties of
reciprocal linear spaces from a combinatorial point of view. For a thorough introduction
to matroids, we refer to [Oxl06].
We start by recalling the definition of a matroid.
Definition 2.1. A matroid is a pair M = (E, B) where E is a finite set, called the
ground set of M, and B is a nonempty family of subsets of E, called the bases of M,
such that the basis exchange axiom holds:
∀B1, B2 ∈B : ∀e1 ∈B1 \ B2 : ∃e2 ∈B2 \ B1 : (B1 \ e1) ∪{e2} ∈B.
We say that a subset S ⊆E is independent if it is contained in a basis, otherwise we
call S dependent. A circuit of M is an independent subset S which is inclusion-minimal
among all independent subsets. We further denote the rank of any subset S ⊆E by
rkM(S) := maxB∈B |S ∩B|. For S = E we also simply write rk(M) := rkM(E), which
is the cardinality of any basis of M. By clM(S) := { x ∈E | rkM(S ∪{e}) = rkM(S) }
we denote the closure of S in M. A subset F ⊆E is called a flat of M if F = clM(F).
The groundset E is always a flat and clM(∅) is unique flat of rank zero. Its elements
are called loops. The beauty of matroid theory lies in the fact that most of the objects
defined in this paragraph (bases, independent sets, circuits, rank function, closure, . . . )
can be used to give “cryptomorphic definitions” of matroids.
Below we also work with flags of flats, these are collections F of flats which are
pairwise comparable under inclusion:

We now switch gears and recall the fundamentals of projective duality, the most im-
portant of which are the definition of conormal variety and of polar degrees. For more
