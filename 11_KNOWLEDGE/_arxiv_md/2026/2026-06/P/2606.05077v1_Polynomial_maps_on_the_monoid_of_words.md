POLYNOMIAL MAPS ON THE MONOID OF WORDS
JOSE MAR´IA ALMIRA
Abstract. We study the space P(S, C) of polynomial maps f : S →C, where S = A∗is the
monoid of words based on a finite alphabet A under concatenation. To motivate the study of
this space, we first briefly visit the theory of polynomial and semipolynomial maps defined on
an arbitrary monoid, with range a commutative group. Our results are motivated by previous
work of Shulman [6], [7].
1. Polynomial maps with range a commutative group
Throughout this paper, we assume that (S, ·, e) is a monoid and (G, +, 0) is a commutative
group, and consider the operators:
Ls : GS →GS;
Ls(f)(t) = f(st) −f(t),
Rs : GS →GS;
Rs(f)(t) = f(ts) −f(t),
which are called the left and right difference operators, respectively. In this noncommutative
setting, polynomial maps are defined as follows: The constant functions f : S →G are poly-
nomial maps. If the constant is equal to 0, then its functional degree is −∞. If the constant
is different from 0, then its functional degree is 0. Inductively, for each n ≥1, the function
f : S →G is a polynomial map of functional degree ≤n if and only if for each s ∈S, the
functions Rs(f), Ls(f) are both polynomial maps of functional degree ≤n −1. The minimal n
with this property is called the functional degree of f. If we only use the operator Ls, we speak
of left-polynomial maps and left-functional degree; if we only use the operator Rs, we speak of
right-polynomial maps and right-functional degree.
We denote by Pn(S, G) the set of all polynomial maps f ∈GS with functional degree ≤n.
For left-polynomials maps of left-functional degree ≤n, we use the notation PL

3.1. Connections to other fields. The monoid of words S = A∗under concatenation, along
with its associated subword counting functions fw(x) = [x]w, plays a central role across several
domains of algebra, discrete mathematics, and theoretical computer science.
In formal language theory, a celebrated theorem of Simon [8] characterizes piecewise testable
languages precisely by means of subword configurations. Specifically, two words x and y are
indistinguishable by any piecewise testable language of height k if and only if they possess
exactly the same set of subwords of length up to k. The subword counting maps fw provide a
refined, quantitative framework for evaluating these combinatorial properties.
From an algebraic perspective, the pointwise product of subword counting maps directly
mirrors the algebraic definition of the shuffle product ⊔and the infiltration product (or quasi-
shuffle product) on free algebras [9]. When evaluating a product of two subword counters, such
as fu · fv, the resulting expansion yields a linear combination of functions whose highest-degree
terms correspond exactly to the formal permutations generated by interleaving the sequences
u and v while preserving their respective linear orderings. The emerging lower-degree terms
account precisely for the combinatorial overlaps or “clashes” of identical characters.
