Background: Neutrinoless double β-decay (0νββ), if observed, would provide unequivocal evidence of physics
beyond the Standard Model. 48Ca is an interesting candidate system to study because it has the largest Qββ value
among all ββ transitions and is also unstable against single β-decay. The observation of both β and ββ-decay in
the same isotope would provide a unique opportunity to benchmark theoretical calculations of β and ββ-decay
matrix elements and could provide insight on the quenching of the axial vector coupling constant, gA.
Purpose: Perform a precise measurement of the mass of 48Sc to enable a new determination of the 48Ca – 48Sc
single β-decay Q value and perform an updated determination of the 48Ca single β-decay lifetime to compare
with experimental lower limits.
Method: The TITAN Penning trap mass spectrometer at the TRIUMF facility was utilized for a high-precision
measurement of the 48Ca single β-decay Q value. This was achieved through cyclotron frequency ratio measure-
ments of 48Ca+/48Sc+ and 48Sc+/48Ti+ using the Time-of-Flight Ion Cyclotron Resonance (TOF-ICR) technique.
Results: The 48Ca β-decay Q value was determined to be Qβ(48Ca) = 279.14(50) keV, a factor of 10 more
precise than the previous value given in the 2020 Atomic Mass Evaluation [Wang, et al., Chin. Phys. C 45,
030003 (2021)]. This Q value was used to determine the 48Ca single β-decay partial half-life, with the result
T β
1/2(48Ca) = 5.09(5) × 1020(g−2
A ) y.

compared to calculations using the previous Q value. Our result is marginally closer to the experimental lower
limit T β

I.
INTRODUCTION
Searches for neutrinoless double β-decay (0νββ) pro-
vide the exciting possibility of discovering physics beyond
the Standard Model. If observed, 0νββ would indicate
that the neutrino is a Majorana particle and that lepton
number is not conserved. It would also enable a determi-
nation of the effective Majorana neutrino mass. Among
the 35 naturally occurring primordial isotopes that are
ββ-decay candidates (where single β-decay is energeti-
cally or otherwise forbidden), 11 are considered the most
prominent for 0νββ searches because they have Q val-
ues, Qββ > 2 MeV, which results in a higher expected
decay rate. 48Ca stands out among these as the isotope

angular momentum difference between initial and final
nuclear states: ∆J= 4, 5 or 6 (see the decay scheme in
Fig. 1).
In 48Ca, as with a number of other ββ-decay can-
didates [1–3], the rare but Standard Model allowed

model calculations that determine the 48Ca β-decay half-

the first direct measurement of the 48Ca single β-decay

TITAN Penning trap at TRIUMF. We also discuss up-
dated calculations of the 48Ca single β-decay rate and the
implications for observing this highly forbidden decay.
II.
EXPERIMENTAL DESCRIPTION

UMF (see Fig. 2) was used to perform high-precision
cyclotron frequency ratio measurements between singly-
charged 48Ca, 48Sc, and 48Ti ions.
The ions of interest were provided by the TRIUMF
Isotope Separation and ACceleration (ISAC) facility [18]
that produces rare isotopes via the isotope separation
on-line (ISOL) technique. In this experiment, the 44 h
half-life isotope 48Sc was produced by a 70 µA, 520 MeV
proton beam impinged on a low-power tantalum target,

An initial purification step was performed using a mass

Next, ions were delivered

The cooler-buncher is a lin-

ions are cooled via collisions with the gas, reducing the
transverse emittance and energy spread of the beam.
The accumulated ions were extracted as low-emittance
bunches, accelerated to 2.2 keV, and delivered to either
the Multi-Reflection Time-Of-Flight Mass Spectrometer
(MR-TOF) [21, 22] or the Measurement Penning Trap
(MPET) [23].
In the MR-TOF, ions are confined between two electro-
static mirrors and undergo multiple reflections, extend-
ing their flight path within a compact geometry. Since
time-of-flight scales as t ∝

sive reflections. The TITAN MR-TOF has been used for
mass measurements and mass separation, achieving re-

respectively. In this work the MR-TOF was used to iden-

In the Penning trap, ions are confined by the superpo-
sition of a strong homogeneous magnetic field, ⃗B = Bˆz,
that provides radial confinement, and a quadrupolar elec-
trostatic potential, that confines the ions in the axial di-
rection with a simple-harmonic motion at the axial fre-
quency νz. In Penning trap mass spectrometry, the goal
is to determine the mass of an ion via its free-space cy-
clotron frequency [25, 26],
νc = qB
2πm.
(1)
However, the electric field has the effect of modifying an
ion’s cyclotron motion, so that inside the trap it revolves
at the reduced-cyclotron frequency, ν+. The electric field
also introduces a second radial motion, the magnetron
motion, at frequency ν−, that results from the ⃗E × ⃗B
drift experienced by an ion in the trap. The free-space
cyclotron frequency of Eqn. (1) can be recovered by com-
binations of the trap frequencies, ν+, ν−, and νz.
Of
particular relevance for this work is the relationship
νc = ν+ + ν−,
(2)
that holds true for an ideal Penning trap, and can be
considered exact to the level of precision of the measure-
ments presented here [27, 28]. At TITAN, the MPET
operates in a B = 3.7 T field, provided by a supercon-
ducting solenoidal magnet, resulting in a cyclotron fre-
quency for singly-charged A = 48 ions of approximately
1.2 MHz. The TITAN MPET was recently upgraded to
the CryoMET design for operation at cryogenic temper-
atures, and the work presented here represents the first
online measurement after this upgrade [29–31].
In the MPET, the cyclotron frequency was determined
using the time-of-flight ion cyclotron resonance (TOF-
ICR) technique [32, 33]. Ions were first prepared with
initial magnetron motion by injecting them off-center
from the trap axis using a Lorentz steerer [34]. A “dipole
cleaning” radio frequency (rf) drive was then applied at
the trap cyclotron frequency corresponding to the other
ions that were delivered in the beam from ISAC to re-
move them from the center of the trap and avoid poten-
tial frequency shifts due to Coulomb interactions. Ions
were then subjected to a quadrupolar rf excitation at
νrf ≈ν+ + ν−, which couples the ions’ radial motions,
resulting in an increase in radial energy when magnetron
motion is converted into cyclotron motion.
Ions were
subsequently ejected from the trap toward a microchan-
nel plate detector (MCP) and their time-of-flight (TOF)
was recorded.
The TOF is minimized for ions with a
larger radial energy, which occurs when νrf = νc. Data is
acquired by scanning νrf over a frequency range close to
νc for subsequent bunches of ions and creating a TOF vs.
νrf spectrum. A fit of the theoretical line shape [33] to
the data can then be performed to extract νc, as shown

is then obtained.

In this work, pairs of ions used in a given cyclotron
frequency ratio measurement have the same mass num-

Relativistic shifts, for example, which depend on the ion
mass and motional amplitude, have a completely negli-
gible effect on the ratio due to the small relative mass
difference between the pair of ions being evaluated.
Systematic shifts due to imperfections in the trapping
fields, described by higher-order terms in the expansion
of the magnetic field and electrostatic potentials (see e.g.

ejection from the cooler-buncher by 0.5 µs. This would
affect the axial amplitude of the ions in the trap and, as
a result, probe the effect of field imperfections. No sta-

derived ratio was observed under these different condi-
tions, indicating that these effects are beyond the statis-
tical sensitivity of the measurement.

the cyclotron frequency on ion count was observed within
uncertainties.

IV.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
A.
Q values
The cyclotron frequency ratios listed in Table I can
be converted into Q values, corresponding to the en-
ergy equivalent of the mass difference between parent and
daughter atoms as follows,
Q = (MP −MD)c2 = (MP −me)(1 −¯R),
(3)
where MP is the mass of the parent atom, MD is the mass
of the daughter atom, me is the mass of the electron [37],
and c is the speed of light. Atomic masses were obtained
from AME2020 [14]. The uncertainty in MP contributes
insignificantly to the uncertainty in Q because 1 −¯R <
10−4 for all ratios measured. In Eqn. (3) we have ignored
electron binding energies for the singly-charged ions since
these are ∼6 – 7 eV for the isotopes we studied and are
insignificant compared to the statistical uncertainties.
The resulting Q values are listed in Table II. Our mea-
surement of the 48Ca double β-decay Q value is in agree-
ment with the AME2020 value, which was obtained from
Penning trap measurements [38–40], differing by 1.14(87)
keV or 1.3σ. Our results for the 48Sc and 48Ca single
β-decay Q values are in agreement with the AME2020
value, which is dominated by the 5 keV/c2 uncertainty
in the mass of 48Sc.
Our 48Sc Q value is a factor of
7 more precise than AME value. Our two 48Ca single
β-decay Q value results—one from the alternating data
and one from the simultaneous data—listed as Alt. and
Sim. in Table II are, respectively, factors of 6 and 2.5
more precise than the AME value. In addition to our
direct 48Ca single β-decay Q value measurement, we can
also combine our result for the 48Sc β-decay Q value with
the precisely known 48Ca 2β-decay Q value to obtain an
additional independent determination of Qβ(48Ca). This

V.
CONCLUSION
Using Penning trap mass spectrometry, we have per-
formed the first direct measurement of the 48Ca single
β-decay Q value. Our result confirms the previous AME
value, which relied on the mass of 48Sc determined from
nuclear reaction data, but is a factor of 10 more precise.
Using this new result we have performed updated shell
model calculations to determine the 48Ca single-β-decay
half-life.
The uncertainty in the half-life has been re-
duced by a factor of about 30 compared to the result
obtained using the previous Q value, providing an ac-
curate and precise result that can be used for planning
future experiments that could search for 48Ca single β-
decay. Our result confirms that the single β-decay half-
life is only a factor of about ten longer than that of the
already observed double β-decay half-life, and about five
times longer than the current experimental lower limit
on the 48Ca single β-decay half-life.
Hence, detection
of 48Ca single β-decay could be within reach of future
experimental efforts.
Finally, we have also performed the first direct mea-
surement of the 48Sc single β-decay Q value, providing
a direct experimental determination of the mass of 48Sc
with 10-fold improvement in precision compared to the
previous AME value.
