Abstract
Financial resilience concerns the rate at which a position recovers, or further dete-
riorates, in response to adverse conditions. As a first step, [LFRZ25] introduced the
resilience rate, defined as the expected instantaneous rate of (favorable) change of a
price or risk-assessment process. Since this quantity captures only the conditional mean
of future increments, it cannot distinguish between positions having the same expected
recovery but different conditional risk profiles. We obtain a richer characterization by
evaluating such increments through a genuine, possibly nonlinear, dynamic risk mea-
sure. More precisely, for an Itô process π and a normalized, cash-additive dynamic risk
measure ρ, we define the resilience evaluation by

Introduction
As risks resolve over time and information is revealed progressively, risk measurement is
inherently dynamic. The arrival of new information, and the occurrence of adverse events
in particular, continuously reshape the assessment of a financial position. Dynamic risk
measures formalize this by assigning to a position X a process (ρt(X))t∈[0,T ] of conditional
evaluations along a filtration F = (Ft)t∈[0,T ], the position being acceptable at time t when-
ever ρt(X) ≤0 (see, e.g., [Del06; Bio09; FR04; FS16]). Among these, the dynamic risk
measures induced by backward stochastic differential equations (BSDEs) provide a natural
and tractable class of nonlinear, time-consistent conditional valuations, whose structural
properties are inherited from those of the BSDE driver (see [Pen97; BE09; EPQ97; Ros06]).
While this literature analyzes the dynamic evolution of the level of risk, a complemen-
tary and largely unexplored question concerns its rate of change once a position becomes
unacceptable. This is the perspective of financial resilience: the pace at which a position
recovers, or further deteriorates, after breaching the acceptability set. As a first step in

2.2
Dynamic risk measures
We set the basic definition of dynamic risk measure, adopting the actuarial sign convention
and interpreting it as risk evaluation of an asset through time; see [BE09; FR04].
Definition 2 (Dynamic risk measure). Let XT be a vector subspace of L1(FT ) containing
constants, and define Xt := XT ∩L1(Ft) for t ∈[0, T). A dynamic risk measure on XT
is a family ρ = (ρt)t∈[0,T ] such that ρt : XT →Xt for any t ∈[0, T], and further satisfies
the monotonicity property: For any X, Y ∈XT , if X ≤Y , then ρt(X) ≤ρt(Y ) for any
t ∈[0, T].
A static risk measure is defined as the evaluation at time 0 of a dynamic risk measure;
see [Del12; FS16; FR02; BLR18].
Let us now fix XT as above. We present a non-exhaustive list of well-known axioms for
a dynamic risk measure ρ on XT .
1. Normalization: ρt(0) = 0 for any t ∈[0, T].
2. Cash-additivity: For X ∈XT , and t ∈[0, T], if h ∈Xt, then ρt(X + h) = ρt(X) + h.
3. Positive homogeneity: ρt(αX) = αρt(X) for X ∈XT , α > 0, and t ∈[0, T].
4. Convexity: ρt

t ∈[0, T].
5. Coherence: cash-additivity + convexity + positive homogeneity.
For further details on the financial interpretation of the properties listed above, interested
readers are referred to [FS16; FR02; BE09; Ros06]. We here briefly recall that positive
homogeneity implies normalization, hence a coherent risk measure is always normalized.

2. Cash-additivity: ρ is cash-additive if g is independent of y, namely, P ⊗ℓ1-a.e., we have
g( · , y, z) = g( · , 0, z) for all (y, z) ∈R × Rm. In this case, we identify g with a driver
defined on Ω× [0, T] × Rm.
3. Positive homogeneity: If g is P ⊗ℓ1-a.e. positively homogeneous in (y, z), then ρ is
positively homogeneous.
4. Convexity: If g is P ⊗ℓ1-a.e. convex in (y, z), then ρ is convex.
5. Coherence: If g is independent of y and is P⊗ℓ1-a.e. convex and positively homogeneous
in z, then ρ is coherent.
The converse implications, namely the derivation of structural properties of the driver
from the corresponding properties of the induced risk measure, hold under additional as-
sumptions ensuring the local representation of the driver. We refer to [Jia08] in the Lipschitz
case and [MY10; ZL18] in the quadratic case.

5
Conclusion
This paper introduced a local notion of financial resilience based on the infinitesimal behavior
of dynamic risk measures. We showed that the resilience evaluation for general Itô processes
via BSDE-induced, cash-additive, normalized, and convex risk measures is well-defined and
admits an explicit representation. The examples and counterexamples clarify both the scope
of the construction and the role of the structural assumptions imposed on the risk measure.
Several natural extensions remain open. A first direction is to pass from the Brownian
filtration considered here to a Brownian–Poisson filtration, allowing both the underlying
processes and the risk evaluations to exhibit jumps. A second direction is to replace the
deterministic-time formulation by a formulation indexed by stopping times, which would
make the resilience evaluation more flexible for applications involving random horizons and
event-driven risk assessment.
