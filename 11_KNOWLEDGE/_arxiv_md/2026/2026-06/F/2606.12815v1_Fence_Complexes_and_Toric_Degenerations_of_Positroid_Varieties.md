FENCE COMPLEXES AND TORIC DEGENERATIONS OF POSITROID VARIETIES
CAMERON CHANG, PRANAV ENUGANDLA, AND JOSEPHINE HLAVINKA
Abstract. We associate to each positroid variety in the Grassmannian Gr(k, n) a polyhedral com-
plex, which we call a fence complex. Fence complexes consist of unions of faces of the Gelfand–Tsetlin
polytope Pk,n associated to a fundamental weight ωk. We show that these fence complexes are
homeomorphic to closed balls. Furthermore, they endow the Gelfand–Tsetlin polytope with the
structure of a regular CW complex, giving a polyhedral complex presentation of the regular CW
complex structure on Gr(k, n)≥0. We also show that the Ehrhart polynomial of a fence complex
equals the Hilbert polynomial of the associated positroid variety. We prove that under the Sturm-
fels–Gonciulea–Lakshmibai degeneration of Gr(k, n) to the toric variety of the Gelfand–Tsetlin
polytope, positroid varieties degenerate to the reduced union of toric varieties corresponding to
their fence complexes. As an application, we classify when positroid varieties contained inside hook
Schubert varieties are arithmetically Gorenstein. We also derive a recursive character formula for
cyclic Demazure modules, which we show is equivalent to a formula of Almousa, Gao and Huang.

1. Introduction
The theory of projective toric varieties over C gives a beautiful connection between algebraic
geometry and the combinatorics of polytopes.
Namely, to any toric variety X ⊂PN
C , one can
canonically associate (in a way depending only on X and its embedding) a lattice polytope P ⊂Zn,
where n = dim X. Then, the following remarkable phenomena hold:
(1) (Correspondence of strata) Since X is toric, we have a torus T ⊂X with an action on itself
that extends to all of X. There are only finitely many orbits of this action, and they form a
stratification of X, in the sense that the closure of an orbit O is the union of orbits. There
is an inclusion preserving bijection between the faces of P and the torus orbit closures of X,
with a face F contained in another face F ′ if and only if the corresponding orbit closure OF
is contained in OF ′.
(2) (Nonnegative geometry) We say a point p ∈PN
C is nonnegative if p = [x0 : x1 : · · · : xN]
where the xi are all nonnegative real numbers. The locus of nonnegative points in PN
C is
denoted (PN
C )≥0. Given a variety X ⊂PN
C , we define the nonnegative part of X to be
X≥0 := {p ∈X : p ∈(PN
C )≥0}.
When X is toric, the moment map with respect to the torus action gives a homeomorphism
between X≥0 and P.
This homeomorphism maps the nonnegative parts of torus orbit
closures to their corresponding faces. In particular, the faces of P endow X≥0 with the
structure of a regular CW complex structure.
(3) (Lattice point count interpretation) We define the Ehrhart polynomial of P to be ehrP (m) =
|mP ∩Zn|, where mP = {x ∈Rn : 1
mx ∈P}. Then, the Hilbert polynomial HX(m) of X
equals ehrP (m).
(4) (Ehrhart–Macdonald Reciprocity) We also have a combinatorial interpretation for the Hilbert
polynomial of X evaluated at negative numbers. Namely, HX(−m) = (−1)dim P intehrP (m),
where intehrP (m) equals the number of interior lattice points of mP.
For more details and definitions regarding these facts see [11] or [18]. Motivated by the above
phenomena, one could ask whether there exist similar polyhedral models for other stratified vari-
eties. A natural candidate is the Grassmannian Gr(k, n) of k-planes in n-space with the Schubert
stratification. The group of upper triangular invertible matrices B ⊂GL(n) acts on Cn, and hence
Gr(k, n). There are finitely many orbits under this action, and the closure of a B-orbit is the union
of B-orbits, so this is indeed a stratification. The closures of B-orbits are called Schubert varieties
and have been widely studied for a multitude of reasons, not least of which the fact that they form
a basis for the cohomology ring of Gr(k, n).
For the purpose of generalizing (2), however, this stratification does not behave particularly well.
Namely, if we take an open Schubert variety X◦⊂Gr(k, n), then, it is not true that (X◦)≥0 is
homeomorphic to Rm for some m. For instance, in P1

two Schubert varieties, X(1) and X∅. X∅is the point [1 : 0] and X(1) equals the P1

Hence, (X(1))≥0 is homeomorphic to R≥0, which is not homeomorphic to R.

Given this, one may wonder whether Gr(k, n)≥0 can in fact be identified with a polytope, with
faces of the polytope corresponding to positroid cells. It is not hard to see this is impossible. For
instance, in [35], Rietsch and Williams observe that Gr(2, 4)≥0 is four dimensional, and has four
3-dimensional positroid cells, but one can easily see that no four dimensional polytope with only
four facets exists. Thus, it may seem unlikely that one can hope for the same nice interplay between
polytopes, Ehrhart theory and nonnegative geometry as in the toric case.
Nonetheless, in this paper, we observe that by replacing "faces of polytopes" with "unions of faces
of a polytope", one can formulate analogues of toric phenomena (1)-(4) for positroids. In particular,
for each k ≤n, we consider a polytope Pk,n, to be thought of as a polyhedral model of Gr(k, n).

1.1. Relationship to Previous Work. Toric degenerations of various subvarieties of the Grassman-
nian have been studied often in the literature. While this is by no means an exhaustive list, we
mention a few results particularly relevant to our work. Sturmfels was the first to consider a toric
degeneration of Gr(k, n) to the toric variety of Pk,n in [40]. In [20], Gonciulea and Lakshmibai
calculate the flat limit of all Schubert varieties in Gr(k, n) under this degeneration. Since Schubert
varieties are special instances of positroid varieties, our fence complex construction agrees with the
Gonciulea–Lakshmibai degeneration.
Gonciulea and Lakshmibai also show that the complete flag variety degenerates to the toric variety
associated to a Gelfand–Tsetlin polytope. In [28], Kogan and Miller show that in an affine neighbor-
hood of the standard flag, Schubert varieties degenerate to the union of toric varieties associated to
faces indexed by pipe dreams. In Kim’s thesis (cf. [24]), he removes the hypothesis of restricting
to an affine neighborhood. Kim also shows that Richardson varieties in the complete flag variety

Degenerations of positroid varieties to subspace arrangements under the Hodge degeneration have
also been studied in the literature. In particular, [25] exhibited a Gröbner degeneration of the ho-
mogeneous coordinate ring C[Πw

degeneration is compatible with the Hodge degeneration. In [2], Almousa, Gao and Huang give an
explicit Gröbner basis for this degeneration. Our work makes heavy use of this Gröbner basis in
tandem with a technical result (see Theorem 6.13) in order to prove Theorem D.
In the same paper, Almousa, Gao and Huang also give a proof of a recursive formula for the
character of cyclic Demazure modules, independent of their Gröbner basis construction. Their ar-
guments connect the multigraded Hilbert series of positroid varieties (and hence cyclic Demazure
characters) with the multigraded Hilbert series of matrix Schubert varieties. They then use an
inductive recurrence on the multigraded Hilbert series of matrix Schubert varieties, coming from a
geometric argument, to deduce the corresponding recursion for cyclic Demazure characters. We use
our toric degeneration and the polyhedral geometry of Fw
u to prove a seemingly different character
formula, which makes no reference to matrix Schubert varieties. However, we show our formula is
actually the same as theirs, giving a new proof of their formula.
The fact that the totally nonnegative Grassmannian with the positroid stratification is a regular
CW complex was proved in [19] by Galashin, Karp and Lam. The proof is rather hard and relies
on, for instance, the generalized Poincaré conjecture. Our results do not give a new proof of this
theorem. Instead, we simply observe that the Fw
u give a regular CW complex structure on Pk,n, and
since Gr(k, n)≥0 is also a regular CW complex with the same cell closure poset, the two must be
isomorphic as regular CW complexes. It would be very interesting to give a new proof of Galashin,
Karp and Lam’s theorem using our results.

2.1. Positroid Varieties and k-Bruhat Combinatorics. We briefly define (closed) positroid varieties
here, but we recommend that readers unfamiliar with positroid varieties consult the survey [36].
Throughout the paper, all of our varieties are over C. We fix G = GLn(C), B ⊂GLn(C) the
subgroup of upper triangular matrices, and B−⊂GLn(C) the subgroup of lower triangular matrices.
Then, G/B is the complete flag variety, parametrizing all flags

Definition 2.1. A positroid variety Π is the image πk(X) of a Richardson variety X under πk.
Throughout the paper, we write Πw

irreducible, positroid varieties are closed irreducible subvarieties of Gr(k, n). They form a stratifi-
cation of Gr(k, n) which refines the Schubert and Richardson stratifications. Positroid varieties are
indexed by many different beautiful combinatorial objects, but we will focus on an indexing set that
comes naturally from the projected Richardson description.
The naive hope would be that positroids are in bijection with the Richardson varieties projecting
down to them, and hence in bijection with Bruhat intervals [u, w]. This turns out to be far from
true: in many cases we have Πw

if we place conditions on the Bruhat interval, this does become a bijection.
Definition 2.2. A permutation w ∈Sn is k-Grassmannian if its only possible descent is at k, i.e. we
have w(1) < w(2) < · · · < w(k), and w(k + 1) < w(k + 2) < · · · < w(n).
Remark 2.3. If we identify the permutations w in Sn such that w([k]) = [k] with Sk × Sn−k in the
natural way, then the k-Grassmannian permutations are precisely the representatives of cosets of
Sk × Sn−k in Sn that are minimal with respect to Bruhat order. As this is a useful description, we
identify Sk × Sn−k with the subgroup of permutations w ∈Sn fixing [k] for the remainder of the
paper.

Theorem 2.4 ([27, Theorem 5.9], [36, Theorem 1.29]). For every positroid variety Π, there exists a
unique Bruhat interval [u, w] with u, w ∈Sn such that w is k-Grassmannian and πk(Xw

Lemma A.12. For w ∈Sr, let x ∈[r] satisfy w(x) > x. Then, either x is a minimum of its cycle

maximum of the cycle, and let zl be the minimum, i.e. zl ≤zj ≤z1 for all j ∈[m]. By definition
of a cycle, we have zt = wt−1(z1) for 1 ≤t ≤m, and w(zm) = z1. Define the periodic function
f : Z →R given by f(i) = zi where indices are given mod m. Then, f has a unique continuous
extension F : [1, m] →R such that F is linear on [i, i + 1] for all i ∈[m −1].
Note that x ̸= z1 since w(x) > x and x ̸= zl by assumption. Then, by the Intermediate Value
Theorem, since F(1) < x < F(l), there exists some α ∈(1, l) such that F(α) = x. Since F is
piecewise linear and with finitely many different linear parts, none of which are constant, on [1, l],
there exist finitely many such α. Suppose α is the largest possible, i.e. F(β) ̸= x for β ∈(α, l).
If α is an integer, then F(α + 1) < F(α), otherwise either α + 1 = l, or F(α + 1) > x > F(l),
and the Intermediate Value Theorem contradicts our choice of α.

contradiction. Hence, α is not an integer. Thus, α ∈(i, i + 1) where 1 ≤i ≤i + 1 ≤l. If F is
increasing on (i, i+1), then i+1 < l, and F(i+1) > x > F(l), and once again the Intermediate Value
Theorem contradicts our choice of α. Hence, F is decreasing on (i, i + 1), so F(i) > x > F(i + 1).
In particular, zi > x > zi+1 = w(zi), as desired.
