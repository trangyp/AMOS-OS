Abstract
We propose an algebraic framework for the strange metal regime of strongly
correlated electrons.
We show that the exceptional superconformal algebra
D(2, 1; α) admits two distinct contractions of its conformal sector: one to a pair
of canonical fermions, the underlying degrees of freedom of the Landau–Fermi
liquid (LFL), and one to the algebra of Hubbard operators, which characterise a
distinct metallic regime, the Hubbard–Fermi liquid (HFL). We argue that com-
petition between these two metallic states drives the emergence of the strange
metal as a 0+1D superconformal bath. We analyse the resulting thermodynam-
ics, and obtain a parameter-free prediction, 4π2γ−1 = χ−1

Sommerfeld coefficient to the static spin and charge susceptibilities. We further
show that the LFL–HFL transition is discontinuous at low temperature, owing
to a degeneracy at the emergence of the HFL, and map out the resulting phase
diagram. We connect the framework to microscopic lattice models and to the
phenomenology of correlated insulators.

Introduction
The quantum theory of metals rests upon an algebraic assumption: that the electronic correlations
are governed by the canonical anti-commutation relations, {cσ, c†
σ′} = δσσ′. This underpins the
microscopic derivation of Landau–Fermi liquid (LFL) theory, and its reduction of the quantum
many-body problem to coherent quasiparticles. Yet in strongly correlated systems, such as the
cuprates and heavy fermion compounds, this framework systematically fails [1, 2]. A prominent
example is the strange metal, a macroscopic regime whose anomalous thermodynamics have resisted
a canonical description [3].
We argue that this failure can be traced algebraically.
The electron itself admits a second
distinct algebraic formulation, introduced by Hubbard in 1965 [4]. In previous work we cast this as
the Lie superalgebra su(2|2), and proposed that it governs a metallic state distinct from the LFL,
termed the Hubbard–Fermi liquid (HFL) [5, 6]. The Hubbard interaction enters as an emergent
chemical potential, splitting the electronic band in two. A consequence is that the Luttinger sum
rule, which encodes the topological protection of the LFL, is intrinsically violated here. The two
metallic states are not adiabatically connected, and the study of this LFL–HFL transition is a
central focus of this work.
We propose an algebraic framework that connects the canonical and Hubbard formulations of
the electron.
We show that both descend from the exceptional Lie superalgebra D(2, 1; α), as
distinct contractions of its conformal sector. This algebra is distinguished: it is the only simple
Lie superalgebra with continuously deformable structure constants [7], parameterised by α, and
we will show that this governs the LFL–HFL transition. The algebra’s conformal sector encodes
a purely temporal scale invariance, which we conjecture arises dynamically from the competing
coherence scales of the two metallic states, reducing the system to an effective 0+1D superconformal
bath. Evaluating the resulting Schwarzian thermodynamics in the semiclassical regime, we derive
a parameter-free thermodynamic relation:

We characterise the LFL–HFL transition within this framework. We show that it is discontin-

entropic grounds that the discontinuity terminates at a finite-temperature critical endpoint. The
loss of a coherent spatial description here is the origin of the 0 + 1D bath, and we present the
resulting phase diagram in Fig. 1.
This 0 + 1D description connects to, but is distinct from, the SYK [8, 9] and DMFT [10]
frameworks. Unlike those, which respectively rely on infinite-range couplings or infinite spatial
dimensions, we argue that a 0 + 1D bath emerges in finite-dimensional lattices at the LFL–HFL
transition. In this way, our approach offers a realisation of local quantum criticality, a dynamical
dimensional reduction proposed phenomenologically for the strange metal in cuprate and heavy
fermion systems [11,12].

Sec. 3, we detail the exceptional Lie superalgebra D(2, 1; α), and demonstrate how it contracts to
yield the two distinct electronic formulations. In Sec. 4, we present our central conjecture for the

The electronic degree of freedom
The core challenge of many-body quantum physics is the exponential complexity of interacting
systems, and an efficient description relies upon identifying appropriate degrees of freedom. A well-
chosen degree of freedom isolates the most physically relevant correlations, providing the foundation
for an effective quasiparticle description.
Mathematically, a quantum degree of freedom is specified by the algebra it obeys. In Heisen-
berg’s equations of motion, the dynamics of an operator O are governed by the commutator [H, O].
In the presence of interactions, these equations cannot be closed exactly, and approximations are
required.
The choice of algebra guides the truncation of these equations, ultimately dictating
the dispersive modes. Indeed, any perturbative approximation carries an inherent, often implicit,
assumption: that the chosen algebraic framework encodes the system’s dominant physics.
Here, we apply this principle to the electron [5,6]. The Hilbert space of a single electron consists
of four basis states:

two fundamentally different metallic regimes.

2.2.1
The Hubbard–Fermi liquid
We argue that the Hubbard formulation of the electron characterises a legitimate state of elec-
tronic matter, a metal with long-lived quasiparticles organised by the su(2|2) algebra. As this is
fundamentally distinct from a Landau–Fermi liquid (LFL), we term this instead a Hubbard–Fermi
liquid1 (HFL).
Hubbard operators, and related approaches, have long been employed to study the interacting
electron problem [4, 20–23].
There is an important distinction to make however.
These works
treated Hubbard operators as a computational basis for computing the electronic Green’s function,
with violation of the Luttinger sum rule an artifact of an uncontrolled approximation. We argue
that by regarding the Hubbard algebra as a true degree of freedom, the violation is an intrinsic
feature that distinguishes the metallic state from a LFL.
There is an obstacle to establishing the HFL however. It is not straightforward to develop
a perturbative expansion which respects conservation laws order by order. Within the canonical
formalism, the anti-commutator yields a scalar identity, which guarantees the applicability of Wick’s
theorem and enables the construction of the Luttinger–Ward functional Φ order by order. The
existence of Φ rests upon δΣk
δGk′ = δΣk′
δGk , which is a flatness condition for Φ. In contrast, the Hubbard
anti-commutation relations yield non-scalar terms, the spin and charge generators. While in the
limit κ →0 the algebra flattens, for finite κ these non-canonical contributions render Wick’s
theorem inapplicable, and their inherent curvature obstructs the definition of a corresponding Φ-
functional. This raises the question of how to formulate a systematic conserving expansion for the
non-canonical degree of freedom, and offers an explanation for why there is no known counterpart
of the Luttinger sum rule.

direct parallel is found in localized magnetism, where the physical operators obey the su(2) spin
algebra. With respect to a polarised reference state |↑⟩, the algebra is cast as

2.3
Competing metallic regimes
We have argued that the canonical and Hubbard formulations of the electron are not merely comple-
mentary mathematical bases, but that they characterise two distinct metallic states. The canonical
formulation underpins the LFL, where the charge carrier density is fixed by the Luttinger sum rule.
The Hubbard formulation underpins the HFL, where the Hubbard interaction serves as an emer-
gent chemical potential that splits the electronic band in two, and the Luttinger count is generically
violated.
This mismatch of the Luttinger count is a signature of the distinct nature of the two regimes.
Within the LFL, the Luttinger count is a topological property of the Fermi surface, protected so
long as the quasiparticles remain coherent. As the HFL Fermi surface violates the Luttinger count,
any transition from a LFL to a HFL requires a Fermi surface rearrangement. The two metals
cannot be adiabatically connected, any path joining them at T = 0 must be non-analytic.
The LFL–HFL transition is not confined to the infrared. The splitting of the electronic band
in two requires a global redistribution of spectral weight, across the energy scale of the emergent
chemical potential U [28]. This distinguishes it from conventional transitions, in which the spectral
rearrangement is confined to the low-energy vicinity of the Fermi surface.
This large-scale spectral rearrangement presents a theoretical hurdle. It cannot be captured per-
turbatively, neither by truncating the equations of motion of the canonical many-body perturbation
theory, nor through an as-yet-unestablished non-canonical expansion. Instead, an overarching al-
gebraic framework is required, and this is what we propose in Sec. 3.
We can frame the location of the LFL–HFL transition by considering the competing coherence
scales of the two metallic regimes. Deep within the LFL and HFL phases, their ground states
are protected by their respective coherence scales T ∗
LFL and T ∗
HFL, below which their dispersive
quasiparticle modes are robust. However, as the system is tuned towards the LFL–HFL phase
boundary, these protection mechanisms compete. We may identify the transition regime by the
condition where these coherence scales intersect, T ∗
LFL ∼T ∗
HFL, and we will argue in Sec. 4 that
this occurs at finite temperature.
At temperatures above this intersection, the coherent quasiparticle of either metal is lost. We
argue that this is a critical regime, born of LFL–HFL competition, which realises an emergent
superconformal symmetry, governed by the algebraic structure of Sec. 3.

4
The strange metal and its exceptional thermodynamics
In Sec. 2 we presented the canonical and Hubbard forms of the electron as two distinct ways to
characterise metallic behaviour, the LFL and HFL. In Sec. 3, we showed that the exceptional
Lie superalgebra D(2, 1; α) admits contractions of its conformal symmetry in two distinct limits,
which map precisely onto these two electronic algebras. We now argue that D(2, 1; α) provides
the organising principle for the LFL–HFL transition, with its conformal sl(2, R) sector governing
an emergent temporal dynamics, enabling a reorganisation of the electronic spectrum. This is our
conjecture for the exceptional origin of the strange metal: it is a manifestation of an emergent
superconformal symmetry arising from competition between the two metallic states.
The emergence of D(2, 1; α) as a macroscopic symmetry presents a strict kinematic constraint.
Its conformal sector, sl(2, R), is the conformal algebra of a zero-dimensional space, with purely

4.2.3
The loss of spatial coherence
Up to this point, we have conjectured the loss of spatial coherence that gives rise to the 0 + 1D
bath. In Sec. 4.2 we showed that the bath is stable: once formed, electronic hopping is an irrelevant
perturbation. We now discuss this dimensional reduction from the perspective of the two metallic
states.
That the LFL–HFL transition is discontinuous, with a finite-temperature endpoint, has an
important consequence: both Fermi liquids remain robust near the transition, with their respective
coherence temperatures finite. The quasiparticle weight remains finite, and the transition is non-
perturbative from either metallic ground state. Thus, we cannot expect to trace the dimensional
reduction within either framework.
We highlight that the transition itself is of an unconventional kind. While it is discontinuous
with a finite-temperature endpoint, it is not a conventional symmetry-breaking transition, as the
two metallic states are not distinguished by a local order parameter. Instead, they are distinguished
by the topology of their Fermi surfaces, each protected by a robust quasiparticle.
Near the transition, both formulations of the electron are reflected in the spectrum, but the
low-energy weight is determined by which of the two has the higher coherence scale, with its
quasiparticle defining the Fermi surface (Fig. 1(a)). Below Te the transition is discontinuous, and
the Fermi surface rearranges across it. At the approach to Te the transition becomes continuous,
but the two formulations cannot simultaneously govern the low-energy dynamics as their Fermi
surfaces are incompatible. We argue that, where their coherence mechanisms compete, a coherent
momentum-space description must break down.
Our conjecture for the strange metal is that here only the local degrees of freedom remain to
organise the spectrum, and that these arrange into the uncontracted representation of D(2, 1; α),
giving rise to the scale-invariant 0 + 1D bath at the junction of the two metallic regimes.

through the diffusion of the conserved charge, spin and energy densities of the bath, in analogy with
transport in coupled-SYK lattices [41,42]. The conductivities are set by the Einstein relations,

Microscopic framing
So far our discussion has been completely general, based on the assumption that the LFL and
HFL exist as distinct metallic states, and our conjecture that the superconformal algebra D(2, 1; α)
governs the transition between them via an emergent superconformal bath.
We now turn to a microscopic perspective, as an exploration of where the LFL–HFL transition
may occur. As a function of band filling, we expect that the HFL regime, when it exists, is naturally
anchored around half-filling, while the LFL regime extends from the band edges. A natural question
then is whether there are limits in the parameter space of microscopic models where the LFL–HFL
transition occurs right at half-filling, while a Mott gap is maintained, or alternatively where the HFL
regime extends to the edges of its split bands. Here we leverage some exact results to suggest that
the former occurs for the standard Hubbard model, while the latter occurs for the Hubbard-Shastry
B (HSB) model.

5.1.3
Mapping the transition
We now consider the location of the LFL–HFL transition as a function of filling. Here we focus on
the parent metallic states, overlooking ordering instabilities. We argue that the Hubbard model
is a limit where the HFL phase is fully suppressed and the LFL extends to the Mott boundary at
half-filling, while a Mott gap is maintained, and that HSB and EKS are models where the HFL
extends to the extremes of the fully empty and fully occupied band.
For the Hubbard model this is borne out in the infinite-dimensional limit. Single-site DMFT
shows that once a Mott gap opens at half-filling, it is separated from the doped metallic state
by a first-order transition, and this remains the case as the strength of the Hubbard interaction
is increased [10, 52]. Employed as an approximation in finite dimensions, it yields the frequency
dependence of a momentum-independent self-energy, with the metallic state obeying the Luttinger
sum rule, and the inference is that this describes a LFL that extends to the discontinuous Mott
boundary. We highlight that this is consistent with the kinetic term of the Hubbard model having
the conventional canonical bilinear form, (κ = λ = 0). We suggest that the finite-temperature
endpoint Te merging with the Mott boundary here is a special feature of this model. Within our
framework, this corresponds to the subtle α →0 limit discussed at the end of Sec. 4.4, and we will
return to this again from both a material and criticality perspective in Sec. 5.2.1.
Taking κ > 0 introduces correlated hopping and spin and charge interactions. Generically, we
expect a HFL regime to open up as these are introduced, with the consequence that the LFL–HFL
transition moves away from half-filling.
For the HSB model, the evidence that the HFL regime extends across the whole (split) band
is its protected fermionic modes, Eq. (64). As these carry momenta ⃗0 and ⃗π, we interpret these as
modes at the edges of the band. Moreover, in the limit to the EKS model at κ →1, and generally
when t± = 0, it follows from Eqs. (65) and (66) that the electronic spectrum is strictly split into
two, with the Hubbard interaction shifting the split bands oppositely in the spectrum. We thus
suggest that the HFL phase extends across the whole band for the HSB model. This has a notable
consequence: the band remains split with a Mott gap for all 0 < κ ≤1, with the gap only vanishing
in the non-interacting limit, κ →0. We distinguish this from the Hubbard model, where above 1d
the Mott gap only opens at finite U. We interpret this as follows. For the Hubbard model the split
bands are incoherent, and the Mott gap opens once these shift apart. By contrast, for the HSB
model the splitting occurs directly at the centre of the electronic band, opening a gap for κ > 0.
Moreover, this analysis offers an interpretation of the HSB model’s dynamical fermionic symme-
try as another special limit of our framework. The edges of the band represent the dilute limits of
electronic excitations. As the LFL–HFL transition approaches these extremes, the residual entropy
of the 0 + 1D bath ceases to be extensive, and the critical endpoint Te drops to zero. From the
perspective of D(2, 1; α), this corresponds to the limit α →1, where the algebra becomes osp(4|2)
(App. C). Based on this, we conjecture that the HSB model exhibits a zero-density quantum critical
point [53].
5.2
Material perspective
This framing may help to understand which microscopic interactions drive the stability of the HFL
at finite doping. In turn, this influences the temperature of the ordering instability of the strange
metal, as discussed in Sec. 4.1.4.
To ground this in a material context, it is instructive to view our framing through the lens of the
21


## Page 24


Zaanen-Sawatzky-Allen (ZSA) classification of transition-metal compounds [54]. The ZSA scheme
distinguishes correlated insulators based on the competition between two energy scales: the on-site
Coulomb repulsion U of the transition-metal d-orbitals, and the charge-transfer energy ∆required
to move an electron from the surrounding ligand p-orbitals to the metal. Their competition leads
to the following dichotomy:
• Mott-Hubbard insulator: U < ∆. The low-energy excitations are confined to the transition
metal sites, and Hubbard U is the dominant interaction.
• Charge-transfer insulator: ∆< U. The low-energy excitations are hybridized metal-ligand
states, resulting in an effective single-orbital description with correlated hopping amplitudes,
along with local spin and charge interactions.
The Hubbard model is the paradigmatic model of a Mott-Hubbard insulator. Although the specific
couplings of the HSB model are fine-tuned and do not map to measured material parameters, we
offer the perspective that it can be viewed as an effective single-orbital model of a theoretically
idealised charge-transfer insulator.
5.2.1
Mott criticality
We focus now on Mott-Hubbard insulators. These are expected to be well-described by the Hubbard
model, and so within our framework the critical endpoint is merged with the Mott boundary. The
critical behaviour here is termed Mott criticality, and the prevailing paradigm is a DMFT prediction
of a mean-field, liquid-gas Ising universality class [52].
The status of this prediction is, however, unsettled. In a canonical Mott-Hubbard insulator
Cr-doped V2O3 mean-field scaling is reported [55], with the caveat that the critical endpoint is
obscured by magnetic and structural ordering. Alternatively, in frustrated organic Mott insulators
where this ordering is suppressed and the endpoint exposed, non-mean-field criticality is instead
reported [56,57], and the topic remains under debate [58].
Our framework offers a fresh perspective.
As discussed at the end of Sec. 4.4, the critical
endpoint being merged with the Mott boundary represents a subtle limit of our formalism, α →0,
where the UV scale of the superconformal bath of Sec. 4 drops down into the IR.
We can also track this limit algebraically. The limit α →0 is λc →∞where the charge sector
becomes infinitely stiff, and at the level of D(2, 1; α), the charge generators drop out of Eq. (15),
and decouple from the algebra, reducing it to psu(1, 1|2) (App. C). We thus conjecture that Mott
criticality has a PSU(1, 1|2) superconformal symmetry. Here the super-Virasoro extension is the
small N = 4 superconformal algebra [59]. As for the strange metal, this critical regime is 0 + 1D,
and we interpret the dimensional reduction that occurs here as the Mott localisation of the electron.

Discussion
We have developed an electronic interpretation of the exceptional Lie superalgebra D(2, 1; α),
demonstrating that its structure encodes a phenomenology mapping directly onto the strongly
correlated electron problem. Within this framework, D(2, 1; α) governs an effective 0 + 1D super-
conformal bath, which we interpret as the strange metal. We argued that the LFL and HFL metallic
states emerge as spatial coherence is restored, with the canonical and Hubbard formulations of the
23


## Page 26


electron arising as distinct contractions of its conformal sector. We also examined the limit where
the HFL is suppressed, and traced the emergence of Mott criticality.
Central to this framework is the LFL–HFL transition. It appears to be of an unconventional
kind: it is discontinuous, with a finite-temperature critical endpoint, yet there is no associated
symmetry-breaking.
The two metallic states are distinguished not by a local order parameter,
but by the topology of their Fermi surfaces, each protected by a robust quasiparticle. Within our
framework, this transition has its origin in the deformable structure constants of D(2, 1; α): the
superconformal bath is thermodynamically stable for α > 0, whereas the Hubbard contraction is
locked to the degenerate point α = −1, and thus the HFL cannot emerge continuously from the
bath (Sec. 4.2.1). We are not aware of a parallel transition of this kind, and it is natural to relate
this to the status of D(2, 1; α) as the only simple Lie superalgebra with continuously deformable
structure constants. In this sense, the transition is exceptional in its origin.
From our semiclassical analysis of the thermodynamics of the superconformal bath, we obtained
a parameter-free experimental prediction, Eq. (41), relating the Sommerfeld coefficient to the static
spin and charge susceptibilities. This provides a falsifiable test of our framework. Potential testing
grounds are the strange metal regimes of the cuprates [1] and heavy-fermion compounds [2]. While
the thermal and spin properties of these materials have been extensively characterised, the precise
measurement of the charge response has proven more challenging [68]. More recently, 2D moir´e
materials, such as magic-angle twisted bilayer graphene, have been shown to exhibit strange metal
behaviour [69]. Here the experimental difficulty is inverted: χc is accessible via local compressibility
measurements [70], but γ and χs are more challenging owing to the small sample volume.
Beyond direct experimental measurement, our framework can be investigated numerically.
Prominent here are the DMFT family of methods [10, 71]. Exact in the limit of infinite dimen-
sions, the approach offers a window onto the metallic, insulating and critical regimes within a
single framework. Cluster extensions, which incorporate short-range spatial correlations, have been
shown to describe discontinuous finite-temperature transition away from half-filling [72]. While
the thermodynamic variables are computable within this approach, we are not aware of a single
study that simultaneously extracts all three (γ, χs, χc) for the critical regime. There is a caveat
worth highlighting, however. As discussed in Sec. 5.2.1, single-site DMFT renders the critical end-
point mean-field. It would be interesting to determine whether cluster extensions can resolve the
intrinsically local superconformal criticality predicted by our framework.
Our analysis of the thermodynamics of the superconformal bath leaves several open directions.
While we focused primarily on the semiclassical saddle-point approximation, the one-loop quan-
tum correction is exact, as discussed in Sec. 4.1.3. For the entropy this can be inferred from the
temperature dependence of the source-free partition function, and we obtained a logarithmic cor-
rection. The spin and charge response requires incorporating sources, and we leave the analysis of
this to future work. It would be of interest in particular to establish whether there is a subleading
correction to the exceptional thermodynamic relation, which may have its own thermodynamic
signature. The study of transport is another open direction. We have argued that spin and charge
must diffuse, as governed by the Einstein relations, and leave the analysis of the diffusion constants
to future work.
The Hubbard–Fermi liquid is a pillar of our framework.
While we have argued that it is
a legitimate state of electronic matter, there is not yet a settled framework for characterising
it. We highlighted in Sec. 2.2.1 that the requirement of a conserving, Φ-derivable perturbative
scheme may be too stringent, due to the intrinsic curvature of the Hubbard algebra. As spin-wave
24


## Page 27


theory faces the same obstruction, we view this as a problem of method rather than of existence.
The development of a systematic framework for the HFL thus remains an important outstanding
direction.
It is instructive to contrast the HFL with other proposals for a metal which violates the Luttinger
sum rule. The fractionalised Fermi liquid, FL∗, is an approach that factorises the electron, c ∼f b,
accompanied by an emergent gauge field, and is typically formulated at the level of an effective field
theory rather than a microscopic operator identity [17]. In contrast, the HFL is a linear splitting
of the electron, e.g. c†
↓= q↑◦−q†
↓•, such that excitations retain the electronic quantum numbers.
Another prominent approach is the notion of Mottness, which ties the Luttinger violation to zeros
of the canonical Green’s function [73]. Within our framework, the canonical quasiparticle remains
robust right up to the LFL–HFL transition, beyond which the low-energy spectrum is organised by
the Hubbard formulation, and zeros of the canonical Green’s function play no role.
Our framework extends naturally to Kondo lattice systems [19], relevant for heavy-fermion
compounds [2]. As detailed in App. B, the local moment is incorporated by enlarging the su(2|2)
representation, with the conduction-electron ⃗s and local moment ⃗S entering through the combi-
nation ⃗Σ = ⃗s + ⃗S. The Kondo coupling then contributes to the emergent chemical potential that
splits the band, and the splitting of the electron carries over. In this setting we regard the Heavy
Fermi liquid as an instance of the HFL, with the local moment entwined with the electron rather
than free to order independently. We can thus view the heavy-fermion phase diagram from the
perspective of the LFL–HFL transition, with the strange metal emerging from the rearrangement
of the Fermi surface.
Finally, while our proposal for the exceptional origin of the strange metal stands independent of
holographic duality, we highlight that our framework has deep roots in the literature of this subject.
The algebraic structures we employed have been developed within the study of integrability in
the AdS/CFT correspondence [74,75], notably Beisert’s discovery that the exceptionally extended
su(2|2) algebra governs the integrability of the 1D Hubbard model, and its relationship to D(2, 1; α)
[16]. In addition, the superconformal theories we invoke for the strange metal and Mott criticality
have been studied as the near-horizon descriptions of extremal black holes [36, 39, 59], and these
results helped inform our formulation of the D(2, 1; α) effective action. Looking forward, it may be
interesting to explore a holographic description of the LFL–HFL transition.
7
Conclusion
We have developed a unified algebraic framework for the strongly correlated electron problem,
encompassing the LFL and HFL metallic states, the strange metal, and Mott criticality.
The
two metallic states descend as distinct contractions of the exceptional Lie superalgebra D(2, 1; α),
the unique simple Lie superalgebra with continuously deformable structure constants. We have
argued that this deformability is the origin of the transition between the two metals, and of the
superconformal strange metal that emerges from it.
From the thermodynamics of this critical
regime we obtained a parameter-free relation between the Sommerfeld coefficient and the static
spin and charge susceptibilities, a prediction by which the framework can be tested.
