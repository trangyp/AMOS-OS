Abstract: We study the IG(JPC) = 0+(1++) K ¯Kπ system in an infinite-volume three-
body unitary framework, focusing on the pole content of the region of the f1(1285) and
f1(1420) resonances. The coupled πa0-K ¯K∗amplitude is constructed in the spectator-
isobar representation, where the one-particle-exchange interaction required by three-body
unitarity automatically incorporates the triangle-singularity mechanism. The short-range
three-body interaction is constrained by fitting the 0+(1++) component of the BESIII
K0
SK0
Sπ0 invariant-mass distribution in the J/ψ →γ(K0
SK0
Sπ0) decay. Analytically con-
tinuing the fitted amplitude to the relevant unphysical Riemann sheets, we find two robust
poles:
psf1(1285) = (1277 ± 2 ± 1) −i (12 ± 1 ± 0) MeV ,
psf1(1420) = (1435 ± 2 ± 7) −i (40 ± 2 ± 1) MeV .
The pole trajectories indicate that the f1(1285) originates from dressing a bare state intro-

the nearby bare state, supporting its hadronic-molecule interpretation. We also find an
additional pole deeper in the complex plane in the best-fit amplitude on the same Rie-
mann sheet as the f1(1285). This additional pole is generated by the P-wave πa0 contact
interaction alone. It has a sizable cutoff and two-body-input dependence, and leaves little
visible imprint on the physical lineshape. Finally, we provide a detailed and pedagogical
appendix on how three-body cuts affect the solution of the integral equation.

Introduction
Three-body dynamics is a long-standing problem in hadron physics. Many hadron reso-
nances are observed through decays into three or more particles, and in several important
cases the nearby two-body subsystems are themselves resonant. The separation between
a genuine quantum chromodynamics (QCD) state and a kinematic enhancement is then
not always transparent from a lineshape alone.
Examples range from light-meson sys-
tems [1–11] and baryon excitations with large ππN components [12–18] to near-threshold
charmonium-like structures [19–24]; see also Refs. [25–28] for recent reviews.
Even for
genuine resonances, such as the X(3872) [29–31] and T +
cc [32–39] in the hidden-charm and
doubly charmed sectors, their couplings to D ¯Dπ or DDπ channels are crucial to determine
the physical lineshapes and the pole positions.
Several complementary approaches have been developed to treat such three-body ef-
fects. Dispersive methods and Khuri-Treiman equations have been extensively used to de-
scribe three-meson final-state interactions in light-hadron decays [40–46]. Non-relativistic
Lippmann-Schwinger equations are particularly useful for heavy near-threshold systems,
where the relevant momenta are small and unstable two-body constituents can be included

explicitly [30, 34, 47–52]. Relativistic field-theoretic formulations and finite-volume three-
body quantization conditions provide another route, especially for connecting lattice-QCD
spectra to infinite-volume amplitudes [37, 38, 53–57]. The approach adopted in this work
is the infinite-volume unitary amplitude in the spectator-isobar representation, in which
the amplitude is constrained by three-body unitarity and can be analytically continued
to search for resonance poles [58–62]; the corresponding finite-volume unitarity method
can also be utilized in lattice-QCD studies [63–69]; see Ref. [70] for a textbook overview.
The relation between different formulations of three-body dynamics has been discussed in
Refs. [39, 70–76].
In this paper we apply the infinite-volume unitary framework to the IG(JPC) =
0+(1++) K ¯Kπ system, with particular emphasis on the two structures historically known
as the f1(1285) and f1(1420). Both states have played an important role in the spectroscopy
of axial-vector mesons. Experimentally, the f1(1285) is a well-established narrow state [77],
while the f1(1420) appears close to the K ¯K∗threshold and is observed dominantly in the
K ¯Kπ channel [78–86]. In the πa0 →ηππ channel, the experimental situation is more sub-
tle: a clear f1(1285) signal is accompanied by additional strength around 1.4 GeV, which
has sometimes been associated with the f1(1420) [87].
The theoretical interpretation of these structures is not settled. In conventional quark
models, the f1(1285) and f1(1420) are assigned to the same 1++ nonet [88–90]. On the
other hand, because the f1(1420) lies very close to the K ¯K∗threshold and couples strongly
to that channel, it has also been interpreted as a hadronic molecule [91, 92]. A different
conclusion was reached in unitarized chiral approaches, where the f1(1285), rather than the
f1(1420), can emerge as a dynamically generated K ¯K∗state [93–95].1 In that picture, the
enhancement near 1.4 GeV in the K ¯K∗channel may arise from the f1(1285) tail together
with the opening of the K ¯K∗channel [97] (which is, however, inconsistent with the strongly
enhanced sharp peaks observed), while that in the πa0 channel is attributed to the triangle
singularity mechanism.
A triangle singularity is a logarithmic branch point that occurs when the three internal
particles in a triangle diagram can simultaneously go on shell and move collinearly so that
the process can be interpreted as a classical space-time process [98–100]. It can produce a
resonance-like peak when it is close to the physical region; see Ref. [25] for a detailed review.
For the present quantum numbers, the relevant mechanism is f1(1285) →K ¯K∗, followed
by ¯K∗→¯Kπ and K ¯K →a0, producing a K ¯K ¯K∗triangle loop contribution to the πa0 final
state. Such a singularity is expected to generate a non-trivial structure in the lineshape
near the K ¯K∗threshold [97, 101–107]. Therefore, a meaningful interpretation of the region
of the f1(1285) and f1(1420) should not simply add or remove Breit-Wigner resonances, but
should ask whether physical poles remain after the kinematic effects required by three-body
dynamics are included.2

Three-body unitary amplitude
2.1
Bethe-Salpeter equation
We construct the K ¯Kπ scattering amplitude using the infinite-volume three-body unitary
framework developed in Refs. [58–62]. This approach is based on a 1 + 2 decomposition,
in which the full three-body amplitude T3 is expressed in terms of a reduced amplitude
T describing the interaction between a spectator particle and the remaining two-body
subsystem (referred to as the isobar or dimer), as illustrated in Fig. 1. The factors outside
the parentheses correspond to the isobar dissociation vertices, which connect to the external
asymptotic three-body states. Inside the parentheses, the relevant degrees of freedom are
those of the spectator and the isobar, and their dynamics can be further separated into
reducible and irreducible contributions. The former corresponds to processes in which the
spectator does not participate in the scattering and propagates freely from the initial to the

In this BS equation, there are four building blocks to be specified. Two of them are
fixed by unitarity, namely the one-particle-exchange term B and the isobar “self-energy”
(encoded in τ). Their on-shell contributions generate the imaginary part of the amplitude,

constrain the real part of the inverse amplitude even in the physical region. Therefore,
two additional off-shell inputs are required: the K-matrix of the two-body subsystem (also
contained in τ) and a short-range three-body contact interaction C. The former can be
determined by fitting two-body scattering phase shifts, while the latter may be fixed from
the lineshapes of 1 →3 decay processes.

representation, as shown in Fig. 2. Throughout this work, time is taken to flow from right
to left, such that the diagrammatic representation of amplitudes follows the same ordering
