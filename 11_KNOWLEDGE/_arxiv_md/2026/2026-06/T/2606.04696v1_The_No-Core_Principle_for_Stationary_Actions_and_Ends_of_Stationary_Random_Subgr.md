The No-Core Principle for Stationary Actions
and Ends of Stationary Random Subgroups
Yair Hartman, Nadav Kalma
Abstract
We prove a no-core principle for stationary actions of countable groups.
Namely, if a Borel set intersects almost every orbit in finitely many points
and has positive measure, then it is supported, modulo null sets, on the
finite-orbit part of the action. This extends to stationary actions a basic
regularity phenomenon known for measure-preserving actions.
We apply this principle to the geometry of Stationary Random Sub-
groups. For a finitely generated group, we prove that the Schreier graph of
a stationary random subgroup has almost surely 0, 1, 2, or infinitely many
ends. Finally, we contrast this probabilistic regularity with the topologi-
cal notion of Boomerang subgroups: for every k ≥3, including k = ℵ0, we
construct a Boomerang subgroup of F3 whose Schreier graph has exactly
k ends.

Introduction
In the theory of group actions on measure spaces, one considers probability
measure-preserving (p.m.p.) actions and non-singular actions, namely those that
preserve the measure class. Non-singular actions provide a broad framework
requiring minimal rigid structure. While every p.m.p. action is inherently non-
singular, the former exhibits a wide variety of strong regularity properties.
Of particular interest to our work is the fact that, on the infinite-orbit part,
p.m.p. actions do not admit Borel selectors of positive measure.
Stationary actions occupy an intermediate realm; these are actions equipped
with a measure that is invariant under convolution with respect to a probability
measure µ ∈Prob(Γ) (a “random walk”) on the group. Every p.m.p. action is µ-
stationary, for every µ, and every µ-stationary action is non-singular, assuming
that µ is generating, namely, the support of µ generates Γ as a semigroup. A
key advantage of stationary measures over p.m.p. actions is their guaranteed
existence on compact spaces, even for actions of non-amenable groups. At the
same time, stationary measures are more “regulated” than general non-singular
actions.
A natural question arises: which of the properties and regularities of p.m.p.
actions extend to the broader context of stationary actions?

Our first result shows that if a stationary action admits a positive-measure
“core”, then that core is necessarily supported on finite orbits.
Theorem 1.1 (No-core principle for stationary actions). Let µ ∈Prob(Γ) be a
generating measure, and let (Γ, µ) ↷(X, ν) be a stationary action. Let C ⊆X
be a measurable set with ν(C) > 0, such that for ν-a.e. x ∈X, |Γx ∩C| < ∞.
Then for ν-a.e. x ∈C, |Γx| < ∞.
Equivalently, almost every point in C has a finite orbit.
We call a set C as in the theorem above a core.
Our result holds in particular for p.m.p. actions, as they are µ-stationary
for every generating µ. This result for p.m.p. actions is known using tools of
equivalence relations and Descriptive Set Theory (see, for example [Gab25]).
It is interesting that our proof uses random walks to prove this result, even in
the p.m.p. setup. One should note that the no-core principle does not hold for
general non-singular actions.
The No-Core Principle was first introduced in [AB22] to prove a result re-
garding the space of ends for unimodular random manifolds. We apply this
principle to address a similar question in the theory of Stationary Random Sub-
groups (SRSs).
Let Γ be a discrete countable group, let SubΓ denote the compact space
of all subgroups of Γ, equipped with the Chabauty topology, and with the
conjugation action of Γ. A random subgroup is a probability measure on SubΓ.
An Invariant Random Subgroup (IRS), introduced in [AGV14], is a conjugation-
invariant probability measure on SubΓ. Equivalently, the conjugation action
Γ ↷(SubΓ, ν) is p.m.p. Similarly, a µ-Stationary Random Subgroup (SRS) is
a µ-stationary measure on SubΓ.
By viewing a subgroup geometrically via its Schreier graph, one can ask
which geometric properties of normal subgroups generalize to IRSs, and subse-
quently, which properties of IRSs extend to SRSs. In this article, we address
this question concerning the ends of a graph.
For a locally finite graph, the space of ends is a topological invariant that for-
malizes the notion of “directions to infinity”. The classical theorem of Freuden-
thal [Fre44] and Hopf [Hop44] establishes that the Cayley graph of a finitely
generated group has exactly 0, 1, 2, or infinitely (uncountable) many ends. Fur-
thermore, this number is independent of the choice of generating set, making it
a group invariant. Building on this, Stallings [Sta71] proved that the number of
ends reveals deep algebraic information about the group; for example, a group
is 2-ended if and only if it is virtually Z.
Motivated by these classical results, we say a collection of graphs is end-
rigid if each of its graphs has exactly 0, 1, 2, or infinitely many ends. Thus the
collection of Cayley graphs of finitely generated groups is end-rigid. Equiva-
lently, the Schreier graphs associated with normal subgroups (which are Cayley
graphs of quotients), are end-rigid. Abels [Abe73] proved that the collection
of quasi-transitive graphs (i.e., all graphs with a finite number of orbits under
their automorphism group) is end-rigid. In terms of SubΓ, all Schreier graphs
of subgroups with finite conjugacy classes are end-rigid.

For IRSs, unimodularity gives the stronger conclusion that the infinite case
is uncountable. Our argument for SRSs rules out finite numbers of ends larger
than two, but does not rule out countably infinitely many ends.
Simultaneously with and independently of our work, Levit and Silman [LS26]
proved a related result in a slightly different setting, using different tools. Their
argument applies to the general framework of random metric-measure spaces
and they also show that when the end space is infinite, it is topologically, then
it is a Cantor set. Moreover, they classify the homeomorphism types of surfaces,
generalizing the work of Biringer-Raimbault [BT16] on unimodular Riemannian
manifolds to the stationay setup.
In the finitely generated group setting, however, the approach taken in [LS26]
requires the random walk to be finitely supported and symmetric, assumptions
that are not needed in our argument. This leaves open the following natural
question: can a µ-SRS for an infinitely supported or non-symmetric measure
have countably infinitely many ends almost surely?

Preliminaries
Non-singular actions, invariant measures, and stationary
measures
Let Γ be a discrete countable group acting on a compact topological space X
by measurable automorphisms, making X a Γ-space.
This action naturally
extends to the space of Borel probability measures Prob(X) via the formula
(g.ν)(E) = ν(g−1E).
A measure ν ∈Prob(X) is ergodic if every Γ-invariant Borel set has measure
0 or 1. The action of Γ on (X, ν) is non-singular if g.ν and ν are mutually
absolutely continuous (meaning they share the same null sets) for all g ∈Γ.
A measure is invariant if g.ν = ν for all g ∈Γ. However, invariant measures
on compact spaces are only guaranteed to exist when Γ is an amenable group.
A meausre µ ∈Prob(Γ) is called generating if the support of the measure
generates Γ as a semigroup, meaning S
n≥1 supp(µ∗n) = Γ. Given a generating
probability measure µ ∈Prob(Γ), a measure ν ∈Prob(X) is µ-stationary if it
satisfies the convolution equation ν = µ ∗ν = P
g∈Γ µ(g)g.ν.
Stationary measures are highly useful because a µ-stationary measure is
guaranteed to exist on any compact Γ-space, even when invariant measures do
not. Furthermore, if µ is a generating measure, any µ-stationary measure is au-
tomatically non-singular. This establishes a natural hierarchy: every invariant
measure is µ-stationary, and every µ-stationary measure is non-singular.

SubΓ, IRS and SRS
We denote by SubΓ ⊆2Γ the space of subgroups of Γ, equipped with the
subspace topology (commonly referred to as the Chabauty topology).
Note
that SubΓ is a compact metrizable space, and Γ acts on it by conjugation:
g.H = gHg−1.
An Invariant Random Subgroup (IRS) is a Borel probability measure λ ∈
Prob(SubΓ) which is invariant under the conjugation action of Γ. An IRS can
be seen as a probabilistic generalization of a normal subgroup, in the sense that
the Dirac measure δN is an IRS if and only if N ⊴Γ is normal.
Given a probability measure µ ∈Prob(Γ), a µ-Stationary Random Subgroup
(SRS) is a Borel probability measure ν ∈Prob(SubΓ) which is stationary with
respect to µ and the conjugation action. Any IRS is an example of an SRS, but
there are many more SRSs than IRSs.

Ends of graphs and random subgroups
The space of ends of a locally finite graph G, denoted by Ends(G), formalizes the
“ways to go to infinity” in the graph. It is constructed as the inverse limit of the
connected components of the complement of finite balls. This construction en-
dows Ends(G) with a compact topological structure (see for example, [Kr¨o05]).
The classical Freudenthal-Hopf [Fre44; Hop44] theorem dictates that the
number of ends of a finitely generated Cayley graph is always 0, 1, 2, or 2ℵ0.
Abels [Abe73] later generalized this result, proving that the same holds for any
quasi-transitive graph.
One gets a measurable function Ends : SubΓ →N ∪{0, ∞} assigning H
the cardinality of Ends(Sch(H\Γ, S)). Because conjugating H merely re-roots
the graph without altering its global end structure, this function is Γ-invariant.
Consequently, for any ergodic measure on SubΓ, the number of ends is almost
surely constant.
For normal subgroups (whose Schreier graphs are Cayley graphs) and sub-
groups with finitely many conjugates (which yield quasi-transitive Schreier graphs),
the Freudenthal-Hopf and Abels theorems directly guarantee that the number
of ends is 0, 1, 2, or 2ℵ0.
Lyons and Schramm [LS99] extended this result by showing that any uni-
modular random graph almost surely has 0, 1, 2, or 2ℵ0 ends. Since the Schreier
graph of an Invariant Random Subgroup (IRS) inherently corresponds to a uni-
modular random graph [BT16], it follows that any IRS almost surely satisfies
this same restriction on its ends. For an analogous generalization to unimodular
random manifolds, see [BR17; AB22].

Core and stationary actions
In [AB22, Theorem 1.15], the authors introduce the notion of a core for a
random manifold and formulate the “No-Core Principle” which characterizes
the conditions under which a core may or may not exist.
Drawing inspiration from their work, we generalize the definition of a core to
arbitrary Γ-spaces as follows.
Definition 3.2 (Core). Let (X, ν) be a probability Γ-space. A Borel subset
C ⊆X is called a Core of the action if for ν a.e. x ∈X,
|Γ.x ∩C| < ∞
In words, a Core is a way to select from each orbit a finite set, in a Borel fashion.
For example, if every orbit is finite, then every measurable set is a core.
More interestingly, consider a Borel selector. Namely, let (X, m) be a probability
measure preserving action of Z, and let C be a Borel set that intersects every
Z exactly at one point. It is easy to see that by (the classical) Kac’s formula,
in that case, m-almost every orbit must be finite.
We think of this setup, when the orbits are finite, as trivial. To get a non-
trivial example of an action with a core, one can consider a transitive Γ-action
(for some Γ) on an infinite (necessarily countable) set X, equipped with a fully
supported measure η ∈Prob(X). In that case, every finite subset is a core,
although the orbit is infinite.
Our main theorem states that for stationary actions, the only case in which
there is a (non-trivial) core, it is due to a trivial reason: finite orbits.
Theorem 3.3 (No-core principle for stationary actions). Let µ ∈Prob(Γ) be a
generating measure, and let (Γ, µ) ↷(X, ν) be a stationary action. Let C ⊆X
be a core with positive measure. Then for ν-a.e. x ∈C, |Γx| < ∞.
In words, almost every point in a core has a finite orbit.

The Boomerang counter example
A Boomerang subgroup, as introduced in [GL25] is a subgroup ∆< Γ such that
for every g ∈Γ the sequence (gn∆g−n)∞
n=0 has a subsequence which converges
to ∆. If λ ∈Prob(SubΓ) is an IRS, then λ-a.e. subgroup is Boomerang.
That is, Boomerang is a topological property (in SubΓ), it is “the area in
which IRSs are happening”. Hence, classifying the Boomerang subgroups, yields
a classification of the possible IRSs, bypassing the very nature of an IRS as a
probabilistic object.
It is therefore natural to ask whether IRS end-rigidity
(the IRS version of Theorem 1.2) can be proved by first proving a pointwise
statement for all Boomerang subgroups. The following example shows that this
strategy cannot work.
