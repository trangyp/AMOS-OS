ABSTRACT
Context. Cosmic rays (CRs) play a key role in the interstellar medium (ISM) by regulating the chemical evolution of the gas and its
coupling to the magnetic field in the densest and coldest regions of molecular clouds. However, the CR ionisation rate of H2 (ζ2) is
one of the most debated parameters characterising molecular clouds due to the uncertainties in its estimation.
Aims. We aim to derive a new and reliable indirect analytical method to probe the electron fraction, x(e), and ζ2 of the gas across
multiple density regimes. We further apply this novel framework to a parsec-scale filament to homogeneously map a significant sample
of fields with different physical conditions and test their impact on x(e) and ζ2.
Methods. Recent estimates of x(e) and ζ2 suffer from limitations, mostly driven by observational restrictions. We thus developed a new
analytical framework based on the chemistry of N2H+, N2D+ and DCO+. We mapped the emission from their ground transition towards
the parsec-scale filament of the infrared dark cloud (IRDC) G035.39-00.33 with new observations from the NOrthern Extended Array
(NOEMA) at a resolution of 3′′ (or ∼9000 au at the cloud distance). By combining this novel survey with ancillary observations of
C18O (1 −0), we determined the CO depletion factor, the N2H+ deuterium fraction and, ultimately, x(e) and ζ2 in G035.39-00.33.
Results. The CO depletion is significant and widespread in G035.39-00.33. Its depletion factor, fD, shows a positive correlation with
column and number densities of H2 in the cloud, with the highest intensities in both N2H+ and N2D+, and with the sites displaying
an enhanced deuterium fraction (RD). The electron fraction varies by two orders of magnitude within ∼10−9 −10−7 and shows a
functional dependence on the number density of H2 in the region. This correlation is consistent with that determined in low-mass
cores with similar degrees of CO depletion, but now extended to parsec scales, and with the predictions from chemical models. ζ2
varies by three orders of magnitude within ∼10−18 −10−15 s−1, and most fields show values of ∼2.3 × 10−18 s−1 on average. ζ2 shows
a functional dependence on N(H2), as predicted by theoretical models, but its scatter and its values, on average lower than the typical
ζ2 for the ISM, suggest the presence of local enhancement and overall attenuation of the CR flux taking place in G035.39-00.33.
Conclusions. fD and RD show values consistent with theoretical predictions and previous independent studies. On the other hand,
x(e) and ζ2 show values at the lower end compared to independent estimates in filaments and cores. The values of ζ2 are consistent
with those reported for other IRDCs and giant filaments, but lower compared to theoretical predictions for the column density regime
sampled by G035.39-00.33. CR attenuation can come from the change in magnetic field strength and morphology previously reported
in G035.39-00.33, which explains the reduced values of x(e) and ζ2. Fields with ionisation rates of ζ2 ≳10−16 s−1 may be affected by
the local acceleration of CRs or by the magnetic pockets formed from the uneven magnetic field instead.

1. Introduction
Cosmic rays (CRs) are charged accelerated particles that play
a key role in the evolution of the interstellar medium (ISM)
(Padovani et al. 2020). At densities n ≳103 cm−3, low-energy
CRs (E < 10 GeV) mostly interact with H2, causing its ioni-
sation at a rate ζ2. This ionisation rate, in turn, triggers many
processes within molecular clouds. First, H+
2 rapidly forms H+
3
(Herbst & Klemperer 1973), initiating ion-neutral chemistry in
dense clouds and allowing the formation of fundamental ions
and complex molecules (Herbst & van Dishoeck 2009). Recom-
bination of these ions with electrons and neutrals is the main
source of heating in molecular clouds and filaments (Glassgold
et al. 2012), thus setting the thermal pressure that confines the

dense cores embedded within them (Goldsmith 2001). The bal-
ance between ions and neutrals, usually expressed via the elec-
tron fraction x(e) = n(e)/n (with n(e) and n number densities
of electrons and total gas, respectively), influences the coupling
between the gas and magnetic field (McKee 1989) with the re-
sulting magnetic pressure potentially slowing the gravitational
collapse of individual cores (Shu et al. 1987). Ultimately, CRs
play a pivotal role in shaping the initial conditions for star for-
mation through their ionisation rate ζ2.
Measuring ζ2 is however complex. Its most direct probe is
the abundance of H+
3. This species has several vibrational transi-
tions in the infrared (IR) (Oka 1980), where detections have been
reported since the 1990s (see Oka et al. 2019). As a homonuclear
and symmetric molecule, H+
3 lacks rotational transitions in the
millimetre regime (mm), where emission from the cold gas of
molecular clouds peaks instead. The elusiveness of H+
3 in these
regions has so far prevented direct and accurate measurements
of ζ2 towards the sites of star formation.
Proxies were therefore used over the years to determine ζ2
in the ISM. Early studies using radicals (Black & Dalgarno
1977), neutrals (van Dishoeck & Black 1986), and ions (van
der Tak & van Dishoeck 2000) reported a homogeneous value
of ∼10−17 s−1 across the ISM, consistent with the theoreti-
cal prediction based on the CR spectrum (Spitzer & Tomasko
1968; Webber 1998). During the past 30 years, studies in dense
cores within low-mass clouds (Caselli et al. 1998), intermediate-
mass envelopes (e.g. Ceccarelli et al. 2014; Fontani et al. 2017),
and high-mass regions (e.g. Luo et al. 2024) reported ionisa-
tion rates varying by up to four orders of magnitude (ζ2 ∼
10−18−10−14 s−1). Inhomogeneous sampling of ζ2 from scales of
∼0.1 pc (Redaelli et al. 2021) up to ∼1 pc (Socci et al. 2024b)
with tracers sensitive to selected environments (evolved vs pre-
stellar; Luo et al. 2024; Bovino et al. 2020, respectively) made
the link of ζ2 to the physical (e.g. density) and environmental
(e.g. feedback) properties of the region more complex.
Recently, two methods proved reliable in mapping ζ2 across
scales. The first method, proposed by Bovino et al. (2020), is
based on the observations of H2D+, the product of the first
deuteration of H+
3 (Watson 1974). Together with observations
of DCO+, HCO+, and CO, this method allowed Sabatini et al.
(2023) to probe ζ2 in two massive clumps at scales of ∼0.005 −
0.1 pc. The second method, proposed by Caselli et al. (1998),
uses observations of DCO+, HCO+, and CO, plus assumptions
on the abundances of species that cannot be observed in the
dense gas (e.g. O, HD). This method allowed Pineda et al.
(2024) to probe ζ2 in a low-mass proto-cluster on scales of
∼0.01 −0.5 pc. Both studies reported a variation of ζ2 in the
regions related either to a change in the column density of H2,
N(H2), or to the production of CRs from local sources (i.e. pro-
tostars), in agreement with predictions from theoretical models
(Padovani et al. 2009, 2016, respectively).
Despite connecting the large dynamic range in ζ2 to the
properties of the region in a self-consistent way, these methods
still suffer from limitations, mostly related to observations (see
Redaelli et al. 2024, for a full discussion on the two methods).
The method of Bovino et al. (2020) depends on the abundance
of H2D+ whose ortho spin state is the only one with a transi-
tion in the millimetre regime (at ∼372 GHz). Comprising only
a fraction of its total (ortho and para) abundance, ortho-H2D+ is
rarely extended in clouds and its detection becomes challenging
on scales ≳0.2 pc. The method of Caselli et al. (1998) depends
on the number density of H2, n(H2), which is complex to mea-
sure in clouds. A poorly estimated n(H2) could cause ζ2 to in-
crease for increasing density, in contradiction with the theory of
CR propagation in a progressively denser medium. Finally, both
methods require an estimate of the column density of HCO+,
which is often hampered by the optical thickness of its transi-
tions, especially of its ground state (e.g. Vasyunina et al. 2012).
Our aim in this work is to minimise these limitations with a
new analytical estimate of ζ2 based on the abundance of N2H+
and N2D+. We will then test this framework at parsec scales
in an infrared dark cloud (IRDC). IRDCs were first identified
as dark objects that obscure IR stellar light across the Galactic
Plane at wavelengths from ∼10 µm to 100 µm (Perault et al.
1996). Systematic surveys confirm IRDCs as extended (sizes
≳5 pc) and massive (≳5000 M⊙) objects with average densities
∼2×103 cm−3 (Simon et al. 2006a,b). High-mass star-formation
is often hosted in IRDCs (e.g. Peretto et al. 2013), of which the
massive (≳100 M⊙) and compact (≲0.7 pc) IR-dark clumps
(Ragan et al. 2006) act as potential precursors (Rathborne et al.
2006). Previous works in IRDCs (Entekhabi et al. 2022) and
other Galactic filaments (Clarke et al. 2024) reported values of
ζ2 ≲4 × 10−18 s−1. These ionisation rates, reduced compared to
other estimates and theoretical predictions (see Padovani et al.
2024, and references therein), hinted towards an attenuation of
the CR flux due to the density regime of the region or its Galac-
tocentric distance.
Our target, G035.39-00.33 (Cloud H hereafter, see Butler &
Tan 2009, 2012) is a massive IRDC (∼1.7 × 104 M⊙) located
at a heliocentric distance of 2.9 kpc (Simon et al. 2006b). Part
of the W48 molecular complex (see Nguyen Luong et al. 2011;
Cosentino et al. 2025, and references therein), Cloud H appears
as a narrow ridge with extinctions AV ≳25 mag (Kainulainen &
Tan 2013) engulfed in the feedback from nearby high-mass star-
formation sites (see Fig. 1 and also Liu et al. 2026). This filamen-
tary cloud hosts several clumps and cores with masses ≳60 M⊙
(Rathborne et al. 2006) and up to ∼30 M⊙(Butler & Tan 2012),
respectively, that are potential precursors of stellar clusters. Re-
cent observing campaigns towards Cloud H at low (≳20′′) and
high (≲5′′) resolution targeted the dense gas morphology and
structure, as probed by N2H+, of its northern filament (Henshaw
et al. 2014, 2016; Barnes et al. 2018, 2021). These works re-
vealed a complex sub-structure of filaments, fibers, and cores
directly connected to the star-formation sites within the fields
(Carey et al. 2009; Jiménez-Serra et al. 2010; Nguyen Luong
et al. 2011). Finally, Cloud H exhibits significant degrees of CO
depletion (with depletion factors fD ≳4; Hernandez et al. 2011;
Jiménez-Serra et al. 2014) and high deuterium fractions (probed
as RD =
N2D+
N2H+ ∼0.04) on scales ≳1 pc (Barnes et al. 2016).
These properties highlight Cloud H as an ideal target for deter-
mining ζ2 at parsec scales with our new analytical framework.
The paper is structured as follows: we introduce the method
and the main assumptions behind its application (Sect. 2); we
then demonstrate its reliability and limits of application via
a direct comparison with the solution from chemical models
(Sect. 3); we next introduce the observations, both novel and
archival, used in the work (Sect. 4); we continue by present-
ing the analysis performed and its main results concerning the
spatial distribution of the tracers, the determination of the col-
umn densities, the degree of depletion, and the deuterium frac-
tion (Sect. 5); finally, we discuss the electron fraction and the
cosmic-ray ionisation rate (CRIR) determined, their correlation
with number and column density, respectively, plus their rela-
tion to the magnetic field orientation and strength in Cloud H
(Sect. 6). Section 7 summarises the main findings of this work
and our conclusions.

2. Analytical formulae
N2H+, HCO+ and N2D+, DCO+ are the direct products of pro-
tonation and deuteration of N2 and CO, respectively. The main
pathways of formation for these species involve H+
3 and its D-
bearing isotopologues (i.e. H2D+, D2H+, D+
3). The properties
of these ions and of their precursors, however, deeply influence
the gas regime they probe. First, N2 formation in the gas phase
is slower than that of CO (Aikawa et al. 2005) and its freeze-
out onto dust grains occurs at higher number densities (a few
∼105 cm−3; Caselli et al. 2022) compared to the latter species
(n ≳104 cm−3; Bergin & Tafalla 2007). Second, the formation
of N2D+ is hindered at temperatures TK ≳20 K due to decrease
of the H+
3 isotopologues in the gas phase (Gerlich et al. 2002),
while the formation of DCO+ can still occur via CH2D+ (Roueff
et al. 2007; Parise et al. 2009; Öberg et al. 2012). Third, the effec-
tive critical densities of N2H+ and N2D+ are higher than those of
HCO+ and DCO+ for the same transition (Shirley 2015). Finally,
the nuclear spin bore by the nitrogen atoms in N2H+ and N2D+
causes the splitting of their spectral lines into multiple hyperfine
components (Turner 1974). Upon assumption of local thermody-
namic equilibrium (LTE), the total intensity of the line, as well as
its opacity, is then divided among these components via their sta-
tistical weights (although excitation anomalies may exist; Caselli
et al. 1995). Then it is common to find at least one isolated op-
tically thin hyperfine line from which to infer the total intensity
(e.g. Chen et al. 2019), in contrast to the often optically thick
HCO+ emission (e.g. Vasyunina et al. 2012). These properties
allow N2H+ and N2D+ to sample efficiently the cold and dense
gas in molecular clouds (i.e. T ≲20 K, n ≳104 cm−3; Bergin &
Tafalla 2007) and make their deuterium fraction RD = N(N2D+)
N(N2H+) a
more suitable probe of this gas regime compared to its N(DCO+)
N(HCO+)
counterpart (e.g. Crapsi et al. 2005).

3. Comparison with chemical models
We tested the reliability and range of applicability of our ana-
lytical formulation against the chemical modelling provided by
pyRate (Sipilä et al. 2015). pyRate is a 0-D chemical soft-
ware that constructs and solves a system of ODEs across a de-
fined time range, initial elemental abundances, and initial phys-
ical properties. The initial elemental abundances, as well as the
chemical network, that we use as input are those from Sipilä
et al. (2022). The latter comprises prescriptions for the spin-
state chemistry, a multi-layered ice chemistry, gas-grain chem-
istry, plus detailed rate coefficients for the H+
3 + H2 system (see
Sipilä et al. 2016, 2017; Sipilä & Caselli 2018). We will now
briefly discuss the reasoning behind the choice of the physical
parameters used to run our realisations with pyRate.

4.2. Auxiliary observations
In addition to our suite of ions, Eq. 8 requires an estimate of
other parameters: the total column (and number) density of H2,
N(H2) (n(H2)), the degree of CO depletion, expressed through
the depletion factor fD and the kinetic temperature TK. Thanks
to the wealth of data available for Cloud H, we can estimate these
parameters with ancillary observations from various facilities.
Figure B.1 (left) shows the total column density of H2 to-
wards the northern filament of Cloud H. The map was obtained
from a cross calibration of the NIR (UKIDSS/GPS photometry
in the JHK bands; Lucas et al. 2008; Kainulainen et al. 2011)
and MIR (8 µm data from Spitzer/GLIMPSE; Benjamin et al.
2003; Butler & Tan 2012) dust extinction measurements at a na-
tive resolution of ∼2′′. We refer the reader to Kainulainen &
Tan (2013) for a full description of the technique and to Sect. B
for a description of the filament structure as seen in dust extinc-
tion. We briefly note a few caveats to the technique: the MIR
data saturates at high column densities, thus becoming unreli-
able at extinctions ∼90 −115 AV (Butler & Tan 2012); a col-
umn density map based on dust extinction is not applicable in
regions containing localised IR sources, which appear as regions
of artificially low (or unconstrained) values of N(H2) (Butler &
Tan 2009); combining the two IR datasets requires knowledge of
the dust opacity law, for which the authors report an uncertainty
to the method of 36% (Kainulainen & Tan 2013). The first two
limiting factors have a mild impact on our analysis as the highest
extinction values in the region are ∼70 AV and most bright IR
sources reside outside the filament spine (see Fig. B.1, left panel;
Kainulainen & Tan 2013). The original map, obtained in units of
mass surface density (Σ), was converted to molecules of H2 by
assuming solar abundances of the elements (Asplund et al. 2021)
and dividing by µH2mp, with µH2 = 2.81 mean particle mass per
H2 and mp mass of the proton.
Figure B.1 (centre) shows the number density of H2 towards
the northern filament of Cloud H. The map was obtained by ap-
plying a denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) to the
original surface density map of the region. We refer the reader
to (Xu et al. 2023) for a full account on the implementation of
the technique to this dataset and to Sect. B for a description of
the filament structure in number density. We discuss here a few
limitations of the technique: the DDPM retains the resolution
of the original map (i.e. ∼2′′), but it is similarly deficient to-
wards the IR-bright sources; comparisons with full magnetohy-
drodynamic (MHD) simulations reveal that the DDPM underes-
timates by ∼10% the expected density structure of the cloud
with a ∼38% deviation around its mean value. The map, ini-
tially reconstructed as total number density, n = nH + 2n(H2),
was converted in density of H2 by assuming a negligible fraction
of atomic hydrogen (i.e. n ∼2n(H2)).
Figure 3 (panel d) shows the velocity-integrated intensity of
C18O (1−0) towards the northern filament of Cloud H. The ob-
servations were performed on the 29th of March 2022 (PI: J.
Tan) using the 16-element heterodyne radio camera Argus (Sieth
et al. 2014) mounted on the Green Bank Telescope (GBT). The
map was obtained using the fast-mapping technique by scanning
the pixel receivers along Galactic longitude in rows sampled ev-
ery 5.58′′. After the baseline removal and re-gridding, the final
C18O (1−0) spectral cube has a pixel scale of 2.57′′ and a spec-
tral resolution of 0.182 km s−1. The rms noise level, determined
across channels without emission, is 0.55 K with an absolute
flux calibration error of 15%. We refer the reader to Law et al.
(2024) for a full account on the data acquisition and reduction
(performed in G28.37+0.07, or Cloud C, Butler & Tan 2009, but
with the same observational setup and calibration as for Cloud
H), while the C18O emission is further compared to that of the
ions in the next section.
Figure B.1 (right) shows the kinetic temperature map to-
wards the northern filament of Cloud H. The map was obtained
with observations from the Very Large Array (VLA) of the
first two inversion transitions of ammonia, NH3 (1,1) and (2,2)
(Sokolov et al. 2018). These data were further combined with an-
cillary GBT observations of the same transitions (Sokolov et al.
2017) to recover the total flux and achieve a final resolution of
5.44′′. The kinematics of Cloud H is complex, and its structure
comprises multiple velocity components along the line of sight
(e.g. Henshaw et al. 2013). Our kinetic temperature map is de-
rived from the modelling of the emission from NH3 (1,1) and
(2,2) towards the velocity-component F2 (Sokolov et al. 2019),
which encompasses the full coverage of our NOEMA+IRAM-
30m map. We refer to Sokolov et al. (2019) for details on the
analysis of the ammonia transitions and to Sect. B for a descrip-
tion of the temperature variations across the filament.
These ancillary observations provide the final ingredients for
measuring the electron fraction and CRIR throughout the cloud.
To achieve a more direct comparison and given the similar res-
olution among all observations, these ancillary maps were re-
gridded to the same resolution and pixel size of our NOEMA
maps. In the next section, we will make use of these observa-
tions to study the link between our molecular tracers, the main
parameters derived from their analysis, and their contribution in
Eqs. 3 and 8, along with those of density and temperature.
5. Physical properties of Cloud H
Employing the observations just described, we compute the
CRIR throughout Cloud H using Eq. 8. The parameters needed
to perform this calculation are described as follows: first, we
discuss the spatial morphology of our suite of molecular tracers
(Sect. 5.1); second, we use the integrated intensity maps of our
tracers to determine their column densities under a few assump-
tions (Sect. 5.2); third, we determine the CO depletion factor, fD,
from the C18O column density (Sect. 5.3), the deuterium frac-
tion, RD, from the N2D+ and N2H+ column densities (Sect. 5.4),
and discuss their values in the context of the cloud; finally, we
compute the electron fraction, x(e) (Sect. 6.2), and the CRIR, ζ2
(Sect. 6.3), using Eqs. 3, and 8, respectively.

DCO+ show emission peaks often anti-correlated to those of
N2H+ and within themselves as well. These spatial changes in
emission could imply a shift in the dominant deuteration process
that occurs within the cloud (see Sect. 2) due to external factors,
such as temperature, density, and the CRIR.

5.4. Influences on the deuterium fraction in Cloud H
The deuterium fraction, calculated as RD = N(N2D+)/N(N2H+),
shows values ranging two orders of magnitude throughout Cloud
H (see Fig. 4, panel b). Values within ∼0.0015−0.15 with a cor-

−0.02 are consistent
with those reported in independent studies of other IRDCs (Mi-
ettinen et al. 2011; Gerner et al. 2015). These values are also
consistent with those reported in low-mass cores with a simi-

2005). Simulations of turbulent magnetised filaments, such as
Cloud H (Henshaw et al. 2014; Liu et al. 2018), report deuterium
fractions of ∼0.01 −0.1 on timescales of ≲0.4 Myr (Körtgen
et al. 2018), relatively consistent with our findings. Nevertheless,
our values of RD remain a factor of a few below those reported
in high-mass starless cores (i.e. RD ≳0.3 Fontani et al. 2011;
Kong et al. 2015), which is potentially related to the different
conditions of those cores compared to Cloud H.
The wide range of deuterium fractions derived in Cloud H
is unevenly distributed on the map. Although most of the val-
ues along the filament spine cluster around RD ∼0.1, three main
features emerge: a peak towards the North, a peak towards the
South, and a dip at the centre. The two peaks show values of
≳0.1 extended over sizes of ∼0.1 −0.3 pc and corresponding to
depletion factors around ≳7. These high deuterium fractions are
co-spatial with the peaks of N2D+ emission and are consistent
with the values previously reported at lower resolution in the re-
gion (Barnes et al. 2016). The dip is centred on the point source
with the MIR counterpart located SE of the MM7 clump (red
diamond; Gutermuth & Heyer 2015). Here, despite the presence
of depletion factors fD ∼10, the deuterium fraction rapidly de-
creases from ∼0.1 to ≲0.05 and below. This rapid drop, milder
in Barnes et al. (2016) due to the lower resolution of the obser-
vations (i.e. 27′′), is likely related to the more evolved stage of
the point source (see Fig. B.1, right).
Deuterium fractions are sensitive to temperature variations
as deuteration processes have reaction rates which vary signifi-
cantly with temperature (Watson 1974). The formation of N2D+
from N2 is quite affected by this effect, especially for TK ≳20 K
(e.g. Fontani et al. 2011; Pazukhin et al. 2023). Thus, the afore-
mentioned drop in deuterium fraction towards the centre of the
map may be consistent with the increase in temperature (above
≳15 K) seen towards the edges of the MM7 clump (see Fig. B.1,
right panel). The presence of multiple point sources, among
which the more evolved one (red diamond), provides a source
of gas heating and reinforces the picture of RD decreasing from
young and starless regions to more star-forming ones reported
by previous authors (Emprechtinger et al. 2009; Fontani et al.
2011; Gerner et al. 2015; Sabatini et al. 2024).
6. Electron fraction and cosmic-ray ionisation rate
In the last section, we obtained all the parameters required to
determine x(e) and ζ2 in Cloud H. Now, we will discuss their
estimated values as follows: first, we will present the typical
values retrieved in Cloud H and their corresponding degree of
accuracy (Sect. 6.1); second, we will outline the typical val-
ues and distribution of x(e) (Sect. 6.2) and ζ2 (Sect. 6.3) across
the filament; then, we will address the dependence of x(e) on
n(H2) (Sect. 6.4); finally, we will discuss the attenuation of ζ2
with N(H2) in comparison with the theoretical predictions for
the propagation of CRs in the cloud (Sect. 6.5).
6.1. Analytical estimates and error budget
Equations 3 and 8 allow us to estimate x(e) and ζ2 with a nominal
accuracy of a factor of 3 associated to the method. Many of the
parameters embedded in this calculation, however, bear uncer-
tainties: the flux calibration in our observations (Sect. 4.1); the
systematics in the methods to derive N(H2) and n(H2) (Sect. 4.2);
the errors in Galactocentric distance of the Sun and in the Galac-
tic gradients (Sect. 5.3). The propagation of these uncertainties
can easily grow above the measured values for x(e) and ζ2. Given
that both quantities are definite positive (i.e. with 0 as a lower
bound), this propagation is not reasonable nor accurate as it as-
sumed an underlying Gaussian distribution for the two param-
eters. We therefore assume x(e) and ζ2 to follow a log-normal
distribution, propagate their errors accordingly and report their
median values and prediction intervals.

6.2. Low electron fractions across the filament
The electron fraction, x(e), shows a variation mostly confined
within two orders of magnitude throughout Cloud H (Fig. 4,
panel d). The morphology of its distribution closely follows
that of RD and fD: those regions where RD ≳0.1 and fD ≳7
show electron fractions x(e) ≲10−8, even down to x(e) ≲10−9
when the depletion factors reach ∼10. A clear example is MM7
where most values are within x(e) ∼10−9 −10−8, which agrees
well with the number density reaching its highest values (i.e.
n ≳2 × 104 cm−3; McKee 1989). Temperature seems to play
a secondary role in the variation of the electron fraction behind
RD and fD: its values ∼20 K towards MM7 have only a marginal
influence over x(e), while values up to ∼10−7 are reported in the
colder (∼10−15 K), but still dense (n ∼104 cm−3) northern and
southern parts of the filament.
The wide variety of conditions covered in Cloud H makes
x(e) vary within (0.002−8)×10−7 around its median of 1×10−8.
These electron fractions are lower, or marginally consistent on
average, than those estimated using analytical methods in cold
clouds (∼1 −3 × 10−6; Dalgarno & Lepp 1984), low-mass cores
(∼10−8 −10−6; Caselli et al. 1998), sub-parsec molecular cloud
cores (∼0.5−2×10−8; Anderson et al. 1999), and sub-parsec fila-
ments (∼0.3−30×10−7; Pineda et al. 2024). Chemical modelling
of the emission from various molecular tracers, both in steady
state (Schilke et al. 1991; de Boisanger et al. 1996; Williams
et al. 1998) and with a time dependence (Shalabiea & Greenberg
1995), instead returns electron fractions in a better agreement
with our results, with averages of ∼10−8 and lower to upper
limits of ∼2 × 10−9 and ∼2 × 10−7, respectively.
The large variation in values may come from the different
mass regimes of the sources and their degree of irradiation.
Other works relying on the chemical modelling of tracers in in-
dividual sources highlight these influences: low-mass pre-stellar
cores exhibit electron fractions within ∼10−9 −10−8 (Caselli
et al. 2002; Maret & Bergin 2007); high-mass cores hosting or
with neighbouring protostars exhibit electron fractions within
∼0.5 −1.5 × 10−7 (Bergin et al. 1999). The majority of Cloud
H shows x(e) ≲10−8, consistent with the low-mass pre-stellar
cores and the almost absence of point sources along the fila-
ment spine; nevertheless, several pixels reach electron fractions
x(e) ≳0.5 × 10−7, suggesting that either some of these neigh-
bouring point sources and protostars or the magnetic field mor-
phology may influence the ion budget.
6.3. Cosmic-ray ionisation rate in Cloud H
An ionisation rate of ζ2 ∼10−17 s−1 is still often considered
the standard value for CRs propagating throughout the ISM (see
discussion in Oka 2019). However, in the last 30 years, mul-
tiple studies reported systematic variations of ζ2 in several ob-
jects. Caselli et al. (1998) and Redaelli et al. (2025) determined
ionisation rates within ∼10−18 −10−16 s−1 in samples of low-
mass cores, skewed more towards values of ∼10−17 −10−16 s−1.
Studies of individual pre-stellar cores, high-mass envelopes and
high-mass star-forming regions returned a similar order of mag-
nitude (Maret & Bergin 2007; Ivlev et al. 2019; Sabatini et al.
2020; Redaelli et al. 2021; Sabatini et al. 2023). Finally, values
above ∼10−16 s−1 (Podio et al. 2014), and up to 10−14 s−1 (Cec-
carelli et al. 2014; Fontani et al. 2017; Cabedo et al. 2023), were
reported only in regions with sources capable of locally accel-
erating CRs (i.e. protostars and outflows; Padovani et al. 2015,
2016). Despite the large scatter in estimates, these works demon-
strated that local conditions in the region can influence the stan-
dard ionisation rate from Galactic CRs.
The same wide range of ionisation conditions applies within
Cloud H. The CRIR shows values of ∼(0.004 −4) × 10−16 s−1
around its median of 2.3 × 10−18 s−1 throughout the cloud (see
Fig. 4, panel d). This variation of three orders of magnitude
in ζ2 mirrors that of x(e): the central region comprising the
MM7 clump shows the lowest degree of ionisation with val-
ues below ≲10−18 s−1, even lower than those reported in other
massive clumps; the regions bright in N2D+ (i.e. I(N2D+) ≳
1.5 K km s−1; see Fig. 3, central panel), besides MM7, exhibit
values a factor of a few above ≳10−18 s−1, closer to those of
massive clumps and low-mass cores; the northern and southern
part of the filament, where x(e) ∼10−7, reach instead values up
and above ∼10−17 s−1: the southern portion is hosts a few out-
flows powered by the point sources and protostars, as hinted by
the broad SiO lines detected in those sub-regions (Jiménez-Serra
et al. 2010; Liu et al. 2026); the northern portion may be affected
by a change in magnetic field morphology and strength instead
(Liu et al. 2018). Our results in Cloud H demonstrate that parsec-
scale filaments are optimal regions to study a variety of different
ionisation conditions.
Variations of ζ2 within a single region were recently stud-
ied in other sub-parsec- and parsec-scale filaments. Pineda et al.
(2024) determined a median value for the CRIR of ζ2 ∼3 ×
10−17 s−1 in the low-mass NGC 1333 SW filament in Perseus
(see e.g. Walawender et al. 2008) with rates growing to ∼
10−16 s−1 towards protostars in the fields. Socci et al. (2024b) re-
ported analogous results towards the massive filament that hosts
the Orion molecular clouds 2 and 3 (OMC-2/-3; see e.g. Peterson
& Megeath 2008). Despite the common enhancement in ζ2 seen
towards the local sources across the three regions, NGC 1333
SW and OMC-2/-3 still show ionisation rates an order of magni-
tude higher than those in Cloud H on average. This discrepancy
suggests that either the ambient flux of CRs in the former two

this study. From this premise and a few assumptions, McKee

make the former interpretation not applicable to Cloud H. Within
the latter instead, column densities in Cloud H do not appear to
drive its low values of ζ2 according to the CR propagation mod-
els, as shown in Fig. 7. We thus believe that the magnetic field
plays a pivotal role in the attenuation of the CRIR.
Liu et al. (2018) studied the magnetic field strength and mor-
phology in Cloud H via observations of the dust polarisation.
They reported several properties that can affect the CR propaga-
tion and, thus, ionisation: the magnetic field orientation, with re-
spect to the filament spine, changes from mostly parallel towards
the North (above the red line in Fig. 4, panel d) to mostly perpen-
dicular towards the South (below the red line), with the expected
dominant components being poloidal and toroidal, respectively;
the magnetic field strength varies from ∼65 µG throughout the
filament to ∼90 −140 µG towards the dense clumps in the re-
gion (from North to South, c3 and c4 in Liu et al. 2018, see
gold crosses and circles in Fig. 4, panel d). Along converging
field lines with a monotonically increasing magnetic field in-
tensity, the focusing and mirroring of the CR pitch angles is
balanced. This means that ζ2 can be calculated as if the field
were constant, irrespective of its morphology (Padovani et al.
2020). When magnetic field minima and maxima are alternated,
magnetic pockets may form and favour mirroring over focusing,
thus reducing the CR ionisation by up to an order of magnitude
towards the regions with a strong toroidal component (Silsbee
et al. 2018). We therefore argue that the values of ζ2 within
(0.5 −4) × 10−18 s−1 towards MM7 are a consequence of the
uneven magnetic field strength throughout Cloud H, which may
form magnetic pockets in the inter-clump filament.
CRIR values above ≳10−16 s−1 are potentially affected by lo-
cal sources instead. These estimates of ζ2 cross models H and
U , which were adapted to the ionisation rates in diffuse clouds,
and lack any correlation with both N(H2) and TK. Both argu-
ments support an enhancement of the local CR flux: given the
average temperatures of these fields (within ∼10 −15 K) and
their position in the inter-clump parts of the filament (see Fig. 4,
panel d), these high values of ζ2 may reflect the presence of CRs
mirrored back from the clumps towards the filament, plus their
acceleration from the large-scale supernova remnant or local out-
flows (Liu et al. 2026).
7. Conclusions
We studied the electron fraction and CRIR at parsec scales across
the filamentary IRDC G035.39-00.33 (Cloud H) using a newly
developed analytical framework. We derived these two parame-
ters using a new set of NOEMA+30m observations for our main
suite of tracers, N2H+, N2D+, and DCO+ (1−0). These were
paired with ancillary observations of C18O (1−0), plus estimates
of the kinetic temperature of the gas, its column and number
density of H2 from several facilities, all re-gridded to a common
resolution of 3′′ (or ∼0.04 pc) and pixel size of 1′′. Our analysis

range of physical properties offered by Cloud H. It challenges
other analytical methods (Caselli et al. 1998; Bovino et al. 2020;

Equations 3 and 8 comprise several parameters that we can
probe, described in Sect. 5, but some we cannot probe as well.
For the latter, we will assume typical abundances with respect to
H2 reported in the literature and discuss their potential depletion
in relation to that of CO.
HD and D2, have abundances of 3.2 × 10−5 and 4 × 10−6, re-

H+

et al. 2004). The abundances of atomic nitrogen (N) and oxygen
(O), with respect to H, were reported as 7.5 × 10−5 (Meyer et al.
1997) and 3.25 × 10−4 (Meyer et al. 1998) from spectrographic
measurements towards several sight lines. Later estimates and
effective freeze-out onto dust grains (e.g. Caux et al. 1999; Vas-

abundances with respect to H2 hereafter, starting from N2 at
x(N2) = 7.56 × 10−5 (Semenov et al. 2010). For atomic O, the
choice is complex, as this species is progressively locked in CO
and H2O. CO locks around x(CO) ∼10−4 of this oxygen, while
H2O shows variations in abundance within ∼(0.001 −1) × 10−4
(van Dishoeck et al. 2021). The corresponding variation in the

value of x(O) = 2 × 10−4, although a variation of up to a factor

Appendix A.4: Cross-check between assumed and modelled
abundances
We finally tested the reliability of our assumed abundances when
compared to those derived from PyRate. The outputs consid-
ered are those computed at t = 106 yr given the steady-state
assumption made when deriving the analytical formulae (see
Sect. 2). Figure A.1 shows the abundances of HD, D2, O, N2,
and g−as derived from the chemical model (dashed lines) and
assumed in our analytic approach (solid lines4).
The abundances of HD, D2, O and N2 from the model may
be overestimated by more than an one order of magnitude com-
pared by those we assumed for values below ≲6×103 cm−3 and
above ≲105 cm−3. The former regime is dominated by the unat-
tenuated, or marginally attenuated, UV flux, which causes the
molecular species to disappear from the gas (HD, D2, N2) while
the atomic species remain abundant due to the absence of deple-
tion. The latter regime sees depletion affecting the abundances
of N2 and O instead, while those of HD and D2, reduced by the
deuteration of ions, are replenished by D and become progres-
sively constant (Flower et al. 2004). For number densities within
∼6×103−105 cm−3, the abundances of HD, D2, O and N2 we as-
sumed are in closer agreement with those from the model within
a factor of ≲9. This discrepancy is further reduced to a factor
of ∼3 when considering the typical number density regime sam-
pled in Cloud H, namely ⟨n(H2)⟩= 1.8+0.2
