ABSTRACT
JWST’s Little Red Dots (LRDs) display a unique constellation of features that do not occur simul-
taneously in any other class of galaxies or AGN. Here we observe that many of these features find
parallels in the 19th century Great Eruption (GE) of η Carinae and a sub-class of supernovae (Type
IIn). Drawing on these stellar phenomena – outflows trapped by dense circumstellar gas envelopes
– we sketch a possible scenario for LRDs. Outflows from the central engine produce an enshrouding
envelope of gas that may be thought of as a slow wind. This dense wind and its enormous extent
produce an opacity so high that a pseudo-photosphere forms within the wind, obscuring the central
engine and manifesting as a blackbody-like continuum. Radiation from the buried engine powers the
system. The engine may also launch fast winds that crash into the existing envelope to generate
shocks. Lines form within the wind above the photosphere – electron scattering and absorption in
the clumpy (ionized + neutral) medium account for broad wings and P-Cygni cores. A key impli-
cation is that inferences of “overmassive black holes” may be interpreting this wind-like physics as a
virial broad-line region. We propose an escape velocity argument to constrain the mass of the engine,
which yields M < 105M⊙for the typical LRD. The lack of variability and low surface gravity of
the photosphere provide further support for intermediate mass (M ≈103−6M⊙), but very luminous
super-Eddington (Lbol/Ledd ≳5) systems, harboring a supermassive star or intermediate mass black
hole. Paralleling the evolution of IIn SNe, dust production in the envelope may mark the beginnings
of classical AGN. This paper explores a possible self-consistent explanation for the entire life-cycle of
LRDs, from their enshrouding in dense gas to their fates as seeds of massive black holes.
1. INTRODUCTION
In 1843, the massive star η Carinae brightened to such
a degree (≈5 mags brighter than today) that it became
the second brightest star in the entire sky (e.g., Smith
& Frew 2011). For almost two decades, η Car was so
prominent that the aboriginal Boorong people of Aus-
tralia adjusted their mythology of the Carina constel-
lation – the newly luminous and newly reddened η Car
was accorded the status of the wife of the other luminous
star, Canopus, and the mother of the fainter surrounding
stars (e.g., Hamacher & Frew 2010). Light echoes of this

The GE is the most well-known example of a class
of objects that we will describe as “enshrouded erup-

≈10 −30% of its mass (present-day mass of ≈100M⊙)

2017; Smith et al. 2018). The result was a dense, slowly
outflowing gas envelope enclosing the star. And then in
1837, η Car, which at that time was likely in a triple star
system (all enclosed within the envelope), merged with
one of its companions (e.g., Portegies Zwart & van den

have culminated in a short-lived transient. But instead,
the “hot wind” from this merger crashed into the sur-
rounding “cold wind”, setting off a cascade of shocks that
slowly worked their way through the dense circumstellar
medium (CSM; e.g., Smith et al. 2018). The highly ef-

wind into radiative energy – > 50% efficiency, compared

sustained over two decades (e.g., Rest et al. 2012).
Shorter-lived, but spectrally similar, Type IIn SNe con-
stitute the best studied class of enshrouded eruptions
(e.g., Schlegel 1990). In fact, Type IIn SNe are defined
by the fact that they are enshrouded – what is common
across IIn SNe is not a kind of central engine, but that
they are all core-collapse explosions that go off within
a dense circumstellar medium (e.g., Smith 2017). The
envelope confines the typically extremely broad lines of
SNe (≳10, 000 km s−1) into narrow cores (hence the “n”
in IIn). As a result of this unique enshrouded configu-
ration, IIn spectra are highly distinctive. For example,
the emission lines show a combination of broad wings
from electron scattering in ionized gas alongside deep, P-
Cygni-like absorption from neutral gas (e.g., Fassia et al.
2001; Dessart et al. 2009; Kiewe et al. 2012; Taddia et al.
2013; Kankare et al. 2025).
The blackbody-like continuum seen across the major-
ity of IIn lifetimes (typically ≈100 days at > 50% from
peak; Hiramatsu et al. 2024) is reminiscent of the stel-
lar spectrum of a low surface gravity hypergiant with
deep absorption features (e.g., Dessart et al. 2016). How-
ever, we are not seeing a stellar photosphere in hydro-
static equilibrium, but instead a “pseudo-photosphere”
or “wind photosphere” or “recombination photosphere”
(e.g., Leitherer et al. 1985; Davidson 1987; Humphreys
& Davidson 1994; Dessart & Hillier 2011; Owocki & Sha-
viv 2016). This is not the actual surface of a star, but
the effective radiating surface in the wind where the op-
tical depth reaches unity.
The GE, for example, dis-
plays a ≈5000 K pseudo-photospheric continuum, while
the ≈40, 000 K surface of η Car at its center is com-
pletely obscured by the enshrouding gas (e.g., Hillier
et al. 2001; Rest et al. 2012; Prieto et al. 2014; Smith
et al. 2018).
The gas is not in local thermodynamic
equilibrium (LTE), but the source function approaches
a Planck function. In detail, at fixed apparent Teff, the
key difference from a true stellar photosphere is that the
wind photosphere spans a far larger extent accompanied
by a continuous temperature and density gradient in-
stead of the sharp cutoff (e.g., Davidson 1987; Dessart
et al. 2016; Davidson 2016). Contrast the thin, skin-like
photosphere of a supergiant star (a few % of the stellar
radius) with the spectral formation region in a IIn that
can span 10s to 100s of au (e.g., Dessart et al. 2016).
This brings us to JWST’s enigmatic Little Red Dots
(LRDs; Matthee et al. 2024). The basic nature of their
central engines remains debated.
Do they host black
holes or supermassive stars? (e.g., Nandal & Loeb 2025;
Chisholm et al. 2026; Martins et al. 2026; Zwick et al.
2026) Are they the elusive seeds of massive black holes
(≲105M⊙; e.g., Greene et al. 2025; Umeda et al. 2025;
Yanagisawa et al. 2026b; Gentile et al. 2026) or are they
instead already so massive that in some cases they come
close to outweighing their host galaxies (≳107M⊙; e.g.,
Maiolino et al. 2025; Juodˇzbalis et al. 2025; Jones et al.
2025; Gupta et al. 2026)?
The key to the puzzle may lie at the very beginning of
the LRD story. What we now understand to be z ≈0
analogs of LRDs (Lin et al. 2026b; Ji et al. 2025c) were
first discovered by Izotov et al. (2007), 15 years prior to
JWST’s launch. These sources were found in a search
for broad-line objects among metal-poor SDSS galaxies.
Coming across these objects, Izotov et al. (2007) clas-
sified them under an ambiguous category: “IIn/AGN?”.
The spectra had some of the apparent signs of AGN (e.g.,
broad Balmer lines), but at the same time were indistin-
guishable from Type IIn SNe. Follow-up spectroscopy
(Izotov & Thuan 2008) showed the sources to be invari-
ant, thereby ruling out IIn. Interestingly, IIn SNe them-
selves first appeared in the literature as “Seyfert I SNe”
(Filippenko 1989): “Had either of these SNe occurred in
the nucleus of a normal galaxy, the nucleus would prob-
ably have been classified as a Type I Seyfert if the only
data available were low-resolution optical spectra”. Fur-
thermore, Filippenko (1989) used single star spectra to
interpret SN1987F and SN1988I. In other words, ever
since their discovery, IIn SNe have been blurring the lines
between black holes and stars.
In this paper we propose that the remarkable similar-
ities across LRDs, IIn SNe, and the GE are far from a
mere coincidence.
de Graaff et al. (2025a) and Naidu
et al. (2025a) marked a turning point in the LRD story
by observing that LRD physics was better understood
not in terms of traditional galaxies and AGN, but by
drawing analogies to stellar astrophysics. That is, the
central engines of LRDs may be thought of as “black hole
stars” (BH*s) – black holes embedded within dense gas
envelopes such that they radiate in a manner reminiscent
of stellar phenomena (e.g., Kido et al. 2025; Liu et al.
2025a; Inayoshi & Maiolino 2025; Inayoshi et al. 2025;
Begelman & Dexter 2025). Indeed, features classically
associated with stars are seen among LRDs – A-star-
like Balmer breaks (e.g., Furtak et al. 2024; Labbe et al.
2024; Wang et al. 2024a), M-dwarf-like water absorption
(e.g., de Graaff et al. 2025b; Wang et al. 2026), pho-
tospheric blackbody-like continua (e.g., de Graaff et al.
2025b; Umeda et al. 2025; Sun et al. 2026; Torralba
et al. 2026; Gentile et al. 2026), P Cygni line profiles
(e.g., Naidu et al. 2024; Juodˇzbalis et al. 2024b; Wang
et al. 2024b; Loiacono et al. 2025; Kokorev et al. 2025),
Cepheid-like pulsation (pending spectroscopic confirma-
tion; Zhang et al. 2025, 2026; Cantiello et al. 2025). The
toolkit, language, and analogies to stellar astrophysics
have the potential for rich insight into the workings of
LRDs – e.g., the Hertzsprung-Russell (H-R) diagram
(e.g., de Graaff et al. 2025b; Umeda et al. 2025; Lin et al.
2026a), the Hayashi track (e.g., Kido et al. 2025; Liu et al.
2025a; Chen et al. 2026), “stellar” atmosphere models
(Liu et al. 2026a; Santarelli et al. 2025), “stellar” evo-
lutionary grids (Roman-Garza et al. 2026), “proto-star”
magnetic fields (Takasao & Inayoshi 2026), “stellar” wind
models (Chisholm et al. 2026; Martins et al. 2026). Here
we hope to extend our understanding of BH*s, drawing

2. EMPIRICAL SIMILARITIES BETWEEN LITTLE
RED DOTS AND ENSHROUDED ERUPTIONS
Here we highlight a constellation of defining empiri-
cal features that occur across LRDs, the GE, and IIn
SNe. Unless stated otherwise, comparisons to the GE
and IIn SNe in this section correspond to the interaction-
dominated phase of these phenomena where these objects
spend a significant amount of time – few months to years
for IIn SNe (e.g., Taddia et al. 2013; Hiramatsu et al.
2024; Ransome & Villar 2025), and two decades for the
GE (e.g., Smith et al. 2018).
While each of the following features, individually, may
be found in some or the other class of AGN or galaxy,
it is remarkable that all of them occur simultaneously in
these objects. That these unusual features go hand-in-
hand (e.g., a V-shaped SED in a point-source virtually
guarantees broad-lines; Hviding et al. 2025) points to a
unified underlying physical mechanism. The vast zoo of
AGN may show a handful of LRD-like properties, but
never display the entire constellation – e.g., Balmer ab-
sorption is seen in a handful of AGN (e.g., Shangguan
et al. 2026), but they are variable (e.g., Park et al. 2026).

3.4. The Co-Existence of Emission Lines and Cold
Photospheric Continuum
LRDs simultaneously display a blackbody-like opti-
cal continuum alongside strong emission lines (e.g., de
Graaff et al. 2025b; Sun et al. 2026; Torralba et al. 2026).
The interpretation of the continuum as arising from a
≈5000 K (pseudo-)photosphere (e.g., Kido et al. 2025;
Santarelli et al. 2025; Begelman & Dexter 2025; Liu et al.
2026a; Roman-Garza et al. 2026) has been challenged.
If there is a cold photosphere (Teff ≈5000 K), then
how are the lines being produced as this temperature
is much too cold to produce a significant quantity of
ionizing photons? And if the lines are being produced
within the photosphere, the approximate surface where
the medium becomes optically thin, how then are they
escaping? (e.g., Madau & Maiolino 2026; P´erez-Gonz´alez
et al. 2026; Ji et al. 2026a; Sneppen et al. 2026a). And
yet, a wide array of stellar phenomena, including IIn
SNe and the GE display exactly this combination of fea-
tures, with the continuum being successfully modeled for
several decades now as arising from an optically thick
pseudo/wind/recombination-photosphere (e.g., Leitherer
et al. 1985; Davidson 1987; Humphreys & Davidson 1994;
Chugai et al. 2004; Dessart et al. 2009; Dessart et al.
2015; Owocki & Shaviv 2016).
The key insight is that lines are produced in the wind,
scattered in the wind, and escape through the wind that
lies above the pseudo-photosphere (Fig. 8). The wind is
launched close to the hot central engine/shock surface,
and remains partially ionized as it expands outwards
(e.g., Dessart & Hillier 2008). The physical conditions
in the wind (dense, optically thick, partially ionized) are
highly conducive to processes such as collisional excita-
tion (e.g., Torralba et al. 2025), Lyman pumping (e.g.,
Kokorev et al. 2025), Balmer scattering (e.g., Chang
et al. 2025), and fluorescence (e.g., Tripodi et al. 2025)
– these processes do not require substantial Lyman con-
tinuum photons. The n ≥2 levels are well-populated
via collisional excitation as well as Lyman pumping – in-
deed, the enshrouded eruptions display densities exactly
in the regime (≳109−11 cm−3) where these channels are
highly efficient (e.g., Drake & Ulrich 1980). Compared to
ground state atoms, n = 3 −4 atoms are much easier to
collisionally excite and photoionize – e.g., needing only
< 3646˚A photons that are available even in a Teff ≈5000
K blackbody. It is therefore not a coincidence that some
of the strongest lines in LRD spectra are pumped lines
such as O I and Fe II. The clearest signature of the dom-
inance of collisional excitation and Balmer resonance is
the median Hα/Hβ of LRDs of ≈15 arising from the
dense gas (e.g., Brooks et al. 2025a; Sun et al. 2026; Lin
et al. 2026a). Finally, we note that inhomogeneities in
the enveloping gas may allow hotter photons from the
shock front or accreting central engine to escape into the
wind, which would produce photoionization and account
for high-ionization lines paralleling SN2010jl (e.g., Frans-
son et al. 2014; Tang et al. 2025, 2026).
We emphasize that the radiative transfer processes dis-
cussed in this section are well understood (e.g., Drake &
Ulrich 1980). See e.g., the iconic SN1987A, for which Xu
et al. (1992) note a conspicuous absence of Hβ despite the
strong detected Hα, paralleling the remarkable Balmer
decrements of LRDs. This is explained by Balmer scat-
tering in an optically thick medium where Hβ is trapped
and transformed into Hα and Paα, exactly as proposed
for LRDs (e.g., Izotov & Thuan 2008; Chang et al. 2025;
Yan et al. 2025). Further, the accompanying prominent
O I, seen often in both SNe and LRDs (e.g., Pastorello
et al. 2002; Fransson et al. 2002, 2014; Fox et al. 2020),
supports this interpretation since it requires substantial
optical depth in the Balmer lines for Ly-β pumping to
be efficient (e.g., Grandi 1980).
4. NEW APPROACHES TO LRD CENTRAL
ENGINE MASSES
Line profiles bearing remarkable similarities to those of
LRDs are produced in IIn SNe and LBV eruptions (Fig.
2). These objects do not possess a virialized broad-line
region (e.g., Juodˇzbalis et al. 2025; Jones et al. 2025),
nor do they possess a stratified virialized broad-line re-
gion (e.g., Scholtz et al. 2026b; Madau et al. 2026), nor
one sitting behind a uniform electron scattering screen
(e.g., D’Eugenio et al. 2025b; Ivey et al. 2026). Instead,
the broad Balmer lines are produced and scattered in a
cold, partially ionized wind (red layer in Figure 8). Re-
covering an intrinsic Gaussian profile by correcting for
electron scattering (e.g., Rusakov et al. 2026; Kokorev
et al. 2025) would yield the velocity dispersion of the
cold wind, which is fundamentally different from a virial
broad-line region. If this is indeed the situation in the
LRDs, there is effectively nothing in the Balmer line pro-
files that may be connected to black hole masses via tra-
ditional calibrations. Note that this is a fundamentally
different physical scenario versus classical AGN studied
using virial calibrations (e.g., Greene & Ho 2006) – clas-
sical AGN do not display the constellation of interlinked
properties seen among LRDs.
Fresh approaches to constraining the masses of LRD
central engines are needed.
The analogies to stellar
and supernova physics that we have drawn in this pa-
per present a promising path that we now set out on.

5. DISCUSSION
5.1. What lies at the heart of LRDs?
Until this point, we have remained agnostic. All the
results presented here do not assume any central engine.
A key implication of the enshrouded eruption analogy is
that it is extremely challenging to gather any direct in-
formation about what exactly lies within the dense gas
envelope.
To draw a parallel from the SNe literature,
some apparent “IIn SNe” are not supernovae at all, but
are instead GE-like outbursts (“supernova impostors”;
e.g., Van Dyk et al. 2000; Smith et al. 2010; Humphreys
et al. 2012). A dramatic example is “SN2009ip” that in
reality was a GE-like eruption – only to then explode as a
IIn SN in 2012 (e.g., Mauerhan et al. 2013). The ambigu-
ous class of “IIn/Ia-CSM SNe” – where the nature of the
explosion, thermonuclear (Ia) or core-collapse (II/Ib/Ic),
is unknown – further underscores the difficulty of seeing
past the pseudo-photosphere (e.g., Inserra et al. 2016;
Jerkstrand et al. 2020; Sharma et al. 2023). Long-term
monitoring is what unmasks the SNe impostors, but the
timescales relevant for LRDs (§3.2, §4.3) are more chal-
lenging to observe.
Keeping this ambiguity inherent to the physical setup
in mind, based on indirect arguments and analogies we
discuss two leading candidates for LRD central engines –
supermassive stars and intermediate mass black holes2.
5.1.1. Supermassive Star Central Engines
Supermassive stars (SMS; 103−5M⊙) may play a role
in LRD formation (e.g., Nandal & Loeb 2025; Chisholm
et al. 2026; Martins et al. 2026; Nandal et al. 2026; Zwick
et al. 2026). LRDs have a propensity for strong Nitrogen
emission (e.g., Isobe et al. 2025; Morel et al. 2025), imply-
ing extreme [N/O] and [N/C] abundances that are typi-
cally found in the Milky Way among stars born in dense,
massive clusters (e.g., Belokurov & Kravtsov 2023, 2024;
Naidu et al. 2025b; Schaerer et al. 2025; Ji et al. 2025a).
Dense star clusters are exactly where SMS are predicted
to form via runaway collisions and produce such peculiar
abundances (e.g., Denissenkov & Hartwick 2014; Gieles
et al. 2018; Charbonnel et al. 2023; Nandal et al. 2025;
Rantala 2026; Williams et al. 2026). Further, the age
distribution of metal-poor Milky Way globular clusters
– possible birth sites of SMS – may mirror the redshift
evolution of the LRD number density (Chisholm et al.
2026). Sun et al. (2026) demonstrate LRD hosts to have
undergone recent starbursts, possibly linking the forma-
tion (or activation) of LRD central engines to very re-
cent star-formation, which at high-redshift proceeds in
the form of dense cluster formation (e.g., Fujimoto et al.
2024; Adamo et al. 2024; Claeyssens et al. 2025).
Fi-
nally, the LRD luminosity function has a sharp cutoff at
the bright-end (e.g., Ma et al. 2025; Weibel et al. 2026),
which may correspond to the general relativistic instabil-
ity limit for the maximum mass of an SMS (≈105−6M⊙;
e.g., Woods et al. 2017; Nandal et al. 2024; Saio et al.
2024) provided it has an Lbol/Ledd comparable to the
GE and IIn SNe.
2 The unknowability and Schr¨odinger’s cat-like state of what lies

Against this backdrop, given the connections to mas-
sive star phenomena made in this work (e.g., the GE
arising from a ≈102M⊙star), it is natural to conjecture
that the formation of LRDs may be linked to even more
massive stars (≳103M⊙).
We envision that an SMS
eruption that enshrouds the star would be followed by
a succession of increasingly violent eruptions that could
power the LRD phase via CSM interaction. Each discrete
eruption – of which there may be several dozens (e.g.,
Nandal et al. 2026) – may sustain an LRD-like spectrum
for decades to centuries (§3.2) over the ≈1 −2 Myr life
of the SMS. Enshrouding the central engine in this man-
ner elegantly sidesteps the issue of accreting external gas
and robbing it of angular momentum for it to settle into
a fully enclosing (covering fraction approaching ≈100%)
gas envelope (e.g., Yanagisawa et al. 2026a).
Other major challenges for SMS as LRD progenitors
are alleviated by our results. General relativistic (GR)
instabilities set a limit of ≈105−6M⊙for SMS survival
under standard assumptions (e.g., Woods et al. 2017;
Nandal et al. 2024; Saio et al. 2024). The reduced cen-
tral engine masses we find (Table 2) are now compatible
with this limit. Furthermore, the CSM interaction mech-
anism from a succession of winds can elevate the lumi-
nosity of < 105M⊙SMS (≈109.5L⊙at the Eddington
limit) to encompass the entire range for observed LRDs
(≈109 −1011.5L⊙; see Fig. 1). Further, these lower cen-
tral engine masses reconcile upper limits on BH masses in
local GCs (e.g., Baumgardt 2017) with scenarios where
SMS forming in GCs give rise to LRDs (e.g., Chisholm
et al. 2026).
A major remaining challenge is that the short SMS life-
times of ≲1−2 Myr, even under the most optimistic as-
sumptions, including continuous external accretion (e.g.,
Nandal & Chon 2026) are in tension with LRD duty cy-
cle constraints. The typical LRD host spends ≈10 −20
Myrs as an LRD (e.g., Sun et al. 2026). Further, note
that the kind of dense, optically thick winds required to
match the properties of LRDs might appear only towards
the very end of the SMS life just before it collapses into
an IMBH (in the last ≈0.1 Myr; e.g., Nandal et al. 2026),
though these models are still in the very early stages of
development (e.g., Martins et al. 2026).
This tension
may be somewhat alleviated if several SMS form within
the same galaxy in distinct star clusters, as hinted by
a z ≈7 galaxy with multiple LRDs (Yanagisawa et al.
2026b) – together, they may account for the integrated
lifetime. This is not unreasonable, given the multiplic-
ity of GCs observed in galaxies of mass comparable to
typical LRD hosts (≈108M⊙; e.g., Matthee et al. 2025;
Sun et al. 2026). For instance, arguably the best-studied
high-redshift (z ≈2) galaxy with mass comparable to
LRD hosts (≈108M⊙; Matthee et al. 2025; Lin et al.
2025; Sun et al. 2026), the Gaia-Sausage Enceladus (e.g.,
Helmi et al. 2018; Belokurov et al. 2018; Naidu et al.
2021), may have hosted an ensemble of ≈30 GCs (e.g.,
Myeong et al. 2018; Massari et al. 2019; Kruijssen et al.
2020).
Indeed, the average number of GCs hosted by
an LRD host galaxy (M⋆≈108M⊙, Mhalo ≈1011M⊙
at z ≈5; Lin et al. 2025; Matthee et al. 2025; Sun et al.
2026) based on simple analytical models of GC formation
is ≈10 −20 (e.g., El-Badry et al. 2019).
We conclude that if not for the central engines of LRDs

5.1.2. All Roads Lead to Super-Eddington IMBHs
Inspired by the massive black holes (≈108−9M⊙) al-
ready in place in the first billion years (e.g., Fan et al.
2001; Ba˜nados et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2020; Wang et al.
2021; Fan et al. 2023), it has long been anticipated that
their rapid rise may require spectacular physical phenom-
ena including e.g., supermassive stars, hyper-Eddington
accretion, and dense gas shrouds (e.g., Rees 1984; Begel-
man et al. 2006; Alexander & Natarajan 2014; Wise et al.
2019; Inayoshi et al. 2020; Volonteri et al. 2021; Natara-
jan 2021).
The terminal state of an SMS (or almost any alterna-
tive for what lies within the gas envelope) is likely to
be an IMBH (e.g., Umeda et al. 2016; Woods et al. 2017;
Nandal et al. 2026). Indeed, this is supported by our cen-
tral engine mass constraints in §4. And so LRDs either
already represent the long-awaited discovery of early Uni-
verse super-Eddington IMBHs (e.g., Greene et al. 2020;
Inayoshi et al. 2020), or are a few Myrs away from turn-
ing into these IMBHs. Further, if these IMBHs are de-
scended directly from SMS – as suggested by the case for
SMS in §5.1.1 – LRDs represent the heavy seeds envi-
sioned by numerous massive black hole formation chan-
nels (e.g., Lodato & Natarajan 2007; Begelman et al.
2008; Volonteri & Begelman 2010).
In the context of our proposed picture (§3, Fig. 8),
IMBHs are long-lived, can drive strong winds (in e.g.,
the form of super-Eddington outflows; e.g., Liu et al.
2025b), and can supplement or act in place of the radia-
tive energy from CSM interaction with accretion energy.
We again emphasize that effectively all signatures that
have been interpreted to arise from an AGN in LRDs
– e.g., broad Balmer lines, high bolometric luminosity –
can be explained as a product of wind and CSM interac-
tion physics. However, it is interesting to note that there
exist a handful of sources that share (some) features of
LRDs (e.g., V-shaped or blackbody-like SEDs), but are
X-ray bright (e.g., Fu et al. 2026; Hviding et al. 2026;
Zhong et al. 2026). If indeed connected to LRDs, these
may be transitional objects where the gas envelopes are
dispersing to reveal an accreting IMBH. As such, they
currently represent the only relatively direct line of evi-
dence for the LRDs’ IMBH nature.
5.2. What follows the LRD phase? Dust Production
and the Beginnings of Classical AGN
Enshrouded eruptions are highly effective at producing
dust (e.g., Fox et al. 2011; Maeda et al. 2013; Smith 2013;
Gall et al. 2014; Morris et al. 2017; Shahbandeh et al.
2025). LRDs may similarly be highly efficient dust fac-
tories. Dust may form in dense winds when the outflow
cools and becomes sufficiently shielded from the central
radiation field, allowing refractory atoms and molecules
to nucleate into seed grains that rapidly grow before the
gas becomes too dilute. Indeed, hints of such dust are
seen most clearly among a subset of z ≈0 LRDs in
the infrared (e.g., Ji et al. 2025c; Lin et al. 2026b; Park
et al. 2026; Lin et al. 2026a), whose infrared excesses
above the photospheric continuum may be attributed to
freshly forming dust (e.g., Carbon and Silicates; see Fig.
7). Further, molecules such as water are beginning to be
detected in LRDs (e.g., Wang et al. 2026), which show
that the optimal conditions for dust-formation such as
cold layers with T < 3000 K are indeed found in these
sources. LRDs may therefore be an abundant, previously
unconsidered source of dust in the very early Universe
– an epoch where signatures of dust are routinely de-
tected, but whose source remains unclear (e.g., Witstok
et al. 2023; Shivaei et al. 2025; Markov et al. 2025; Al-
gera et al. 2026). We defer detailed exploration of the
implications for high-redshift dust physics to Ashall et
al. in prep.
Here we note an intriguing implication for mas-
sive black hole evolution – the extent of the pseudo-
photosphere (≈1300 au) already rivals that of broad-line
regions in low-luminosity AGN (e.g., Bentz et al. 2013).
With dust being produced prolifically, all the ingredients
are in place to form the entire structure of a classical
broad-line region such as a gas disk with a dusty torus.
Given this, it seems plausible that at the end of the en-
shrouded eruption phase, LRDs are poised to transform
into the familiar AGN of the low-z Universe (e.g., Urry
& Padovani 1995).

We argue that LRDs share numerous empirical fea-

rather remarkable, since many of these LRD features are
exceptionally rare across all galaxies and AGN – their
collective co-occurrence in a well-understood set of ob-
jects promises rich insights.

photosphere, excited by collisions, resonance, and
fluorescence in dense, optically thick gas. [§3.4]
• The enshrouded physics of LRDs implies that Balmer
lines are formed and scattered in the cold wind, and
are therefore not tracing a virial broad line region. We
present four arguments for central engine masses –
• Assuming LRDs are super-Eddington by analogy
to the GE and IIn SNe (Lbol/Ledd ≈5−50). [§4.1]
• Using the fastest outflowing absorbing gas in an
“escape velocity” argument. [§4.2]
• Translating the lack of variability into a dynamical
timescale of the pseudo-photosphere. [§4.3]
• Converting the low surface gravity of the pseudo-
photosphere into an enclosed mass. [§4.4]
All four arguments consistently imply intermediate
mass, super-Eddington central engines with M ≲105M⊙
and Lbol/Ledd ≳5 for the typical LRD, with a popula-
tion range of ≈103−6M⊙. There may be no “overmassive
black holes”, with LRDs falling within the scatter of the
local M⋆−MBH relationship. [Table 2, §4, Fig. 10]
• These lower central engine masses, the numerous par-
allels between LRDs and massive star phenomena, and
the route to super-Eddington luminosity via wind inter-
action highlighted in this paper strengthen the viability
of supermassive stars as LRD progenitors. [§5.1.1]
• All signatures that have been interpreted to arise from
an AGN in LRDs (e.g., broad lines, high luminosity) may
be explained via wind interaction. However, circumstan-
tial evidence (e.g., X-ray detected LRD-like objects, life-
times, that all SMS end up as BHs) suggests IMBHs may
be the protagonists of the LRD phase. [§5.1.2]
• Like enshrouded eruptions, LRDs have the perfect com-
bination of cold, shielded dense gas to be prolific produc-
ers of dust, representing a new channel for the early Uni-
verse dust budget. This dust production may mark the
beginnings of classical AGN with e.g., dusty tori. [§5.2]
It is a special thing in the study of the Universe to be
confronted by an entirely new category of astrophysical
object. Just as the Boorong people were transfixed by
η Carinae, and just as the astronomers of the 50s were
seized by quasi-stellar objects, we find ourselves again in
this special place with the Little Red Dots.
