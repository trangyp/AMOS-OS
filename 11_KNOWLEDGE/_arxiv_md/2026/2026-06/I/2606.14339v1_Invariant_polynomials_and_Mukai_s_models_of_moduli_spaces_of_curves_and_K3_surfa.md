INVARIANT POLYNOMIALS AND MUKAI’S MODELS OF MODULI SPACES OF
CURVES AND K3 SURFACES
DAVID SWINARSKI
Abstract. Beginning in the 1980s, Mukai introduced birational models of some moduli spaces of curves
and some moduli spaces of K3 surfaces. They are defined as geometric invariant theory quotients. Little
is known about the boundaries of these spaces.
We describe an approach to efficiently evaluate certain
invariant polynomials associated to these GIT problems. This allows us to show that several singular curves
and surfaces are GIT semistable in Mukai’s models. In the appendix, we give a combinatorial formula for
an SLn-invariant in terms of the Gelfand-Tsetlin basis.
1. Introduction
In a series of papers beginning in 1988, Mukai introduced birational models MX1
g of some moduli spaces of
curves, and birational models MX2
g of some moduli spaces of K3 surfaces [18–22]1. For curves, Mukai’s models
MX1
g are birational to M g, the moduli space of Deligne-Mumford stable curves of genus g, for g = 7, 8, 9.
For genus 10, Mukai also gives a model of the moduli space of curves that lie on a K3 surface; these form a
divisor in M 10. For surfaces, Mukai’s models MX2
g are models of the moduli spaces of polarized K3 surfaces
of genus g for g = 7, 8, 9, 10.
Each of Mukai’s models is defined as a geometric invariant theory (GIT) quotient. These descriptions will
be given in Section 2. What are the GIT stable, semistable, and unstable loci for these GIT problems?
A few foundational results are known for these GIT problems. For K3 surfaces, Greer, Li, and Tian prove
that smooth, Brill-Noether general K3 surfaces are GIT stable in Mukai’s models [14, Thm. 2.9]. For curves,
Farkas and Verra prove that a general smooth or irreducible nodal curve with at most g −1 nodes is GIT
semistable in a GIT problem that is closely related to Mukai’s model [9, Prop. 3.5]; as a corollary, the same
statement is true for Mukai’s models. However, many questions remain open regarding which objects may
appear on the boundary of Mukai’s models.
In [25] the author gave examples of three singular curves that appear in Mukai’s model of M 7.

The proofs of semistability in [25] are classical and explicit: find an invariant polynomial F of Mukai’s GIT
problem and a point [P] parameterizing the curve of interest and show that F([P]) ̸= 0 by direct calculation.
In this work, we improve and expand on the ideas introduced in [25]. Here, we describe a different method
for constructing and evaluating invariant polynomials that is vastly more efficient than the approach used
in [25]. We implement this method in Macaulay2, and use our code to show that several examples in each
of Mukai’s models are GIT semistable. This yields the following theorem.

The examples for genus g = 8, 9, 10 were selected as analogues of the genus 7 examples that appeared in
[25]. The genus 7 examples were selected because they appear in the literature related to Green’s Conjecture
and the Hassett-Keel program. See [25, Section 1.1] for more details and references. However, in our opinion,
the most striking features of the list appearing in Theorem 1.1 are its length and breadth, rather than the
geometric properties of any particular items on the list. It is clear that one could use the methods and code
described here to analyze many more examples for these GIT problems.
Outline. In Section 2 we describe the GIT problems used to define each of Mukai’s models. In Section 3 we
describe the invariant polynomials Fg,d up to scaling that we use to prove the main theorem. In Section 4 we
describe the points in Mukai’s parameter spaces that yield the examples listed in the main theorem. We also
describe in detail how we found these points for two examples (the balanced K3 carpet and balanced ribbon
in genus 9). In Section 5 we prove the main theorem and make some remarks on the computer calculations
involved.
Finally, in the self-contained Appendix, we prove a combinatorial formula for an SLn-invariant in the
representation V (λ) ⊗V (λ∗) in terms the Gelfand-Tsetlin bases of V (λ) and V (λ∗). This formula is imple-
mented in the LieAlgebraRepresentations package and used once in the main part of the paper to give a
fast calculation of one step in constructing the polynomial F8,2. This material is presented in an appendix
because it is thematically distinct from the rest of the paper.

2. The GIT problems associated to Mukai’s models
Each Mukai model is the GIT quotient of a Grassmannian variety by a simple linear algebraic group. We
use the following notation.
Definition 2.1. Let g be a simple Lie algebra of rank n. Let ω1, . . . , ωn be the fundamental dominant
weights for g, and let λ = P ciωi be a dominant integral weight. We write V (c1, . . . , cn) for the irreducible
g-module with highest weight λ.

3. Invariant polynomials associated to Mukai’s models
We have constructed one or more invariant polynomials for each of Mukai’s models. Here, for each Mukai
model MXk

examples. We repeatedly exploit the fact that the representations ΛgV ∗

3.1. How we selected these polynomials. Suppose that ΛgV ∗
g decomposes into irreducibles ⊕V (λi)mi.
We typically select the irreducible V (λi) with smallest dimension. Then we seek the lowest degree d such
that Symd V (λi) contains an invariant. If d = 4, 6 we may seek trivial G-modules among the products of
submodules of Sym2 V (λi). When doing so, we typically select submodules of small dimension, but not
necessarily the smallest ones.

4. Locating specific singular curves and surfaces in the parameter spaces of Mukai’s
models
Our approach to proving the Main Theorem requires that we find points in the parameter spaces of
Mukai’s models corresponding to the singular curves and surfaces listed there.
In Section 4.1, we discuss the coordinates we use for the representations Vg and the equations of the
homogeneous varieties Xg in these coordinates. In Section 4.2, we describe points in the Grassmannians
corresponding to the singular curves and surfaces appearing in the Main Theorem, and give a link to the
author’s website where additional Macaulay2 code can be found verifying these claims. Finally, in Section
4.3, we demonstrate in an example how we obtained these points.

5. Proof of the Main Theorem and remarks on computational performance
5.1. Strategy. Consider the descriptions of the polynomials Fg,d given in Section 3.
These polynomials can be constructed by repeating the following task several times: given a g-module W
and a weight µ, find a submodule U ⊂W that is isomorphic to the irreducible representation V (µ).
We can do this as follows.
(1) Compute the explicit action of the lowering operators of g on W.
(2) Find a highest weight vector of weight µ in W.
(3) Choose a basis of V (µ) and express each basis element as a word in the lowering operators applied
to a highest weight vector.

experimentally test the G-invariance of the polynomials Fg,d by applying a random element of G and checking
that the value of Fg,d remains unchanged. See [26] for some calculations of this type.
