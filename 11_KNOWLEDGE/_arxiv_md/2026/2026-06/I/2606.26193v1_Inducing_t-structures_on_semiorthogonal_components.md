INDUCING T-STRUCTURES ON SEMIORTHOGONAL COMPONENTS
ALEXANDER KUZNETSOV, SHENGXUAN LIU, AND ALEXANDER PERRY
Abstract. Given a triangulated category with a t-structure, we introduce a method for
inducing t-structures on its semiorthogonal components, based on the construction of an as-
sociated perverse t-structure on the ambient category. As applications, we construct bounded
t-structures in many new examples, including: almost all known phantom and quasiphantom
categories; the semiorthogonal complement of the structure sheaf on a Fano variety; the
residual component of an Enriques surface; the categorical resolution of a nodal cubic curve
appearing in an early counterexample to the Jordan–H¨older property for semiorthogonal
decompositions; and Brill–Noether modifications of the derived category of a curve.

1. Introduction
The bounded derived category of coherent sheaves Db(X) on a smooth proper variety X
plays a central role in modern algebraic geometry. One of the most important methods for
analyzing this category is to break it into smaller pieces, via the notion of a semiorthogonal
decomposition. A triangulated category C appearing as a component in such a decomposition
can fruitfully be regarded as a noncommutative algebraic variety. Indeed, even though C does
not possess an underlying space of points, it exhibits many of the structures of (the derived
category of) an actual variety. For instance:
• C admits a Serre functor, which plays the role of the canonical bundle in Serre duality;
• C carries intrinsic motivic invariants, such as K-theory and Hochschild homology,
generalizing the corresponding invariants of varieties; and
• C has an associated moduli stack which parameterizes its objects.
A fundamental feature of the category Db(X) is that it admits a bounded t-structure,
which formalizes the fact that each of its objects can be built up via extensions and shifts
from finitely many objects in the abelian category Coh(X) of coherent sheaves. This paper is
motivated by the question of whether semiorthogonal components also exhibit this structure.

Question 1.1. If C ⊂Db(X) is a semiorthogonal component of the derived category of a
smooth proper variety X, then does C admit a bounded t-structure?
Question 1.1 is wide open. To our knowledge, no negative results are known, while pos-
itive results were previously only known for some very special examples arising as residual
components of low-dimensional Fano varieties [BLMS23, PPZ22, Liu25a].
The purpose of this paper is to provide a new approach to the problem. Given a triangulated
category D, such as Db(X), our main result provides a criterion for inducing a t-structure on
a semiorthogonal component of D from a t-structure on D. This leads to the construction of
bounded t-structures on many interesting semiorthogonal components, including almost all
known phantom and quasiphantom categories.
An important motivation for Question 1.1 is Bridgeland’s theory of stability conditions on
triangulated categories [Bri07]. The handful of residual components of Fano varieties where
stability conditions are known to exist have led to many applications, so it is natural to search
for a construction that applies to semiorthogonal components more widely; since any stability
condition comes with an underlying bounded t-structure, a positive answer to Question 1.1
would provide a first step in this direction. In a sequel to this work, we will build on our
methods to provide criteria for inducing stability conditions on semiorthogonal components.
1.1. Induced t-structures. Recall that a t-structure τ = (D≤0, D≥0) on a triangulated
category D consists of a pair of subcategories D≤0 and D≥0, called the connective and cocon-
nective parts of τ, satisfying three axioms, which can informally be described as compatibility
with shifts, Hom-orthogonality, and the generation property (see Definition 2.5).
Let C ⊂D be a triangulated subcategory, with embedding functor γ : C →D. There are at
least two naive ways to attempt to construct a t-structure on C from the t-structure τ on D.
For the first, consider the following pair of subcategories of C:
γ−1(τ) := (γ−1(D≤0), γ−1(D≥0)),
(1.1)
where γ−1(D≤0) ⊂C denotes the subcategory of objects C ∈C with γ(C) ∈D≤0, and similarly
for γ−1(D≥0). This pair always satisfies compatibility with shifts and Hom-orthogonality, but
the generation property may fail. If, however, the generation property holds, then we say
that τ restricts to C and call γ−1(τ) (or τ|C when we wish to suppress the functor γ) the
restricted t-structure.
For the second construction, assume that C ⊂D is right admissible, i.e., that γ admits a
right adjoint γ! : D →C; there is a parallel story for left admissible subcategories. Then we
may also consider the following pair of subcategories of C:
γ!(τ) := (γ!(D≤0), γ!(D≥0)),
(1.2)
where γ!(D≤0) ⊂C denotes the full subcategory of objects of the form γ!(D) for D ∈D≤0, and
similarly for γ!(D≥0). This pair always satisfies compatibility with shifts and the generation
property, while Hom-orthogonality may fail. If, however, Hom-orthogonality holds, we say
that τ projects to C along γ! or right projects to C, and call γ!(τ) the projected t-structure.
Note that the isomorphism of functors γ!◦γ ∼= idC implies an inclusion γ−1(D≤0) ⊂γ!(D≤0),
i.e. the connective part of (1.1) is always contained in the connective part of (1.2). On the other
hand, if (1.2) is a t-structure, it is easy to see that γ−1(D≤0) = γ!(D≤0) (see Lemma 4.2(3)),
i.e. the connective parts of (1.1) and (1.2) coincide. Thus, (1.1) and (1.2) can be considered

be noetherian (see Remark 1.9), in which case we cannot apply Theorem 1.3.
On the other hand, we prove that in the situation of Theorem 1.7 with only one exceptional

gives one indication that, when the given t-structure on D underlies a stability condition, the
induced t-structures on semiorthogonal components may be better behaved.

which has been applied to the residual components of some low-dimensional Fano varieties.
As we explain in Remark 4.15 and Example 4.16, this construction can be thought of as a
very special case of our results.
1.2. Applications. The forward Hom-vanishing condition (1.7) in Theorem 1.7 is fairly re-
strictive. However, there are still many interesting examples satisfying this condition, some of
which we describe below.
Exceptional objects. As we already mentioned, condition (1.7) in Theorem 1.7 is vacuous when
the exceptional sequence has length 1. Therefore, the semiorthogonal component C defined by
Db(X) = ⟨E, C⟩
admits a bounded t-structure, connectively induced by the standard one on Db(X). More
generally, Theorem 1.7 directly applies to an exceptional object in the standard heart of the
derived category of a noetherian abelian category.

The simplest example is when X is the blowup in a point of a smooth surface Y , and we

For other simple examples of exceptional objects, however, we obtain bounded t-structures
on highly nontrivial categories:
• If X is a Fano variety, then the structure sheaf OX is exceptional. Taking X to be
any stacky weighted Fano hypersurface of index 1, we obtain bounded t-structures on
infinitely many fractional Calabi–Yau categories (Theorem 6.4).
• We construct a bounded t-structure on a curious categorical resolution C of a nodal
cubic curve appearing in one of the early counterexamples to the Jordan–H¨older prop-
erty for semiorthogonal decompositions [Kuz13] (Theorem 6.6); this answers a ques-
tion of Haiden and Wu [HW25], who recently proved that C does not admit a stability
condition.
• We construct a bounded t-structure on the Brill–Noether modifications of the derived
category of a curve recently introduced in [AK25] (Theorem 6.8); as a special case, this
gives such a t-structure on the crepant categorical resolution of the residual category
of a nodal cubic threefold (Example 6.9).
Enriques surfaces. For an Enriques surface X, there is an exceptional sequence L1, . . . , L10
of 10 line bundles, defining an interesting semiorthogonal decomposition
Db(X) = ⟨L1, . . . , L10, RX⟩
that has been studied in various works [Zub97, IK15, LNSZ21, LSZ22]. We prove that RX
admits a bounded t-structure (Theorem 6.10). When X is generic, the exceptional sequence is
completely orthogonal in the sense that RHom(Li, Lj) = 0 for i ̸= j, so Theorem 1.7 directly
applies; in general, we instead appeal to a mild strengthening of this result, Theorem 5.12.
Phantom categories. Perhaps the most interesting application of our results is to phantom
categories. Recall that a semiorthogonal component P ⊂Db(X) of the derived category of
a smooth projective variety is called a phantom if both its Grothendieck group K0(P) and
Hochschild homology HH•(P) vanish. The existence of such strange categories came as a
surprise when Gorchinskiy and Orlov [GO13] and B¨ohning, Graf von Bothmer, Katzarkov,
and Sosna [BGvBKS15] constructed the first examples as semiorthogonal components on fairly
exotic varieties of general type. A striking recent example due to Krah [Kra24] shows that a
phantom can also exist on a rational surface, and further examples of this type were produced
in [KKL+26] (see also [MXY25]).
A phantom category cannot admit a stability condition, due to the vanishing of K0(P).
This suggests that phantoms form a critical test case for Question 1.1. Indeed, since the
first examples of phantoms were constructed it has been a well-known open question —
to our knowledge first raised in print in 2015 by Sosna [Sos20] — whether a phantom can
admit a bounded t-structure. As we show in Theorem 6.12, our Theorem 1.7 applies to give
an affirmative answer for almost all of the known examples of phantom categories, as well
as quasiphantom categories (defined by relaxing the vanishing of K0(P) to finiteness). This
shows that these categories are not as ill-behaved as may have previously been expected.

bounded t-structures. In particular, the bounded t-structures produced in Theorem 6.12 are

From the existence of a bounded t-structure, we also deduce that the above phantoms
and quasiphantoms have a classical generator G ∈P with the property that Exti(G, G) = 0
for i < 0; in particular, A = RHom(G, G) is a coconnective DG algebra such that Dperf(A) is

with vanishing negative self-Ext groups. In Example 6.17, we give a conceptual explanation
of this vanishing for some of these objects, by observing that they are contained in the heart
of an induced t-structure.

1.3. Further directions. It would be very interesting to carry out our general approach to
Question 1.1 via perverse t-structures (detailed in §5.4) in new situations. Even in the situation
of Example 1.2, although we know that the t-structure on Db(X) connectively induces the
standard t-structure on Db(Y ), we only know that the corresponding perverse t-structure
on Db(X) exists when the fibers of f : X →Y have dimension ≤1 as in Example 1.5. If
the perverse t-structure could be constructed without this restriction, it may give a route
to generalizing Bridgeland’s work on derived equivalences of threefold flops [Bri02] to higher
dimensions.
As mentioned above, our interest in Question 1.1 was largely motivated by the theory of
stability conditions. We will return to this topic in a sequel to this paper, where we will provide
criteria for inducing stability conditions on semiorthogonal components. This is subtle since,
as mentioned above, there exist semiorthogonal components (such as phantoms) that do not
admit a stability condition.
Even in cases where stability conditions do not exist, it would be interesting to study moduli
spaces of objects in the heart of the induced t-structure on a semiorthogonal component.
Example 6.17 suggests that such moduli spaces are rich even for phantoms. We highlight two
general questions:
(1) Under what conditions does the property of lying in the heart of an induced t-structure
define an open condition, and hence cut out an open subspace of the moduli space of
all objects?

Definition 2.1. Let D be a triangulated category. A semiorthogonal decomposition of D,
denoted D = ⟨D1, D2, . . . , Dn⟩, is a sequence D1, D2, . . . , Dn of full triangulated subcategories
of D such that:
(1) (RHom-vanishing) For all Di ∈Di, Dj ∈Dj with i > j, we have RHom(Di, Dj) = 0.
(2) (Generation property) For any D ∈D, there exists a sequence of morphisms
0 = Dn →Dn−1 →· · · →D1 →D0 = D
such that for all 1 ≤i ≤n the cone of the morphism Di →Di−1 lies in Di.
The subcategories Di ⊂D are called the components of the semiorthogonal decomposition.
It follows from Definition 2.1 that the filtration of D ∈D appearing in condition (2), as
well as its graded pieces cone(Di →Di−1), are functorial. The functor pri : D →D given by
the ith graded piece is called the projection functor onto Di.
Given a subcategory C ⊂D of a triangulated category D, its right and left orthogonal
subcategories are defined by
C⊥= { D ∈D RHom(C, D) = 0 for all C ∈C and n ∈Z } ,
⊥C = { D ∈D RHom(D, C) = 0 for all C ∈C and n ∈Z } .
In these terms, the following well-known (and straightforward) lemma gives a useful method
for constructing semiorthogonal decompositions.
Lemma 2.2. Let D be a triangulated category. Let B, C ⊂D be triangulated subcategories.
Then the following are equivalent:
(1) D = ⟨B, C⟩is a semiorthogonal decomposition.
(2) B ⊂D is left admissible, i.e. the inclusion admits a left adjoint, and C = ⊥B.
(3) C ⊂D is right admissible, i.e. the inclusion admits a right adjoint, and B = C⊥.
Explicitly, if D = ⟨B, C⟩is a semiorthogonal decomposition, then the projection functor onto B
is the left adjoint to the inclusion B →D, and the projection functor onto C is the right adjoint
to the inclusion C →D.

Definition 2.15. Let A be an abelian category. A torsion pair in A is a pair of full subcate-
