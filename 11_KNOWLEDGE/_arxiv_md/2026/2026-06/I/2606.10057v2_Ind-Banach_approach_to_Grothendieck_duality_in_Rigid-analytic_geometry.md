Abstract
We prove a duality theorem for quasi-compactly supported cohomology of quasi-
coherent sheaves on rigid-analytic spaces, with respect to a smooth and Kiehl partially-
proper morphism. This includes an identification of the dualizing object with volume
forms. The functional analysis underlying our theory does not use condensed mathe-
matics, but rather Ind-Banach spaces, following Ben-Bassat–Kelly–Kremnizer. Nev-
ertheless, our overall strategy is inspired by that of Clausen–Scholze in the complex-
analytic setting.

1.2. Since Serre duality exists in both the algebraic and analytic settings, it is natural
to ask if there is an analytic counterpart of Grothendieck duality. There are two main
obstructions to achieving this goal.
The first problem is that one must find the correct definition of QCoh in the analytic
setting. Fortunately, this has been resolved by new theories of analytic geometry [CS19;
CS22; BKK24].
In short, the correct definition QCoh must account for the analytic
structure on the rings we work with. At the same time, it must also be derived: not only
because this is the most natural way to formulate Grothendieck duality, but also because
of the pathology that the Grothendieck topologies appearing in analytic geometry are not
flat, and so descent for QCoh cannot hold without passing to the stable setting. The
crux is therefore the question of how to synthesise functional analysis and homological
algebra. Our definition of QCoh follows Ben-Bassat–Kelly–Kremnizer [BKK24] and uses
the theory of Ind-Banach spaces, rather than the condensed mathematics of [CS19; CS22].
The second problem is that, for applications in rigid geometry, duality for proper
morphisms is not enough. Intuitively, the analytic topology is much finer than the Zariski
topology and so being quasi-compact is a much stronger condition. For instance, the affine
line A1, the open unit disk ˚
D1, and the upper half-plane are not quasi-compact, but we
should still expect to have a duality theory for these. For a morphism f : X →Y of rigid
or complex-analytic spaces subject to some mild hypotheses, we need to define the functor
f! : QCoh(X) →QCoh(Y ) of pushforward with relative quasi-compact supports, in such
a way that f! is compatible with base-change and satisfies the projection formula. This is
a highly non-trivial problem which has been resolved by the recent theory of six-functor

In (i), a morphism is taut if the underlying morphism of classical rigid spaces is taut
in the sense of Huber [Hub96, Definition 0.4.7, Definition 5.1.2].
This is a very mild

In (ii), qcqs stands for quasi-compact and quasi-separated. In fact, we already proved

In (iii), quasi-smooth Zariski-closed immersion is defined as in Definition 7.18. This
is the derived analogue of a regular closed immersion.
In (iii) and (v), the notion of suave morphism is defined as in [HM24, §4.5]. This
already implies a version of Grothendieck duality up to the identification of the dualizing

In (iv), we say that a morphism is partially proper if the underlying morphism of

of such morphisms as being “without boundary”. Boundaries are a feature of analytic

1.5. By [HM24, Proposition 3.2.2(iii)], part (v) of Theorem 1.2 yields the following: for
every smooth and Kiehl partially-proper morphism f : X →Y of derived rigid spaces, of

statement of Serre duality for rigid-analytic varieties [Chi90; Put92; Bey97] in the following
three ways. Firstly, from the absolute to the relative setting: f is allowed to be an arbitrary
smooth and Kiehl partially-proper morphism, in particular, there are no restrictions on

there are no “Ext-independence” conditions as in [Put92, Main Theorem 5.1]. Thirdly, X
and Y are allowed to be derived, rather than just classical rigid spaces.
1.6. I will outline some potential applications of the main result.
For the first application, we let p be a prime and specialize to the case when the
base field is Cp. We consider a compact open subgroup K = KpKp ⊆GL2(A∞) and
denote Emerton’s completed cohomology of tame level Kp [Eme06] by ˜Hi(Kp, Cp). After
passing to locally-analytic vectors we obtain an admissible locally-analytic representation
˜Hi(Kp, Cp)la of GL2(Qp). In [Pan22], Pan constructs a certain equivariant DX-module
Ola on P1,an such that Hi(P1,an, Ola) ≃˜Hi(Kp, Cp)la.
A related construction of an
equivariant DX-module on P1,an proceeds2 by applying Ardakov’s equivariant Beilinson–
Bernstein localization [Ard21] to the dual of ˜Hi(Kp, Cp)la. In [Pan22, Remark 4.2.10],
Pan posits that the two constructions are related by some kind of Serre duality. But Ola is
not coherent, so the existing Serre duality results do not apply: to establish this relation,
we really need Grothendieck duality.
Serre duality in rigid geometry has important applications in Iwasawa theory, for
instance in the theory of explicit reciprocity laws [SV23, §4.2]. For these applications it
is important to know many functoriality properties of the duality isomorphism. This is
precisely what the six-functor formalism gives.
Serre duality can be used to construct shifted symplectic structures on moduli spaces
of perfect complexes on Calabi-Yau varieties [Pan+13]. Due to its relative nature, it is
plausible that Grothendieck duality can be used to construct families of shifted symplectic
structures. Therefore we expect that our result can be used to construct families of shifted
symplectic structures in analytic geometry.

1.7. Now I will explain the organization of this paper, as well as highlight some ancillary
results which may be of independent interest.
In §2 we develop sheaf-theoretic preliminaries. Many of these results are “folklore” in
the sense that they are already proven in the classical setting in [SGA4-1], and we merely
observe that the argument is formally the same in the higher-categorical setting.
In §3, we recall Raksit’s theory of derived algebraic contexts and the theory of derived
algebras due to Mathew, Raksit and Brantner–Mathew. This is necessary because we
do not make any assumption on the characteristic of the ground field K; for instance,
K = Fq((t)) is permitted.
Therefore, we have to exercise caution in our definition of
“derived algebras”: Raksit’s theory gives the right thing in positive characteristic.
In §4, we use the sheaf-theoretic tools developed to implement the functor of analyti-
fication, with very good functoriality properties: it is a geometric morphism of ∞-topoi.
The analytification functor is written as a composite of two geometric morphisms, using
an intermediate topos introduced in Definition 4.13. This intermediate topos plays a key
role in the remainder of the paper as it is large enough to contain objects of both algebraic
and analytic nature.
In §5, we recall some features of the functor of classical truncation introduced in
[Soo25], and also recast this as a sheaf-theoretic operation. This section is important for
backwards-compatibility. It also allows us to bootstrap results about classical rigid spaces,
to prove results about derived rigid spaces.
In §6 we define the relative spectrum, in analogy with the corresponding construction
in algebraic geometry. This is used to define vector bundles and square-zero extensions of
arbitrary stacks.
In §7 we define cotangent complexes, smooth and ´etale morphisms, and quasi-smooth
Zariski-closed immersions and prove various properties of these.
In §8, we define partially-proper morphisms and prove various properties of these. In
particular we prove structure theorems for partially-proper ´etale morphisms (Proposition
8.14) and Kiehl partially-proper smooth morphisms 8.17.
In §9, we prove the first four parts of Theorem 1.2, and half of Theorem 1.2(v): that
is, we show that f is suave, yielding the equivalence f ! ≃f ∗ˆ⊗Xf !1, but do not yet
identify the dualizing object f!1. We remark that our proof of Theorem 1.2(i) does not
use compactifications.
In §10, we construct the canonical equivalence f !1 ≃det(LX/Y )[d], using a deformation
to the normal cone. Our deformation to the normal cone is based on the construction
from [BH25; HKR25], using derived Weil restriction. The idea of using a deformation
to the normal cone to obtain this equivalence is not original to us.
As far as we are
aware, in algebraic geometry this was first used in [GR17b, Ch. 9, Corollary 7.3.2], and
subsequently in the analytic setting in [CS22; Zav26; Cam26].
The final §11 contains results which are not used elsewhere in the paper. The motiva-
tion is the following. In §10, the deformation space which is constructed is algebro-analytic
in nature: it interpolates between a Zariski-closed immersion and its algebraic normal
bundle. In this section, we show that there is an analytic deformation space: this is a
rigid-analytic space which interpolates between a quasi-smooth Zariski-closed immersion
and its analytic normal bundle.
1.8. It is reasonable to ask what the point of this paper is, given that similar results have
been obtained in the condensed setting in [CS22; Cam26]. This paper can be viewed as
evidence that the theory of Ind-Banach spaces has similarly strong applications to con-
densed mathematics; the latter has enabled many significant breakthroughs. It also shows
that certain underlying ideas of [CS22] are truly robust, so that analytic Grothendieck

Derived algebraic contexts
3.1.
The purpose of this section is the following.
Our Main Theorem 1.2 does not
impose any hypothesis on the characteristic of the ground non-Archimedean field K. For
instance, K = Fq((t)) is permitted. On the other hand, an essential component of our
proof is the the use of derived geometry. Because of this, we need to exercise caution in
positive characteristic, to ensure that we are working with the correct notion of “derived
algebras”. This is achieved via the machinery developed in [Rak26] and [BM25]. Roughly
speaking, our “derived algebras” are an Ind-Banach version of simplicial commutative
rings. The reason why we have to use this particular model, and not, for instance, E∞-
algebra objects, is because it is essential to define the derived symmetric algebra in a way
which avoids introducing higher coinvariants for the action of the symmetric group (which
do not vanish in positive characteristic).

we prefer cohomological indexing notation.
