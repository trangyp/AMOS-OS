It does what it says on the tin: safe synthetic
data from coarsened margins.
Gillian M Raab
University of Edinburgh and the Scottish Centre for Adinistrative Data Research
Abstract. This paper proposes a method of creating synthetic data
(SD) that will have two important advantages for the user compared to
other methods currently available. The first is transparency; unlike other
methods, the person in receipt of the SD will know which of the relation-
ships between variables in the original data will be approximately main-
tained in the SD. The second is a guarantee that the SD is derived from
information that has already been judged to be free of disclosure risk.
This is achieved by first defining and calculating the margins where re-
lationships between variables will be maintained in the SD. Each margin
will then be subject to statistical disclosure control (SDC) to the stan-
dards defined by the data custodian, e.g. top-coding and bottom-coding,
combination of small categories and/or modifying small counts. Further
adjustment of the curated margins is advised by coarsening all counts in
the table to multiples of the disclosure limit. These adjusted margins are
used to create SD by the Iterative Proportional Fitting (IPF) algorithm.
The practical steps involved in creating such SD are illustrated using
data from the 1901 Census of Scotland.

1
Introduction
1.1
Overview
The creation of fully synthetic data (FSD) was first proposed as a privacy en-
hancing technology by Rubin [25] in 1993. The term “fully” is here taken to mean
that all records for all variables in the potentially disclosive ground truth (GT)
data are replaced by values generated from statistical models. It was hoped that
the creation of synthetic versions of administrative data sets held by national
statistics agencies (NSAs) might help to widen access to the information that
can be obtained from such data and thus allow public sector data to be used to
influence policy for the public good.
This paper considers the ways that SD can help this goal to be achieved,
while assuring the public and data custodians in NSAs that releasing SD will
not risk any breaches of the privacy of individuals whose records are held in the
GT.

1.2
How can SD help to widen access?
Data bases of administrative data are generally held in secure environments
where they can only be accessed by staff of the NSAs who analyse them to
create national statistics and reports. Many agencies now have trusted research
environments (TREs) where policy analysts, social scientists and others can ac-
cess the data under strict supervision, without internet access and where research
results can only be taken outside the safe setting after TRE staff have checked
the output for disclosure risk [24, 11, 26]. Processes of obtaining access to TREs
can be long and difficult. A TRE is a difficult work environment as it inhibits
joint working and interpretation of data. There are a variety of possible ways
that SD can be used to overcome these difficulties.
1. REPLACEMENT Use of the FSD instead of the GT to draw policy con-
clusions, possibly with the option of having results confirmed by running
them on the GT before results are published.
2. PLANNING To use the SD to understand the variables in the data and
formulate research plans.
3. TEACHING FSD can be used to create data sets for teaching researchers
about the data held in the TRE and how to use it.
4. CODE WRITING Giving potential users of TREs access to a file with
the same structure (variable names, types and details) as the original data,
to allow code to be developed that they can use when access to the GT is
permitted.
An additional aspect is whether the SD can be accessed outside the TRE,
and if so under what conditions; freely available, only to identified individuals
or under an end-user license agreement (EULA) where the recipient of the FSD
signs up to conditions of use. The UK Data Service defines these three levels of
access as open data, safeguarded data and controlled data1. Data custodians
decide what level of access is appropriate for their FSD, based on their perception
of its potential to lead to a data breach or to a loss of reputation to the agency.

the UK Information Commissioner’s guidance on SD now states:

In the last decade there has been an explosion of developments in methods
for creating synthetic FSD, many based on machine learning (ML) methods. A
large number of websites now offer tools to create FSD. These can be either free
open-source methods or commercial sites, many claiming that their methods can
produce data that will reproduce the results that would be obtained from the
GT without any disclosure risk. For example, the open source library Synthetic
Data Vault claims that “Artificial data give the same results as real data —
without compromising privacy” , despite their methods having been shown to
perform less well than other synthesis methods [9]4.
Unsurprisingly, NSAs take a much cautious view of the ability of FSD to
give the same results as real data and to give protection against any disclosure of
personal data they hold. There have been few releases of FSD from NSAs outside
a TSE as open or even safeguarded data, except for those intended only to be
used for code development. Synthetic data sets created by the US Government
[16, 3] are only released within a TRE5. Even this limited availability of FSD
is no longer being fully supported, given the US Census Bureau’s commitment
to differential privacy (DP), see below. Statistics New Zealand, who were early
pioneers of methods of creating FSD [32] now appear to have only one project
that provides open FSD for 6 variables for a now-discontinued income survey. In
the UK there appear to be only a few examples of the release of FSD, intended
to be used to understand details of GT data, accessible outside a TRE. Two of
these are the simulacrum data on cancer outcomes available from Public Health

Regulatory Agency7. In both cases the web sites from which they can be accessed,
with the appropriate safeguards, carry extensive warnings about the possibility
that their results may differ from what would be found in the GT data. Also,

privacy (DP): a formal guarantee that limits the influence of a single record on
each of the summary statistics used in the creation of the SD. However, making
SD comply with DP can add so much noise as to make it unusable, except for
data sets where the disclosure risk of the GT data is low [6]. It has even been
argued that SD that complies with DP may still pose a disclosure risk[28]. This
paper will not consider DP SD further.

privacy risk, would be to assess its disclosure risk, and adjust the data and the
methods by which it is produced until its disclosure risk is acceptable according

1.4
UK public policy on the release of FSD
In the UK several publicly funded bodies (ADR UK, HDR UK, DARE UK,
SCADR) 11 have been established to facilitate researchers’ access to public sector
data. ADR UK’s current recommendation on the role of FSD [1], is to recommend
that data providers make low-fidelity SD available to researchers. This is defined
as FSD that does not attempt to reproduce the relationships between variables
in the GT [2]. It can be produced in two different ways. The lowest fidelity is
obtained by using only publicly available meta-data on the codes and ranges of
variables in the data. Alternatively, the univariate distributions of the variables
can be used to produce FSD that aims to preserve the univariate distributions.
In this second case the univariate distributions can first be checked to ensure that
no individual can be identified from them. In a recent consultation report [4] data
owners favoured this approach. Two examples of this type of low-fidelity FSD
are now available from the Longitudinal Eduction OUtcomes data set (LEO)12
and from Hospital Episode Statistics provided by NHS England, as part of their
artificial data pilot 13.
1.5
Creating FSD without access to the original GT records
The FSD created from univariate distributions is one example of this approach.
It can be used for developing code and does allow the user to get some experience
of the GT data, but its value for research planning or for creating teaching data
sets is limited. Another approach, designed for creating teaching data sets, is to
use the results of published analyses of GT data to generate FSD with genetic
algorithms, described by [7] and that has been used to create a teaching dataset
from the ONS Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings, linked to 2011 Census
data. [17]. The method we propose for creating FSD has much in common with
these two approaches. Knowledge of the summary data used to create the SD
allows tells the user the relationships between variables that are expected to be
retained in the SD.

– carry out SDC procedures in the GT data, such as top- and bottom-coding
for numeric variables and and grouping them into ranges, form merged cat-

– Decide on the margins to be preserved in the SD and create them from the
GT data.
– Check the margins for small cells below a disclosure limit specified by the
TRE, often the value of 5 or 10, as suggested in [24, 11, 26]. If no tables
require adjustment then proceed to the last two steps.
– Adjust the margins. Values in the tables that are below the disclosure limit
should be replaced and other cells may need to be be adjusted to prevent
disclosure from multiple tables. One option discussed for the example is to
coarsen the counts in the table so that the counts are changed to multiples
of the disclosure limit.
– Create a cross tabulation of all the variables that appear in the adjusted
marginal tables and obtain a fit to this table by Iterative Proportional Fitting
(IPF) [8, 5]
– Generate one or more synthetic tables as samples of any desired size from a
multinomial distributions with proabilities given by the fitted IPF propor-
tions and create SD(s) from these tables.
When any of the margins have been modified to conform to disclosure rules the
margins will no longer be compatible with each other, but this does not prevent
the IPF algorithm from converging, as we show from the example.
The decision on which margins to fit can be taken in various ways. An analysis
of the GT with log-linear models might be carried out to decide on important
relationships between variables. Another approach, that we illustrate below, is
to start with fitting all two-way margins and then explore whether there are any
three-way margins that have not been fitted adequately.
When a table has two or more cells below the disclosure limit, then it will be
considered non-disclosive. But if only one cell is below the disclosure limit then
the table remains disclosive, since the number in the small cell can be determined
by subtraction, and some further adjustment must be made
Where any of the margins contain zero counts, a decision needs to be made
as to whether these are structural zeros: a case that could never arise, such as
answering “yes” to “do you have a driving license” for people under 18. Non-
structural zeros should be counted as disclosive and suppressed by replacing
them with the disclosure limit. Similarly, some missing values are determined by
the structure of the data; for example number of years smoked must be missing
for non-smokers. These structural aspects are essential for any useful SD, but
we do not illustrate them here.
The replacement of random zeros with the disclosure limit also avoids the
problem of the random zero cells being reproduced in the SD. The methods ipf
and catall (for saturated models) implemented in the synthpop package[18] add
a small quantity to all the cells in the fitted probabilities. This is equivalent

This would be recommended to increase the transparency of the method. But it

2.2
Limitations
The two limitations of the IPF algorithm are the need to create a large cross-
tabulation of all the variables, and the large number of iterations that can some-
times be needed for the algorithm to converge. The eleven variables in the ex-
ample discussed below generated a table with just over 50 million cells, analysed
on modest Windows laptop 15. but adding more variables would go beyond the
memory available. With better computational resources this method could be
applied to data sets with more variables.
If the margins used to create the SD are to be published, there is the pos-
sibility of a small cell being identified by combining information from different
tables. Here is a very simple example: two-way tables with two or more values
below the disclosure limit are generally considered to be safe because we can-
not deduce the original values by subtraction from the known total of original
records. However, if we know the one-way margins of the GT data, we can dis-
cover the true counts for any tables where any row or column where there is only
a single cell below the disclosure limit. Even if the one-way tables are not pub-
lished, they could be derived from any tables without modified cells. One way
to overcome this would be for the disclosure control staff to examine all possible
combinations of published tables to check them for disclosure by differencing, a
very tedious task if there are many tables. A more practical solution would be
to coarsen the data by expressing all the counts in the margins as multiples of
the disclosure limit. For a disclosure limit of 10, all the counts in the margins
could be replaced as follows: counts 0-9 to 10, counts 10-19 to 20, counts 20-29
to 30... etc. Differencing such tables would not correspond to the differences in
the original tables.
SD set(s) of any desired size could then be generated by IPF from proportions
calculated from these counts. The sum of the counts in each margin will exceed
the number of records in the original by a different number for each margin. A
fixed number could be subtracted from the cells in every margin to ensure that
the average total counts in all margins is close to the original sample size.
When IPF is carried out with higher order margins, it is important to include
the univariate margins for any variables that do not appear in any of other

3.2
Creating and adjusting margins
To illustrate the method, it is assumed that tables created from this data would
be subject to output checking to ensure that no counts in any table used to
create the SD were below a limit of 10. After the data curation described above
no one-way table had a count below the limit, but 29 of the 55 two-way tables
had one or more counts below the limit. Of the 1,528 cells in these 29 tables 243
(16%) were below 10. New sets of all two-way tables were created from these
margins as follows.
– Disclosure controlled Any counts below 10 in the table replaced with 9
– Coarsened to multiples of 10 , 0-9 to 10, 10-19 to 20 to etc.
– Adjusted Coarsened Adjust margins by subtracting a number from the
counts in each cell of every table to make the average counts per margin
close to the original records
The average count in the disclosure controlled margins was just 28 larger than
the sample size of the GT data. However, the coarsened margins had average
totals that were 220 greater than the GT size. By subtracting 5 from the cells
of all tables this difference was reduced to 27. Table 2 illustrates one two-way
margin with 3 counts below 10 and how it would appear in each of the three sets
of altered margins.
A SD set was generated from the fit to each of these three sets of margins
using routines in the mIPfp package for R18. Each fit followed by the creation
of the SD took around 25 minutes on the Windows 11 laptop described above.
Note that synthesis from disclosure-controlled margins would risk disclosure
of small cells if the margins were published. By comparing it to the GT data we
will be assessing whether the IPF model of two-way margins is an appropriate
generative model for this data set. The other two SD sets should prevent any
disclosure of small counts, even if all the margins are made available either
publicly or to the recipients of the SD.

3.3
Comparing the utility of the three SD sets
To compare each of the SD sets with the GT we start by evaluating how well
they reproduce tables. The measure used is the standardised propensity score
mean square error SpMSE [27]. The propensity score is calculated by combining
the rows of the observed and SD and assigning an indicator variable with the
value 1 for synthetic rows and zero for original rows. The propensity score is then
calculated from the predicted value of the indicator variable from the combined
data, as the mean value of the squared difference of the predicted values from
0.5 for the case when SD and the GT have the same number of records, N 19.
For a table with n cells containing GT counts of yi and synthetic counts si,
i = 1, ...n, the predicted value from the combined data is just (si + yi)/2 for all

tables. No table gave an SpMSE value over 10, but a few tables had values
between 5 and 10, most of which involved sex and age. Examining female heads
of households in the GT revealed a different age structure by sex and marital
status. To examine this for the SD synthetic ages would need to be created from
the grouped values in the SD. This could easily be done by sampling from the
ranges of the groups. If such analyses were of interest to those using the SD some
threeway margins could be included in the IPF fit used to create the SD.
A final check on the SD is to use it to investigate how it might be used to
fit a statistical model to predict an outcome. The outcome selected was whether
a household employed any servants, predicted by logistic regression. Such mod-
elling usually proceeds by first examining the univariate between candidate vari-
ables and the outcome (any servants). As expected from the results above the
SD gave exactly the same picture as the GT. Then a series of models could be
fitted to find one with the best explanatory power and helpful interpretation.
None of the models explored showed a different picture for the SD compared to
the GT.

Conclusions and practical recommendations
The proposed method of creating SD from coarsened margins seems to have
worked well for this example. But this paper is just a proof-of-concept excercise
and much more investigation will be needed to explore it further. It has much in
common with the method developed by the Manchester group [17, 7]. that now
has an open source, easy to use, version available 20. Their method was developed
for use in training courses, and it has been used for this. Training courses would
be an obvious use for the methods proposed here. But an additional use would
be data exploration. If a machine were available with a large RAM, then data
