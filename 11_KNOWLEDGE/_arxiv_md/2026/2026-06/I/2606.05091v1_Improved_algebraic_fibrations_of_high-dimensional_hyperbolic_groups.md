IMPROVED ALGEBRAIC FIBRATIONS OF HIGH-DIMENSIONAL
HYPERBOLIC GROUPS
GIOVANNI ITALIANO, MATTEO MIGLIORINI, AND ANDREW NG
Abstract. For every d ≥3, we construct infinitely many quasi-isometry classes of
hyperbolic groups G of cohomological dimension d that algebraically fibre with finitely
presented kernel. All our groups arise as finite-index subgroups of right-angled Coxeter
groups. In many cases, the L2-Betti numbers of the groups G provide obstructions
to higher finiteness properties of the kernel. Our groups therefore expand the list of
subgroups of hyperbolic groups with exotic finiteness properties.
1. Introduction
A group G is said to algebraically fibre if it admits a surjective map onto Z with
finitely generated kernel. This definition is motivated by the world of 3-manifolds, where
a celebrated theorem of Stallings [Sta61] establishes that, for a compact irreducible 3-
manifold, an algebraic fibration of its fundamental group is always induced by a topological
fibration of the manifold. Agol’s virtual fibring criteria [Ago08], that he used to prove that
all finite volume hyperbolic 3-manifolds virtually fibre, makes crucial use of this. In view
of this, the next natural question is whether all odd dimensional, finite volume hyperbolic
manifolds virtually fibre.
In higher dimensions, stronger hypotheses are needed to upgrade algebraic fibring to
topological fibring as, in general, a topological fibration induces a map on fundamental
groups where the kernel is of type F. Finding examples of higher dimensional hyper-
bolic manifolds whose fundamental groups algebraically fibre with intermediate finiteness
properties has been a topic of interest of late [BM22, IMM24, IM25]. The ultimate goal,
however, is to obtain examples where the manifold topologically fibres.
In his thesis
[Far67], Farrell proves a theorem, which holds in dimensions d ≥6, in a similar spirit to
Stallings’ theorem, that says that an algebraic fibring of a d-manifold group is induced
by a topological fibring if and only if the kernel is of type F and certain K-theoretic ob-
structions vanish. Following Farrell’s result, one ingredient to produce topological fibrings
would be to find algebraically fibrings where the finiteness properties of the kernel can be
improved.
Agol’s virtual fibring criterion crucially makes use of the notion of a RFRS group. We
will not need the definition of a RFRS group, but it suffices to know that all compact
special groups in the sense of Haglund and Wise [HW08] are RFRS. It is known that
arithmetic hyperbolic manifolds of first type have fundamental groups which are cubulated
[BW12], hence virtually compact special [Ago13] and therefore virtually RFRS. Wise
conjectured that all finite volume hyperbolic manifolds are cubulated [Wis20, Section
17.b]. In view of this, a lot of research has focussed on the special case of fibring manifolds
whose fundamental group is RFRS.

Kielak’s theorem provides techniques to prove (1), and Fisher’s theorem allows us to

(2), partly because it appears to be quite resistant to purely homological methods. It is
therefore interesting to understand more broadly fibrations of hyperbolic RFRS groups
with the goal of bringing these techniques back to the setting of hyperbolic manifolds. 1
Recently Lafont, Minemyer, Sorcar, Stover, and Wells [LMS+25] constructed explicit
examples of hyperbolic right-angled Coxeter groups that virtually algebraically fibre, in
each virtual cohomological dimension d ≥2. Their result relies on a recursive construction,
which is an adaptation of a thickening due to Osajda [Osa13] to access high cohomological
dimensions, and on the combinatorial game of Jankiewicz, Norin and Wise [JNW21] to
ensure the virtual algebraic fibring. These groups are not fundamental groups of manifolds,
but they are the first example in which algebraic fibring is shown to happen in hyperbolic
groups of arbitrarily high dimension.
In this paper we strengthen the result of [LMS+25] by proving the following.
Theorem A. For every d ≥3, there exist infinitely many isomorphism classes of hyper-
bolic groups of cohomological dimension d that algebraically fibre with finitely presented
kernel.
All our examples arise as finite index subgroups of right-angled Coxeter groups. Our
method is to combine the strategy of [LMS+25] with a novel coning construction, which
we call collar-coning, that allows us to modify simplicial complexes to be simply-connected
while maintaining the hyperbolicity of the right-angled Coxeter group that they define.
The main strength of Theorem A is promoting the algebraic fibring by proving (2) of
the strategy outlined above for an infinite family of groups. To the best of our know-
ledge, before this paper there were only some sparse examples of hyperbolic groups of
cohomological dimension d > 3 for which F2-algebraic fibring has been already proven,
see [IMM23,IM25,IMM24]. Our examples also contribute to the list of hyperbolic groups
with finitely presented subgroups that have exotic finiteness properties [Bra99, Lod18,
Kro21,IM25,IMP24,LIP24]. Our construction has the advantage of providing an infinite
family, with precise control over the dimension, as well as the nice combinatorial structure
coming from the ambient right-angled Coxeter group.

Moreover, using the same technique as in recent work of Cashen, Dani, Schreve, and
Stark [CDSS25], we can distinguish infinitely many quasi-isometry classes of such groups
in every fixed dimension. Our theorem is therefore upgraded to the following.
Theorem B. For every d ≥3, there exist infinitely many quasi-isometry classes of hyper-
bolic groups of cohomological dimension d that algebraically fibre with finitely presented
kernel.
The finitely presentability of the kernel implies the vanishing of the first two ℓ2-Betti
numbers of the group [Fis24]. Since the Euler characteristic of a group is equal to the
alternating sum of its ℓ2-Betti numbers, its non-vanishing allows us to obstruct some
finiteness properties. In particular, we get the following.
Theorem C. For every n ≥3, there exist infinitely many quasi-isometry classes of
hyperbolic groups of cohomological dimension n that algebraically fibre with kernel of
type F2 but not Fn. Moreover, if n is even, then the same result with Fn−1 in place of
Fn holds.
We believe our result is new even in dimension 3, although of course the most interesting
case of hyperbolic groups of cohomological dimension 3, namely hyperbolic 3-manifolds,
is dealt with by Agol’s resolution of the virtual fibring conjecture [Ago13].
Corollary D. There exist infinitely many quasi-isometry classes of hyperbolic groups of
cohomological dimension 3 that algebraically fibre with kernel of type F2 but not F3.
In the special case n = 4 of Theorem C, we obtain the following corollary, where the
behaviour significantly differs from the case of hyperbolic manifold case.
Corollary E. There exist infinitely many quasi-isometry classes of hyperbolic groups of
cohomological dimension 4 that algebraically fibre with kernel of type F2 but not F3.
Previous work of Kropholler [Kro21] produced examples of cohomological dimension 3,
but in the closing remarks of the paper Kropholler discusses the difficulties of using his
methods to obtain the analogous result in higher cohomological dimensions.
To the best of our knowledge, these are the first examples of hyperbolic groups of
cohomological dimension 4 that fibre with kernel of type F2, and even the first examples of
hyperbolic groups of cohomological dimension 4 with vanishing second L2-Betti number.
This contrasts with the case of hyperbolic 4-manifolds, where the proof of the Singer
conjecture for such manifolds [Dod79] implies that the second L2-Betti number is non-
zero (and in fact the only non-zero L2-Betti number). Moreover, the Singer conjecture
predicts that the same is true for all fundamental groups of closed 4-manifolds that admit
a metric of strictly negative sectional curvature.

2. Preliminaries
2.1. Right-angled Coxeter groups.
Definition 2.1. Given a simplicial graph Γ with vertex set V and edge set E, the asso-
ciated right-angled Coxeter group WΓ is the group with the following presentation:
WΓ := ⟨v ∈V | v2 = 1 ∀v ∈V, [v, w] = 1 ∀(v, w) ∈E⟩
There is a cube complex XΓ associated to a right-angled Coxeter group WΓ, called the
Davis complex. It is a CAT(0) cube complex, whose 1-skeleton corresponds to the Cayley
graph of the group WΓ (with respect with the set of generators given by the vertices of Γ,
and after having identified every bigon to a single edge), and such that the link of every
vertex is isomorphic to the flag completion L of the graph Γ. A more complete description
can be found in [Dav08].
Using the identification with the Cayley graph, every edge of XΓ is labelled by one of
the generators of the group. For this reason, there is a canonical isomorphism between
the link of a vertex of XΓ and L, which respect the labelling.
It is a well known fact that hyperbolicity of a right-angled Coxeter group can be verified
from a combinatorial condition on the defining graph.
Proposition 2.2. [Mou88] A right-angled Coxeter group WΓ is word-hyperbolic if and
only if its defining graph Γ has no induced squares.
2.2. The Jankiewicz-Norin-Wise combinatorial game. In [JNW21], Jankiewicz,
Norin, and Wise introduced a combinatorial game to construct virtual maps from a right-
angled Coxeter group to Z, in a way that is conducive to studying finiteness properties
of the kernel. The main underlying tool for this analysis is Bestvina-Brady Morse theory
[BB97]. We use a slight modification of it, and we briefly summarize the procedure here.
Definition 2.3. Let Γ be a simplicial graph.
(1) A state on a simplicial graph Γ is the partition of its vertices into two sets, called
I and O, standing for “IN” and “OUT”.
(2) A (coloured) system of moves on Γ is a partition of the set of vertex in a finite
number of colours V = Fc
i=1 Vi. The sets V1, . . . , Vc are often referred to as the
colours {1, . . . , c}, or the moves. In this paper we will only consider system of
moves which are sparse, i.e. for which adjacent vertices belong to different moves.

Moves act on states in the following way. Given the move mi and a state S, the state

The (coloured) JNW combinatorial game starts with the assignment of a (coloured and
sparse) system of moves, and the choice of a starting state S0 on Γ. Let c be the number
of moves in the system, and let α: WΓ →(Z/2Z)c the homomorphism that maps every
generator v to the element of the canonical basis em(v) ∈(Z/2Z)c corresponding to the
move m(v) assigned to v. Let G′ = ker α, and X′ = G′\XΓ. Note that G′ is a finite-index
subgroup of WΓ (of index 2c), X′ is a non-positively curved cube complex with 2c vertices,
and such that π1(X′) = G′. Moreover, X′ inherits the labelling of the edges from XΓ, and
so in particular also the natural isomorphisms of each vertex links with the flag completion
L of Γ.
(1) Fix x0 ∈X′ a base vertex. On the link of x0 we assign the starting state S0.
Since every edge of X′ is labelled by a v ∈V (Γ), it is also labelled by the move
associated to v. We can now propagate the state s0 to the other vertices. By
choosing a path connecting x0 to another vertex x, and applying all the moves
appearing in the edges of the paths to s0, we obtain a new state s. It is possible
to verify that s does not depend on the choice of the path, and it is therefore a
well-posed definition for a state on x.
(2) Each edge of X′ inherits opposed status I and O at the two vertices, this allows
us to define a direction on it (going from OUT to IN). The fact that the system of
moves is sparse ensures that on every square parallel edges get the same direction.
(3) This coherence property on parallel edges implies that each cube has exactly one
vertex where all edges have arrows that are going out. We can identify each d-cube
with the standard cube [0, 1]d, by setting this vertex to be (0, . . . , 0).
(4) Following the directions on the edges, we define a diagonal map onto S1 on
each cube of X′, using the composition [0, 1]d →R →R/Z = S1 given by
(x1, . . . , xd) 7→P xi and the projection to the quotient.
(5) This map is a Bestvina-Brady Morse function, and its ascending (resp. descend-
ing) links correspond to the subcomplex of ˜Γ spanned by the vertices labelled with
O (resp. I). This induces a map G′ = π1(X′) →π1(S1) = Z, and we can infer

2.3. Thickening and high virtual cohomological dimension. The cohomological
dimension of a group G is defined as the supremum of the n such that Hn(G, M) ̸= 0 for
some ZG-module M. If G has torsion, its cohomological dimension is always cdG = ∞.
For virtually torsion-free groups, the usual notion of dimension that is used is virtual
cohomological dimension, denoted vcdG, which is the cohomological dimension of any
torsion-free finite index subgroup of G (this is indeed independent of the choice of such
subgroup).
The virtual cohomological dimension of a right-angled Coxeter group can be explicitly
computed from the defining graph Γ.

There are examples of hyperbolic right-angled Coxeter groups of arbitrarily high virtual
cohomological dimension. This fact was somewhat surprising at the time of discovery,
as it does not hold for Coxeter polytopes, nor for Coxeter groups which are Poincaré
duality groups (see, for example, [Vin85]). It was indeed conjectured that there should
be an universal bound on the virtual cohomological dimension of any word hyperbolic
Coxeter group. The conjecture first appeared in print in Moussong’s thesis [Mou88] and
was disproved by Januszkiewicz–Świątkowski [JŚ03] and later independently by Osajda
[Osa13], who constructed families of hyperbolic Coxeter groups of every possible virtual
cohomological dimension. Both of their constructions are purely combinatorial.
We recall Osajda’s construction, as our argument relies on a modification of it.
Definition 2.6. Given a cube complex X, the thickening Th1(X) is the flag simplicial
complex with the same set of vertices as X, and where vertices v0, . . . , vn span a simplex
if and only if they are contained in a common cube in X.
Osajda’s construction proceeds recursively in the following way:
(1) We start with a finite flag-no-square simplicial complex ∆n with Hn−1(∆n) ̸= 0,
take Γn to be its 1-skeleton, Wn to be the right-angled Coxeter group associated
to Γn, and Xn its Davis’ complex.
(2) We select a torsion-free subgroup H < Wn of sufficiently large finite index. Then
the quotient Yn = Xn/H is a negatively curved cube complex, and we can prove
that Hn(Yn) ̸= 0.
(3) We define ∆n+1 = Th1(Yn) to be the thickening. Then ∆n+1 is a finite flag-no-
square simplicial complex with Hn(∆n+1) ̸= 0, and we can proceed inductively.

3. Thickening and JNW game
In this section we recall the main constructions and results of [LMS+25] that are needed
for this paper.
In [LMS+25] the authors introduce the α-thickening to make the Jankiewicz–Norin–
Wise combinatorial game work. For our purposes, it is convenient to split this construction
as a composition of Osajda’s thickening (see Definition 2.6) and an operation defined on
simplicial complexes that we call α-enlargement.
Definition 3.1. Let M be a simplicial complex, and let α: Y →M (0) be a surjective
map. The α-enlargement is a simplicial complex eα(M), equipped with a simplicial map
¯α: eα(M) →M, defined as follows.
• The vertex set of eα(M) is Y .
• Vertices v0, . . . , vn of eα(M) span a simplex if and only if {α(v0), . . . , α(vn)} is
the vertex set of a simplex in M.
• The map ¯α is the unique simplicial map that extends α.
Remark 3.2. Note that a surjective simplicial map f : N →M arises as an α-enlargement
if and only if preimages of simplices in M are simplices in N. In this case, it is easy to
see that N = eα(M), where α = f|N(0).
The α-thickening Thα(X) of the cube complex X, described in [LMS+25], coincides
with eα(Th1(X)).
If α is the identity map, then Thα(X) coincides with the Osajda
thickening Th1(X).
Lemma 3.3. The map ¯α: eα(M) →M is a homotopy equivalence.
This property is standard and was also used in [LMS+25]; we recall the proof for the
reader’s convenience.
Proof. Choose any embedding ι: M →eα(M) so that ¯α ◦ι is the identity. We claim
eα(M) collapses to the image of ι. To see this, note that if v is a vertex not in the image
of ι, then its link is a cone on ι(α(v)), so the removal of the open star of v can be achieved
by a sequence of simplicial collapses. Iterating this procedure yields the claim, which in
turn proves the lemma.
□

For the thickening, the authors consider in particular the map ψ: Y →M (0), where
Y = {(v, w) ∈M (0) × M (0) : d(v, w) ≥2},
and ψ(v, w) = v is the projection onto the first factor. The map ψ is surjective as long as
M is not a cone, that is, no vertex of M is adjacent to every other vertex of M.
The following was implicitly proved in [LMS+25] for k = 0, and its generalisation to
every natural k has a similar proof.
Proposition 3.6. Let M be a simplicial complex, and let k ∈N. Assume that M is not
a cone, and has no k-disconnecting simplices. Then the Coxeter group associated to the
enlargement eψ(M) virtually fibres with kernel of type Fk+1.
Proof. Consider the colouring of the vertices of eψ(M) obtained by partitioning them into
pairs of the form {(v, w), (w, v)}. A balanced state is a state where vertices of the same
colour have opposite states. It is straightforward to see that the orbit consists of all the
balanced states. Choose any balanced state as the initial state.
We want to prove that the orbit is k-legal, that is, all the ascending and descending
links are k-connected. We show it for the ascending links, as for the descending links it is
analogous.
Fix a state of the orbit, and let Z be the subcomplex of M spanned by vertices v such
that (v, w) has status O for some w. Now note that any two vertices v, w of M \ Z are
necessarily connected by an edge. If that were not the case, then (v, w), (w, v) would both
have status I, which contradicts the assumption that we started with a balanced state.
Hence, Z is of the form M \ σ for some simplex σ, and is by hypothesis k-connected.
Since the ascending link is a thickening of Z and is therefore homotopy equivalent to it,
we conclude by the main result of [BB97].
□
We can generalise this result to any enlargement that is large enough. To formalize this,
for a given simplicial complex M and for every h ∈N define πh : M (0)×{1, . . . , h} →M (0)
to be the projection.
Theorem 3.7. Let M be a simplicial complex, and let k ∈N. Assume that M is not
a cone, and has no k-disconnecting simplices. Then for h ≥|M(0)| the Coxeter group
associated to the enlargement eπh(M) virtually fibres with kernel of type Fk+1.
Proof. Let ψ: Y →M (0) be the enlargement map used in Proposition 3.6. Since h ≥
|M (0)|, we can find an injective map ι: Y →M (0) × {1, . . . , h} such that πh ◦ι = ψ. This
uniquely extends to an embedding ¯ι: eψ(M) →eπh(M), satisfying πh ◦¯ι = ¯ψ.
We define the following colouring on the vertices of eπh(M). On the image of ι, we use
the colouring induced by the colouring on eψ(M) described in the proof of Proposition 3.6.
All other vertices are coloured with distinct colours, i.e. a vertex outside the image of ι
does not have the same colour as any other vertex. We choose again any balanced state
as initial state.
The same argument as in Proposition 3.6 allows us to conclude that all ascending and
descending links are k-connected.
□

Definition 4.2. For n ≥5, define the collared cone on an n-cycle to be the flag-no-square
triangulated disk C constructed as following:
(1) Start from an annulus, and subdivide it into n squares, so that its boundary
components are n-cycles. This is the collar of the collared cone.
(2) Add a diagonal to each square such that no two diagonals have the same endpoint.
(3) Finally cone one of the boundary components of the collar to a point, see Fig. 1.
Let M be a simplicial complex, and let γ be an n-cycle in M. ‘Collar-coning the n-cycle
γ’ refers to the procedure of attaching the boundary of the collared cone C to γn.
Remark 4.3. The collared cone on the pentagon is the complement of the star of a vertex
of an icosahedron, see Fig. 2.
More generally, the collared cone on the n-gon is the
complement of the star of a vertex in the dual of the Löbell polyhedron L(n) [Löb31].
The crucial property that will allow our construction to work is the fact that the Löbell
polyhedra can be represented as right-angled polyhedra in H3.

5. Proofs of the main results
In this section we prove the results stated in the introduction.
Proof of Theorem B. For every d ≥3, [LMS+25] show the existence of flag-no-square
simplicial complexes with no 0-disconnecting simplices that define a hyperbolic RACG of
vcd = d. Applying Theorem 4.1 gives rise to flag-no-square simplicial complexes with no
1-disconnecting simplices of vcd = d. By Theorem 3.7, the corresponding enlargement
virtually fibres with finitely presented kernel. This already yields Theorem A.
For l ≥5, the graph product defined on a cycle of length l with vertex groups (Z/2Z)N
is a cocompact lattice GN,l in a Bourdon building [Cap09]. See [Bou97] for the definition of
a Bourdon building. For fixed l, the conformal dimension of the boundary of the building,
and hence that of the group GN,l, goes to infinity with N. We note that although Bourdon
originally considers graph products with vertex groups Z/2NZ, these are commensurable
to the graph products with vertex groups (Z/2Z)N [Kim12, LP26, JŚ01], hence have the
same conformal dimension.
We may assume that the input complex X contains an l-cycle for some l ≥5 (c.f.
[LMS+25, Remark 1.1]). By Theorem 3.7 we can choose an enlargement eπN (X) so that
the thickened complex defines a word-hyperbolic Coxeter group G that contains GN,l as
a special subgroup for all N sufficiently large. Since special subgroups are convex, by
monotonicity this gives a lower bound on the conformal dimension of G. Letting N tend
to infinity gives infinitely many different values for the conformal dimension of G, so no
two are quasi-isometric.

5.2. Exotic finiteness properties. In this subsection we prove the results concerning
exotic finiteness properties of the kernel.
Proof of Theorem C. As in the proof of Theorem B, we start with a flag-no-square sim-
plicial complex M coming from [LMS+25], collar-cone cycles of M, and finish with the
final enlargement. We show that it is possible to obtain a group with non-zero Euler
characteristic by simply extending the coning phase of our construction.
Let M be a flag-no-square simplicial complex. Adding an m-simplex to M changes the
Euler characteristic of the RACG associated to eπh(M) by adding a term that, when h is
large, can be approximated by (−1)m+1 by Proposition 5.3. The procedure of collar-coning
an n-cycle adds n + 1 vertices, 4n 1-simplices, and 3n 2-simplices; therefore, collar-coning
one more n-cycle of M decreases the Euler characteristic of the constructed group by
approximately 1.

6. Further directions
Even though it is likely that almost all our groups already fail to satisfy property F3,
it might be possible to generalise our collar-coning technique to ensure the absence of
2-disconnecting simplices, therefore obtaining a different family of groups fibring with
kernels having property F3.
Concretely, we may view the collar-cone as a truncation of a flag-no-square triangulation
of S2 by removing one of the vertices (see Remark 4.3 and Fig. 2). One might attempt
to cone generators of H2 with a truncation of a flag-no-square triangulation of S3 to
ensure there are no 2-disconnecting simplices. This construction would involve a much
higher combinatorial complexity, as this would require us to cone triangulations of S2,
which are more complicated than triangulations of S1. While in principle it is possible
to envision pushing a similar strategy to even higher finiteness properties, the fact that
there are no flag-no-square triangulations of Sn for n ≥4 [PŚ09] implies that this would
likely require using objects that are not topologically a disc to perform the filling. This
added topological complexity, on top of the combinatorial complexity, leads the authors
to believe this procedure is out of reach with the current technology.
We have shown above that in fact there are many fibrations of hyperbolic groups where
the kernel is finitely presented. However, as mentioned in the introduction, the ultimate
goal is to produce fibrations with kernel of type F. One necessary condition to get type F
kernel is that the group must have vanishing Euler characteristic. It would be interesting
to construct examples where the Euler characteristic is zero, especially in view of the
difficulties with generalising the coning construction to higher dimensions as discussed
above; however it seems unlikely that the groups constructed in [LMS+25] have zero
Euler characteristic, and the coning procedure does not give fine enough control on the
Euler characteristic to construct such an example.
