ABSTRACT
We analyze KMT-2025-BLG-2093, with angular Einstein radius θE = 13.1 ±
2.8 µas, which makes it the second isolated microlens that lies in the “Einstein
Desert” (9 µas < θE < 25 µas) between free-ﬂoating planets (FFPs) on one side
and brown dwarfs and stars on the other. We discuss how its characteristics may
give clues to future exploration of FFPs, especially in the era of satellite missions
that have a major FFP focus, including Earth 2.0 and Roman.

Introduction
Free-ﬂoating planet candidates (FFPs) have been recognized as a class based on a gap
between them and the traditional stellar population of microlenses, as measured by a charac-
teristic parameter, either the Einstein timescale tE (Mr´oz et al. 2017) or the angular Einstein
radius θE (Ryu et al. 2021; Gould et al. 2022; Koshimoto et al. 2023). These two quantities
are related by

However, they enable a somewhat cleaner sample

particularly fast µrel rather than a particularly small θE, whereas this issue is directly resolved
if θE is measured.
In the context of θE measurements, Ryu et al. (2021) designated the gap as the “Einstein
Desert”, and Gould et al. (2022) characterized it as the interval, 9 µas ≲θE ≲25 µas, based
on the complete absence of events in this interval, not only in their own sample, but in
all historical events that had θE measurements at that time. By today, there have been a
total of 12 published FFPs lying below the Einstein Desert1, but only one within it. That
exception was the spectacular event KMT-2024-BLG-0792/OGLE-2024-BLG-0516, which
was just published this year (Dong et al. 2026).
There is nothing sacred about the shores of the Einstein Desert.

the nearer one would have θE = 11 µas (in the Einstein Desert). The previous “complete
absence” of Einstein Desert objects was almost certainly due to a combination of physical

Here we present the second such an intermediate-θE object, KMT-2025-BLG-2093, which
is also the ﬁrst whose physical nature remains unresolved.
2.
Observations
KMT-2025-BLG-2093 was discovered by the Korea Microlensing Telescope Network
(KMTNet, Kim et al. 2016), which employs three 1.6m telescopes, each with (2 deg)2 cam-
eras that are located at CTIO in Chile (KMTC), SAAO in South Africa (KMTS) and
SSO in Australia (KMTA). The event occurred in the overlapping region of ﬁelds KMT02
and KMT42, where the nominal rate of I-band observations is Γ = (4.0, 6.0, 6.0) hr−1 for
(KMTC,KMTS,KMTA), respectively. While some V -band observations were taken during
the event, these are not useful due to high extinction.

3.
Light Curve Analysis
After re-reduction using the tender loving care (TLC) pySIS pipeline of Yang et al.
(2024), KMT-2025-BLG-2093 was found to be well ﬁt by a single-lens single-source (1L1S)
model with ﬁnite source eﬀects, that is, it was a ﬁnite-source point-lens (FSPL) event. See
Figure 1. The parameters of this ﬁt (t0, u0, tE, ρ, Is) are given in Table 1. Here, t0 is the time
of closest lens-source approach, u0 is the impact parameter (normalized to θE), ρ = θ∗/θE is
the normalized source radius, and Is is the source ﬂux expressed in magnitudes, within an
instrumental system in which I = 18 corresponds to fs = 1. Note that because of the “long”
timescale, tE = 1.36 d, the event would fall on the long-side of the timescale gap in the Mr´oz
et al. (2017) distribution, which is centered at tE ∼0.5 d. See their Figure 2. Hence, it would
not have been further investigated if only the tE information had been available.
However, because there is a ρ measurement, it is possible to gauge the signiﬁcance of
the event within a θE framework.
4.
Source Properties
The usual method for determining θ∗(and so θE = θ∗/ρ) is to measure the source color
and magnitude from the light curve, [(V −I), I]s, in the instrumental bands, and then place
the source on an instrumental color-magnitude diagram (CMD) based on the neighboring
ﬁeld stars (Yoo et al. 2004). Then one measures the oﬀset from the centroid of the red
clump, ∆[(V −I), I] = [(V −I), I]s −[(V −I), I]cl, and so infers the dereddened source color
and magnitude [(V −I), I]s,0 = [(V −I), I]cl,0 + ∆[(V −I), I] using the known dereddened
color (Bensby et al. 2013) and magnitude (Table 1 of Nataf et al. 2013) of the clump,
[(V −I), I]cl,0 = (1.06, 14.39). One then translates this result to [(V −K), K]s,0 using the
color-color relations of Bessell & Brett (1988) and ﬁnally infers θ∗using the color/surface-
brightness relations of Kervella et al. (2004).
We are able to carry out the ﬁrst part of this procedure, but only for the magnitude,

Lack of a source-color measurement is a relatively frequent problem in microlensing
analyses, sometimes (as in the present case) due to high extinction, but from various other
causes as well. For FFPs, in particular, the events can be so short that the lower-cadence
second-band observations that are usually taken simply do not cover the signiﬁcantly magni-
ﬁed portions of the event. For example, this will likely be an issue for the Roman microlensing
survey (Penny et al. 2019; Johnson et al. 2022), which is expected to be a powerful probe of
FFPs. There are various methods to address this problem. The one we will use here is to
infer the color from magnitude, which is based on two facts: ﬁrst, microlensing sources are
nearly always in the bulge and hence at a narrow range of distance moduli, and second, that
the relation between color and absolute magnitude is usually relatively tight. For example,
Koshimoto et al. (2023) used this approach in their FFP search of MOA data.

5.
Search for a Host
By deﬁnition, an FFP event is one in which the planetary signal is clearly detected
but there is no clear evidence in the light curve of a putative host of the planet.
Such
evidence would most likely be a distortion of the Paczy´nski (1986) like FFP bump due to
the gravitational shear from the host (as described, for example, by Gould & Loeb 1992), or
a well-separated low-amplitude “bump” (also with a Paczy´nski-like form) directly generated
by the host itself. Of course, even if no such evidence is detectable, there still could be a
host that is too widely separated to betray its presence in the light curve. Thus, all FFP
candidates should be imaged at late times, when the source and the putative host could be
separately resolved in order to conﬁrm (or reject) the presence of a host.
We conduct a search for putative hosts by modeling the light curve as a binary-lens
single-source (2L1S) event, with the “planet” described in Section 3 (with characteristics
tabulated in Table 2) as the center of the system. From a mathematical point of view, this
search is completely straightforward, but it is challenging as a practical matter. The reason
is that, due to high extinction, AI ∼4.5, the observed source is extremely faint, Is ∼21.4,
so any low-amplitude excursion (for example Amax −1 ∼0.1) would have a ﬂux diﬀerence
corresponding to a ∆Ipeak ∼Is −2.5 log(Amax −1) →23.9 magnitude star. Because of the
high cadence, Γ ∼100 day−1, there could be thousands of data points over the bump, with
each one well below the threshold of even a 1 σ “detection”, but nevertheless collectively
giving rise to a formally “robust” ∆χ2 signal. It would then be diﬃcult to distinguish such a
signal from low-level systematics. This, indeed, turns out to be an issue in the present case.
We add three parameters to the search (s, q, α), that is, the host-planet separation (in
units of the θE of the host-planet system), the host-planet mass ratio, and the angle of
the source-lens trajectory relative to the host-planet axis. We ﬁnd a χ2 minimum centered
at (s, q, α) = (6.1, 275, 162◦), The remaining four parameters are essentially the “same”
as in the 1L1S solution, except that (u0, tE, ρ) are now all scaled to the system θE, which
is √1 + q ∼16.6 times bigger than the FFP θE.

The main cause for concern about the reality of this “detection” comes from the fact

four times lower signal than KMTC42. The only two other curves that show notable signal,

centered on HJD′ ∼780 (as one would expect), but an almost equal contribution from the
broad “ﬂat zone” centered on HJD′ ∼860 (for which there is no evident cause). We conclude
that while this “host signature” may be real, there is no compelling reason to believe that
it is.
The only way to convincingly detect a putative host is from late-time imaging.
6.
Discussion
Figure 4 compares KMT-2025-BLG-2093 to the 13 published FSPL FFP events (in-
cluding one that formally lies in the Einstein Desert) in terms of θE, µrel, Is,0 and maximum
magniﬁcation Amax. KMT-2025-BLG-2093 stands out in four ways: It has the second highest
Amax = 14.6, the third faintest intrinsic brightness Is,0 = 16.9, the lowest µrel = 3.6 mas yr−1,
and the second highest θE = 13.1 µas.
These characteristics are related to one another and also to the prospects for future FFP
detections. The most striking is the high peak magniﬁcation. What is shown in Figure 4
is eﬀectively the normalized peak excess ﬂux: Amax −1 = (fpeak −fbase)/fs. Leaving aside
OGLE-2023-BLG-0524, which is the only dwarf-star source in the sample, this is an order of
magnitude higher for KMT-2025-BLG-2093 than for the next highest example (Amax−1 = 1.3
for MOA-9y-5919). High values of Amax require ρ ≪1. The exact formula (ignoring limb
darkening and assuming perfect lens-source alignment, u0 = 0, is Amax = (4/ρ2+1)1/2 →2/ρ.
Because ρ ≡θ∗/θE, large Amax (small ρ) requires large θE (favored by lenses that are pushing

into the Einstein desert) and small θ∗(favored by low luminosity sources). Thus, these three
of the four extreme (or relatively extreme) characteristics are directly connected.
The fourth characteristic, low µrel, is a selection eﬀect that is induced by low θ∗, which in
turn is implied by faint Is,0. While the fraction of events for which the lens transits the source,
which is necessary to induce the ﬁnite-source eﬀects from which ρ (hence, µrel = θ∗/ρtE) can
be measured, does not depend in any way on the value of µrel, the ability to make the µrel
measurement is enhanced by low µrel because the number of data points that are impacted
by ﬁnite-source eﬀects, scales as µ−1
rel . For example, Gould (2022) found that a similar eﬀect
for bound planets causes the number of planetary events with low µrel measurements to
scale ∝µreldµrel, compared to the intrinsic distribution of events, which scale ∝µ2
reldµrel.
For giant-star sources, this selection pressure is minor because for these large stars, the
self-crossing time t∗= θ∗/µrel is long compared to the typical cadence time, Γt∗≫1, at
typical proper motions (µrel ∼2–10 mas yr−1, see Figure 5 of Gould et al. 2022) and typical
observing cadences (Γ ∼1–4 hr−1).
Thus, KMT-2025-BLG-2093 may provide a window into FSPL FFP events that are
recovered from future, more sensitive, searches. For example, the Roman microlensing survey
will probe sources with θ∗that are typically of order 20 times smaller than those monitored
by Gould et al. (2022). The main interest in this pool of sources is that it will probe to
lower θE, including θE ≲θ∗. However, there will also be many events with θE > θ∗and even
θE ≫θ∗. Especially for the latter group most will lack θE measurements, while those with
such measurements, like KMT-2025-BLG-2093, will have Amax ≫1.
