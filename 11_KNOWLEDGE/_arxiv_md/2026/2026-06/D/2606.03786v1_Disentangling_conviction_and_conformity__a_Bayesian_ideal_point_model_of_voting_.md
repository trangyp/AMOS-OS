Disentangling conviction and conformity: a Bayesian ideal point model
of voting behaviour in online debates
Elena Candellone1,2
1Department of Methodology and Statistics, Utrecht University, Utrecht, Netherlands
2Centre for Complex Systems Studies, Utrecht University, Utrecht, Netherlands
June 3, 2026
Abstract
Online debate platforms offer a unique window into the mechanisms driving opinion formation: they capture
both explicit political preferences and the peer environment in which those preferences are expressed. In this
work, I develop a Bayesian logistic regression model, inspired by ideal point models from political science,
to disentangle two competing mechanisms of voting behaviour in online debates: conviction, driven by prior
ideological beliefs, and conformity, driven by peer influence. I apply this framework to the Debate.org dataset,
comprising approximately 341k votes across 78k debates on 48 socio-political topics. As the debate platform does
not provide predefined topic labels for each debate, I infer the topic and stance from the debate text using large
language models, and, with a Bayesian approach, I quantify the relative contribution of each mechanism. I find
substantial heterogeneity across topics: conviction dominates on issues tied to personal freedoms and lifestyle
choices, such as drug legalisation and legalised prostitution, while conformity dominates on several topics widely
regarded as paradigmatic cases of moral conviction, including abortion, gun rights, and global warming. These
results have implications for the stability of online political discourse and the design of deliberative platforms.
Keywords: online political debates; ideal point models; Bayesian inference; social influence; peer
effects.

Introduction
Online platforms have fundamentally changed the way humans discuss political content and form their opinions
about societal issues. They have created new opportunities to connect with virtually everyone with an internet
connection around the globe and to consume and produce content across a wide variety of digital platforms, ranging
from instant messaging to online debates. It has been an unprecedented avenue for both the improvement and
worsening of Western democratic institutions, creating a space for sharing and shaping political views (Adamic &
Glance, 2005; Flamino et al., 2023; T¨ornberg, 2022). Online debate platforms, in particular, aim to simulate an
idealized, Athenian democracy-reminiscent scenario: users can debate and deliberate on an online forum, rather
than in a Greek agora. From a research perspective, these platforms are invaluable tools; more traditional survey
settings often lack information on social influence, making it hard to know whether a respondent’s answer has been
conditioned by someone else. On the other side, experimental settings may not always have information on explicit
opinions; we often rely on observed behaviours rather than self-reported attitudes. Online debate platforms contain
both the opinions expressed by individuals and the social voting context in which those opinions are formed. This
makes them a uniquely transparent window into the mechanisms driving opinion formation on socio-political topics.
Two broad classes of mechanisms are thought to drive opinion expression in such settings. The first is a conviction-
driven mechanism, in which individuals rely on their prior beliefs to make a choice; the second is a conformity-driven
mechanism, in which individuals base their judgments on those of similar peers. These mechanisms, operationalised
here as ideological alignment and peer influence, respectively, are not mutually exclusive: both may operate simul-
taneously, and their relative contributions are likely to vary across topics in ways that are themselves theoretically
informative. Issues tied to personal freedoms and moral identity may be predominantly conviction-driven, topics
where social cues are particularly salient may be predominantly conformity-driven, and others may be shaped

by both in equal measure. Separating them matters because they carry fundamentally different implications: if
voting behaviour is primarily conviction-driven, opinions are stable and resistant to external influence, whereas if
it is conformity-driven, they are malleable and shaped by social cues, with consequences for the stability of online
political discourse and the susceptibility of debate outcomes to manipulation. Crucially, which regime a topic falls
into is not self-evident from its content alone; it is an empirical question that this paper addresses directly.
The conviction-driven mechanism is linked to the moral conviction literature (Skitka, 2010; Skitka et al., 2021),
which explains individual choices as grounded in a relatively stable moral value system, robust to exogenous
influences. Resistance to social influence is stronger on topics tied to core moral values (Aramovich et al., 2012).
Moreover, the conformity mechanism is linked to the social influence literature (Cialdini & Goldstein, 2004; Deutsch
& Gerard, 1955), which explains individual choices as shaped by the behavioral signals generated by peers, a
mechanism that can give rise to group-level stereotypes and polarization even in the absence of underlying ideological
differences (Seckin et al., 2026). Despite their importance, the extent to which these mechanisms come into play
has mainly been studied in the context of controlled social influence experiments (Lorenz et al., 2011; Muchnik
et al., 2013; Salganik et al., 2006). Never, to the best of my knowledge, has the interplay of such mechanisms been
incorporated in a unified framework or a non-experimental setting that distinguishes the effect of these mechanisms
across topics.
A related but distinct body of work comes from the political science literature on voting behaviour, which has
largely focused on estimating latent ideology of legislators from their votes on bills (Clinton et al., 2004; Poole
& Rosenthal, 1985). Ideal point models, reminiscent of item-response models from psychometrics (Baker & Kim,
2004), infer a latent ideological dimension from voting patterns under the assumption that legislators with similar
ideologies vote similarly. These models have been extended to social media applications by Barber´a (Barber´a, 2015;
Barber´a et al., 2015), who applied them to Twitter/X data to jointly infer the latent ideology of users and political
elites based on their reposting behaviour. This is a powerful framework to recover latent ideological positions from
interactional data, but it does not seek to decompose the drivers of voting behaviour: it does not model mutual
influence among voters, nor does it ask whether a given vote reflects prior conviction or social conformity. These
are precisely the questions this paper addresses.
To do so, I develop a Bayesian logistic regression model, inspired by ideal point models, to disentangle conviction
and conformity as drivers of voting behaviour across a broad range of socio-political topics. I apply this framework
to the DDO dataset (Durmus & Cardie, 2018, 2019), collected from the online debating platform debate.org (see
Fig. 1). On this platform, users vote PRO or CON on structured debates and can view the running vote tally
before casting their own vote, making peer influence a plausible and observable mechanism. The two mechanisms
are operationalised as predictors of voting behaviour: the topic-alignment predictor captures the similarity between
the inferred debate topic and the user’s self-reported prior beliefs, operationalising conviction; the peer-influence
predictor is derived from the prior votes of ideologically similar users, operationalising conformity. This framework
makes it possible to address the paper’s central research question empirically: To what extent do ideological align-
ment and peer influence shape voting behaviour in online debates? And how does their relative contribution vary
systematically across socio-political topics?
I find that neither mechanism dominates universally, but that their relative importance depends strongly on the
topic. Conviction-driven topics tend to concern personal freedoms and lifestyle choices, e.g., drug legalisation and
legalised prostitution, where prior beliefs are strongly predictive of voting behaviour regardless of peer environment.
More strikingly, several topics widely regarded as paradigmatic cases of moral conviction, including abortion, gun
rights, and global warming, emerge as conformity-dominated: in these debates, the voting behaviour of ideologically
similar prior voters is a stronger predictor of individual votes than prior beliefs alone.
This paradox, and its
implications for the stability of online political discourse, are discussed in Section 4.

Each debate is assigned a single topic and a stance (PRO, CON, or NEUTRAL). Debates classified as “other”

dj ∈{−1, 0, +1}K with a single non-zero entry at position tj, equal to +1 for PRO or NEUTRAL stances and −1
for CON stances. Neutral stances are grouped with PRO, as they do not convey opposition but rather descriptive
or exploratory framing (e.g., “An overview of the debate surrounding X”). In such cases, the debate presents the
topic without taking a side against it, making a positive or neutral alignment with the topic the more faithful
representation. Grouping neutral with CON would incorrectly imply opposition where none is expressed; treating
them as a separate category would produce an all-zeros debate vector, which would zero out the topic-alignment
term τij for all voters on that debate, effectively discarding potentially informative observations from the analysis.
2.3
Statistical model and Bayesian parameter estimation
2.3.1
Assumptions
My modelling choices are based on four assumptions. First, each observed vote is treated as a binary outcome,
PRO or CON, conditional on participation; abstentions and ties are excluded from the analysis.
Second, user preferences (prior beliefs) and debate content (inferred topics) are represented in a common K-
dimensional space.
Ideological proximity between a user and a debate is captured by a normalized alignment
measure based on their respective representations.
Third, peer influence is local, directional, and time-dependent. A user i is influenced exclusively by users who cast
their vote on the same debate before user i, where temporal order is determined by the vote order in the dataset.
Influence enters additively and is weighted by ideological proximity between users.
Fourth, unobserved heterogeneity at both the user and debate levels is represented via additive random intercepts,
αi and δj. These terms account for persistent individual tendencies (e.g., a general propensity to vote PRO/CON)
and debate-specific effects (e.g., debate controversiality) not explained by observed predictors.
2.3.2
Statistical model
My model specification is inspired by ideal point models widely used in political science to study legislative vot-
ing (Clinton et al., 2004; Jackman, 2001; Poole & Rosenthal, 1985), and by their adaptation to social media
settings (Barber´a, 2015; Barber´a et al., 2015). I depart from Barber´a’s framework in two respects. First, whereas
Barber´a models the influence of a political elite on a user’s majority, I model mutual peer influence among debate
participants, assuming that each user’s vote could be influenced by prior votes on the same debate. Second, my
goal is not to recover users’ latent ideological positions but to quantify the relative contribution of issue alignment
and peer influence to voting behaviour.

associated with debate j. Conditional on user i casting a vote on debate j, let yij ∈{0, 1} denote the observed
outcome, where 1 corresponds to PRO and 0 to CON. The probability of having a PRO vote is modeled using a
logistic regression with two predictors, the topic-alignment τij and the peer-influence φij:

each mechanism by comparing its odds ratio OR = eβ against a threshold of e, corresponding to a one-unit increase

Results & Discussion
3.1
Model fit and interpretation
The full model (Eq. 1), incorporating both topic-alignment (τ) and peer-influence (φ) predictors together with
the user and debate intercepts (α and δ), outperforms both nested specifications according to leave-one-out cross-
validation (LOO-CV; see Appendix A.4 and Table A2). Voting behaviour in online debates is, therefore, better
explained by a joint contribution of ideological alignment and peer influence than by either mechanism alone.
Posterior means and 90% credible intervals (CI) for all topic-specific coefficients (βτ and βφ) are reported in
Table A3 and visualised in Figures 2 and 3. The two mechanisms show substantial heterogeneity across topics.
Under the classification scheme described in Section 2.4, 5 topics are jointly-driven, 3 are conformity-driven, 4 are
conformity-only, 3 are conviction-only, 1 is weakly conformity-driven, and the remaining fall into the indeterminate
category. The following sections describe the results in detail.
3.1.1
Conviction-only topics: topic alignment without strong peer influence
Three topics exhibit a robustly strong topic-alignment coefficient, while the peer-influence coefficient does not
reliably exceed the strength threshold: affirmative action 3, drug legalisation 4, and legalised prostitution 5. In
these cases, agreement between users’ prior beliefs and the debate stance is more strongly associated with voting
behaviour than exposure to prior voters. These topics are characterised by positive values of βτ, suggesting that
users’ prior beliefs about these issues strongly predict their votes. These three topics share a common character: they
concern personal freedoms and lifestyle choices on which individuals typically hold pre-formed, morally grounded
positions (Brandt et al., 2014; Haidt, 2008).

3.1.2
Conformity-driven topics: when peer influence robustly dominates
Three topics show strong evidence that peer influence outweighs topic-alignment, with both mechanisms robustly
strong but the 90% CI of ∆t fully below zero: abortion 6, capitalism 7, and gun rights 8. For these topics, both
mechanisms are robustly active, but the behaviour of ideologically similar prior voters is the reliably dominant
force. The presence of abortion and gun rights in this group is counterintuitive, as both are canonical examples of
morally charged, identity-relevant issues (Skitka, 2010; Skitka et al., 2021). Importantly, the topic alignment is itself
strong for both: prior beliefs are not irrelevant, but peer influence is reliably larger. One possible interpretation
is that, precisely because these topics are so identity-laden, voters are especially sensitive to the cues provided by
ideologically similar peers: seeing that users who share one’s broader worldview have already voted in a particular
direction may reinforce and amplify one’s own tendency to vote the same way, yielding a large peer-influence
coefficient even when individual priors are already strong. This is consistent with recent theoretical work showing
that polarization on morally charged topics can emerge from social interaction and coherence-seeking dynamics
rather than from genuine ideological divergence (Seckin et al., 2026), with empirical work on social proof and
bandwagon effects in online environments (Muchnik et al., 2013; Salganik et al., 2006), and with the wider literature
on political identity and social conformity (Cialdini & Goldstein, 2004; Deutsch & Gerard, 1955).
3.1.3
Conformity-only topics: peer influence without strong topic alignment
Four topics exhibit a robustly strong peer-influence coefficient but a topic-alignment coefficient that does not reliably
exceed the strength threshold: Barack Obama, civil unions, global warming exists, and welfare. The clearest case
is Barack Obama 9, where the peer-influence coefficient is among the largest in the entire dataset and the topic-
alignment coefficient is indistinguishable from zero. For these topics, the social voting context is strongly associated
with individual decisions even in the absence of a robust alignment signal, suggesting that partisan identity cues
and social proof may be sufficient to determine voting behaviour (Iyengar et al., 2012; Muchnik et al., 2013).
3.1.4
Jointly-driven topics: both mechanisms robustly active
Five topics have both odds ratios robustly above the strength threshold, yet the 90% CI of ∆t straddles zero,
meaning neither mechanism can be declared the dominant one: euthanasia, gay marriage, minimum wage, national
health care, and socialism. In these cases, both coefficients are large and nearly equal, reflecting a genuine joint
contribution. These are among the most strongly contested topics in the dataset: users arrive with strong prior
beliefs and are strongly influenced by what their ideological peers have already voted.
This co-occurrence of
both mechanisms at high intensity is itself substantively informative. It suggests that for these topics, ideological
alignment and social influence are complementary. Users who are strongly aligned with a debate stance also appear
to be more, not less, responsive to seeing ideologically similar peers vote in the same direction, consistent with
theoretical accounts of belief reinforcement and echo-chamber dynamics (Cinelli et al., 2021; Sunstein, 2009).
The thematic profile of this group is also noteworthy. Gay marriage and euthanasia are morally and ideologically
charged issues on which both camps tend to hold firm, pre-formed positions. Minimum wage and national health
care are policy debates with a clearer partisan valence in the US context. Despite these differences, all five share a
common feature: they are topics on which ideological identity and social environment are likely to be mutually rein-
forcing, making it difficult for any empirical design to disentangle the two. The jointly-driven classification thus also
flags topics where the mechanisms may be structurally collinear rather than merely statistically indistinguishable.

3.1.5
Indeterminate topics
The remaining topics fall into the indeterminate class: the CI of ∆t straddles zero, and neither odds ratio reliably
clears the strength threshold. This group is heterogeneous. Some topics have moderate-to-large point estimates
for both predictors but wide credible intervals that prevent a clear verdict, such as progressive tax, environmental

3.2
Heterogeneity across macro-categories
Additional patterns emerge when grouping topics by issue area (Table 1). Within social and cultural issues, the
picture is very varied: drug legalisation and legalised prostitution are conviction-only, abortion is conformity-driven,
civil unions is conformity-only, and gay marriage and euthanasia are jointly-driven. This within-issue variation
confirms that thematic similarity does not imply a common underlying voting mechanism.
Within economy, budget, and taxes, capitalism is conformity-driven while minimum wage and socialism are jointly-
driven, with the remaining economic topics indeterminate.
The jointly-driven pattern for minimum wage and
socialism suggests that economic debates with clear ideological valence engage both prior beliefs and social cues
simultaneously.
Topics in the environment, government and politics, guns and crime, and social welfare, healthcare, and education
categories concentrate on the conformity side: global warming exists, Barack Obama, gun rights, and welfare fall
into conformity-only or conformity-driven classes.
In contrast, most immigration, trade, and globalization and
military and foreign policy topics are indeterminate with small coefficients; however, this likely reflects sparse
debate coverage for these topics on the platform (see Figure A3) rather than genuinely weak partisan effects, as
point estimates for peer influence on border fence and related topics are moderate in magnitude. The wide credible
intervals preclude strong conclusions in either direction.
Whether immigration attitudes are less susceptible to peer influence than gun rights or abortion remains an open
empirical question that this dataset is underpowered to answer. Nevertheless, among topics with sufficient debate
coverage to permit precise estimation, these results are consistent with partisan identity cues playing a stronger role
on politically salient topics (Iyengar et al., 2012); the absence of a detectable conformity signal for immigration and
military topics should be attributed to data sparsity rather than interpreted as evidence of weaker partisan dynam-
ics. Overall, these results show that the relative importance of alignment and peer influence varies systematically
across topics, rather than being uniform across issue domains.
4
Conclusion
4.1
Disentangling conviction and conformity
Every choice we make is shaped by a complex interplay of forces: the values instilled by our upbringing, the social
circles we inhabit, and the information environments we navigate. Online platforms have added a new layer to this
complexity, creating spaces where personal beliefs and social signals are constantly entangled. Debate.org was a
particularly interesting example of such an ecosystem: a platform where users voted on controversial topics, making
it an ideal setting to study how conviction and conformity shape opinion expression.
The central contribution of this work is a principled empirical decomposition of these two mechanisms: ideological
alignment and peer influence.
The results show that neither mechanism is universally dominant: the relative
importance of each varies substantially and systematically across topics. This finding aligns with the literature on
the dual-process of attitude formation, which distinguishes between systematic, deliberative reasoning from prior
beliefs, and heuristic reliance on social cues endorsed by others (Chaiken, 1980). This model provides a formal
operationalisation of both processes within a single estimable framework.

The eight-category classification introduced in this paper reveals a more nuanced picture than a simple conviction/-

than the absence of any effect. Among the remaining 16, the asymmetry favours conformity: conformity-side cate-

jointly-driven category is substantively important: topics such as gay marriage, euthanasia, and minimum wage
engage both prior beliefs and peer cues simultaneously, and cannot be meaningfully reduced to either mechanism
alone.
The practical implications for the study of online political discourse differ across mechanisms. Conviction-only
topics, where prior beliefs reliably predict voting behaviour independent of peer environment, are less susceptible
to social influence and may be more stable in the face of debate or counter-argument. Conformity-driven and
conformity-only topics, including some widely regarded as highly polarised (abortion, gun rights, global warming),
are precisely those where the voting behaviour of prior users most strongly predicts subsequent votes. This could
create the conditions for cascade dynamics (Bikhchandani et al., 1992) and potentially for manipulation of debate
outcomes through early voting patterns, with implications for the design of deliberative platforms seeking to foster
genuine opinion exchange. Jointly-driven topics present a distinct challenge for platform design: both mechanisms
must be accounted for, and interventions targeting only one may leave the other unaddressed.
4.2
The paradox of polarised topics
The appearance of abortion, gun rights, and global warming in the conformity-driven and conformity-only classes,
despite their characterisation in the literature as issues of high moral conviction (Skitka, 2010; Skitka et al., 2021),
warrants careful interpretation. Crucially, under the classification chosen in this work, both mechanisms are robustly
strong for the (former) two topics: topic-alignment is not absent, but peer influence is reliably larger. One possible
explanation is that debate.org voters, who are not the debaters themselves but third-party observers, may vote on
the debate differently from how they would form or express personal attitudes in surveys. In the voting context, the
prior vote distribution may function as a relevance or credibility signal; if the crowd of ideologically proximate users
has already voted in a particular direction, this may reinforce and amplify the individual’s own tendency to vote
the same way, yielding a large peer-influence coefficient even when individual priors are already strong (Sunstein,
2009). This interpretation is consistent with recent work on social influence in collective judgement tasks (Lorenz
et al., 2011; Muchnik et al., 2013).
An alternative explanation is that the high peer-influence estimates for these topics reflect their salience and
visibility on the platform: debates on abortion or gun rights attract many voters, increasing the denominator in
Eq. 3 and influencing the precision of the peer-influence term. Future work could separate these effects by examining
the relationship between debate popularity and coefficient magnitude.
4.3
Limitations and future directions
Several limitations should be noted. First, the inference of debate topics from text is imperfect: approximately half
of debates could not be assigned to any of the 48 topics (see Section 2.2), and the assigned topic vectors are neces-
sarily noisy representations of the debates’ ideological content. Future work might explore richer, continuous topic
representations to better capture the multidimensionality of debate content. If excluded debates are systematically
different, for instance, if they disproportionately concern niche or cross-cutting topics where neither mechanism
is strong, the indeterminate category may be underrepresented, and the estimated heterogeneity across retained
topics may overstate the true variation. We cannot fully rule out this possibility with the available data.
Second, the model assumes a linear relationship between predictors and the log-odds of a PRO vote, and therefore
cannot capture nonlinear interactions; for instance, whether the magnitude of topic-alignment moderates suscepti-
bility to peer influence. Such an interaction is theoretically motivated by the moral conviction literature (Skitka,
2010; Skitka et al., 2021), which suggests that stronger ideological commitment may amplify or dampen social
influence effects. A more flexible specification could test this hypothesis directly, and could further clarify whether
the two mechanisms underlying the jointly-driven category operate independently or reinforce one another.
Third, the estimated effects are conditional associations rather than causal quantities. Both predictors are derived
from the same user-level belief representations and observed voting sequences, and may therefore reflect overlapping

or mutually reinforcing processes. The results should accordingly be interpreted as evidence of stable statistical
regularities in voting behaviour, not as identification of distinct causal pathways.
Fourth, the debate.org user base is demographically skewed toward young, male, and politically engaged users (see
Fig. A1), which limits the generalisability of these findings to broader online populations. Replication on other
debate platforms or deliberative environments would strengthen the external validity of the framework.

A.1
User demographics and prior beliefs
Figure A1 summarises the demographic composition of the 15,840 users retained for analysis. The user base is
predominantly young, with the age distribution peaking around 18–25 years and a long right tail extending to
older age groups.
In terms of gender, the majority of users who disclosed their gender identify as male, with
female users representing a smaller but substantial share; a non-negligible fraction preferred not to disclose. The
ethnicity distribution is heavily skewed toward users identifying as white, with other ethnic groups representing
small minorities. A large proportion of users chose “Not Saying” across several demographic dimensions, reflecting
the voluntary nature of self-reporting on the platform.
Politically, the user base spans a broad ideological range. Among those who disclosed a political ideology, Conser-
vative and Liberal are the two most represented categories, followed by Libertarian and smaller groups including
Moderate, Progressive, and Socialist. Party affiliation broadly mirrors this pattern, with Republican and Demo-
cratic users being most prevalent, alongside a notable share of “Undecided” and Independent users. Regarding
religion, Atheist and Christian are the most common self-reported categories, followed by Agnostic and Catholic,
reflecting a user base that skews secular relative to the general U.S. population. On presidential preference, Barack
Obama is the most frequently cited favourite president among users who disclosed a preference, followed at a dis-
tance by “Undecided” and a variety of other figures, including Ron Paul and Donald Trump, consistent with the
platform’s active period spanning the Obama and early Trump eras.

of the broader U.S. or global population. Users tend to be young, male, white, and politically engaged, which
should be borne in mind when interpreting the generalisability of the results.
A.2
Prior beliefs
Figure A2 shows the distribution of self-reported prior beliefs across the 48 topics, separately for PRO and CON
stances. The most frequently reported topics, abortion, death penalty, gay marriage, drug legalisation, and gun
rights, are also among the most debated on the platform (see Figures A3 and A4), suggesting a coherent relationship
between users’ expressed interests and their engagement with debates. For most topics, PRO stances are more

relevant to the interpretation of the model results: topics with sparse prior belief data yield noisier topic-alignment
estimates and wider credible intervals for the corresponding coefficients.
