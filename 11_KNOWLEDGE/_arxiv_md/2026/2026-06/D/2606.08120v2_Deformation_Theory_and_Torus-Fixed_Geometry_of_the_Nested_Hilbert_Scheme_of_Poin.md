Abstract
In this paper, we give a self-contained, expository account of the nested Hilbert scheme
(A2)[n,n+1] = Hilbn,n+1(A2)
from a combination of deformation theory, torus actions, and Young diagram combinatorics. We
first recall the scheme theory and functor basics needed to define Hilbert schemes. We then use
a classic result on first order deformations to identify
TI(A2)[n] ∼= HomC[x,y](I, C[x, y]/I).
For a nested pair I ⊂J, with dimC C[x, y]/I = n + 1 and dimC C[x, y]/J = n, the tangent space
becomes a compatibility kernel
T(I,J)(A2)[n,n+1] ∼= ker(Hom(I, R/I) ⊕Hom(J, R/J) −→Hom(I, R/J)) .
The torus-fixed points are indexed by a partition λ ⊢n + 1 together with a removable corner c
of its Young diagram. This corner is not only combinatorial, but also the monomial form of a
one dimensional socle direction in R/Iλ. The blow-up map to (A2)[n] × A2 has fibres given by
projective spaces of one dimensional quotients of J/mpJ, whose torus-fixed points are addable
boxes of the smaller diagram. These two local fibres explain how the universal family, the
blow-up geometry, and Young diagram combinatorics come together in the study of the local
geometry of the nested Hilbert scheme of points. Finally, we derive the tangent weight formula
at a fixed point (Iλ, Iλ\c) in the torus convention used here. Using the standard arrow basis, we
show in the proof how the arm-leg weights are modified by the compatibility kernel through a
shortening rule determined by c. A Macaulay2 verification computes the compatibility kernel
from monomial syzygies and checks the weight formula for all partitions of size at most 16.

Introduction
Hilbert schemes of points are moduli spaces in algebraic geometry. If X is a smooth surface, the
Hilbert scheme X[n] parametrizes zero dimensional closed subschemes of X of length n. It contains
the open locus of n distinct reduced points, but also nonreduced schemes supported at fewer points.
Fogarty’s theorem says that X[n] is smooth of dimension 2n when X is a smooth surface [Fog68].
This smoothness is one reason why Hilbert schemes of points on surfaces are important examples
in algebraic geometry and also interact with representation theory and symmetric combinatorics
[Nak99, Hai01].
This paper studies the first nontrivial nested Hilbert scheme of points on the affine plane. We
write
R = C[x, y],
S = A2
C = Spec R.
The nested Hilbert scheme
S[n,n+1] = Hilbn,n+1(S)
parametrizes pairs of zero dimensional closed subschemes
Y ⊂Z ⊂S,
length(Y ) = n,
length(Z) = n + 1.
Equivalently, it parametrizes pairs of ideals
I ⊂J ⊂R,
dimC R/I = n + 1,
dimC R/J = n.
The inclusion of ideals is opposite to the inclusion of subschemes, hence I defines the larger subscheme
Z, while J defines the smaller subscheme Y .
Nested Hilbert schemes on surfaces appear frequently in enumerative geometry. For example,
Gholampour–Sheshmani–Yau construct virtual fundamental classes for nested Hilbert schemes of
points and curves on nonsingular projective surfaces [GSY20].
The local geometry of S[n,n+1] can be studied by combining deformation theory with the
combinatorics of torus-fixed monomial ideals. A tangent vector to the ordinary Hilbert scheme is
a first-order deformation of an ideal. For an ideal I ⊂R of colength n, the standard deformation
theory identification is
TIS[n] ∼= HomR(I, R/I),
see [Ser06, Proposition 3.2.1]. For a nested pair I ⊂J, the two first-order deformations must be
compatible with the inclusion. This gives the usual kernel formula for the incidence condition,
compare Cheah’s tangent space formulation for nested Hilbert schemes [Che98, Section 0.4] and
Can’s kernel formula [Can07, Section 5]:

Here ι : I ,→J is the inclusion map and π : R/I →R/J is the quotient map. This compatibility
kernel, proved in Theorem 6.1, is the main link between deformation theory and local geometry of
the nested Hilbert scheme in this paper.
The two dimensional torus
T = (C×)2
acts on S by scaling the two affine coordinates, and hence acts on both S[n] and S[n,n+1]. The
fixed points of S[n] are monomial ideals, indexed by partitions or Young diagrams. At a monomial
ideal Iλ, the ordinary tangent weights are the standard arm-leg weights, and Nakajima described
the formula in an arm-leg form [Nak99, Proposition 5.8], and Haiman uses the same weights in
equivariant localization [Hai98, Section 3]. For the nested scheme, a torus-fixed point is indexed by
a partition λ ⊢n + 1 together with a removable corner c of its Young diagram.
The corner is not only a combinatorial structure, it also has an algebraic meaning. If I ⊂J and
J/I has length one supported at a point p, then J/I is a one dimensional submodule of R/I killed
by the maximal ideal mp. Equivalently, it is a line in the local socle
Socp(R/I) = { ¯f ∈R/I : mp ¯f = 0}.
For a monomial ideal Iλ supported at the origin, the monomial socle directions are exactly the
removable corners of D(λ). Dually, the blow-up map to S[n] × S has fibres described by projective
spaces of one dimensional quotients of J/mpJ, and for monomial ideals, the torus-fixed quotient
directions are addable boxes of the smaller diagram. These two fibre descriptions explain why
removable corners, addable boxes, and first-order nested deformations are different ways of describing
the same incidence condition.
This paper has three expository goals. First, it compares the birational map from S[n,n+1]
to the universal family over S[n+1] with the blow-up map to S[n] × S. Second, it works through
the corresponding local fibre descriptions in terms of socle lines and one dimensional quotients of
J/mpJ. Third, it gives a deformation-theoretic account of the shortening rule for nested tangent
weights in the torus convention used here, via the compatibility kernel. None of these results is new:
the tangent space computation is due to Cheah [Che98, Section 2.6], Chaput–Evain studied the
same geometry using staircases and cleft pairs [CE15, Sections 2.1–2.2], and Koncki–Zielenkiewicz
explained the shortening rule in arrow notation [KZ26, Section 5.3].
We also include a Macaulay2 verification [GS]. We compute the compatibility kernel from
monomial generators and syzygies, then compare the resulting weight multiset with the Young
diagram shortening rule for all partitions of size at most 16.
Section 2 introduces scheme theory and defines Hilbert schemes of points and their first-order
deformations with a brief introduction of deformation theory. Section 3 introduces partitions, Young
diagrams, monomial ideals, corners, and addable boxes. Section 4 then introduces the torus-fixed
geometry of the Hilbert scheme S[n] on the affine plane, including the torus action and the arm-leg
tangent weights. Section 5 studies the nested scheme S[n,n+1], its relation to the universal family and
the blow-up of S[n] × S and the local fibre. Section 6 explains the compatibility kernel formula for
tangent spaces and the shortening rule for torus weights. Finally, Section 7 gives explicit examples
and explains the Macaulay2 verification.

2.1
Affine schemes and closed subschemes
For a commutative ring A, the affine scheme Spec A is the set of prime ideals of A equipped with
the Zariski topology and the structure sheaf OSpec A, see [Har77, Chapter II, Section 2]. If A is
a finitely generated C-algebra, then Spec A is the analogue of an affine algebraic variety. Hilbert
schemes remember nonreduced subschemes. For example, the ideals (x, y) and (x, y)2 have the same
underlying support in A2, namely the origin, but they define different closed subschemes.
A closed subscheme of Spec A is defined by an ideal I ⊂A, see [Har77, Chapter II, Section 3].
It is
Spec(A/I) ⊂Spec A.
The underlying set is the closed set V (I), but the quotient ring A/I also contains nilpotent and
infinitesimal structure. An element ¯f ∈A/I is nilpotent if ¯fN = 0 for some N > 0, and such
elements are the algebraic form of infinitesimal structure.
For example,
C[x, y]/(x, y) ∼= C,
whereas
C[x, y]/(x, y)2 = SpanC{1, x, y}.
Thus (x, y)2 defines a fat point of length three supported at the origin, not a reduced point. In
particular, a zero dimensional closed subscheme of A2

2.2
The functor of points
A scheme X can be studied through its functor of points
hX : (C-schemes)op −→Sets,
hX(B) = HomC(B, X).
An element of hX(B) is called a B-valued point of X. When B = Spec C, this recovers the ordinary
closed points over C. When B is nonreduced, a B-valued point can carry infinitesimal information.
This point of view is standard in moduli theory, see [Har77, Chapter II, Section 2].

Moduli spaces are most naturally defined by functors. A scheme M represents a moduli functor

2.4
The Hilbert functor and representability
Let X be a projective scheme over C. For a numerical polynomial P, meaning a polynomial with
rational coefficients which takes integer values for all sufficiently large integers, the Hilbert functor
HilbP
X : (C-schemes)op −→Sets
sends a test scheme B to the set of closed subschemes
Z ⊂X × B
which are flat over B and whose geometric fibres have Hilbert polynomial P. Here a geometric fibre
means the fibre after base change to an algebraic closure of the residue field of the base point.
Grothendieck’s representability theorem says that this functor is represented by a projective
scheme HilbP (X) [Gro61]. Equivalently, HilbP (X) carries a universal closed subscheme
U ⊂X × HilbP (X),
flat over HilbP (X), such that every flat family
Z ⊂X × B
with Hilbert polynomial P is obtained uniquely by pulling back U along a morphism
B −→HilbP (X).
This is the fine moduli property of the Hilbert scheme. Nakajima recalls this statement in the form
used for Hilbert schemes of points in [Nak99, Theorem 1.1].
For explicit constructions of Hilbert schemes of points, see Gustavsen–Laksov–Skjelnes [GLS07],
and for explicit affine open charts covering Hilbn(A2), see Huibregtse [Hui02].
2.5
Hilbert schemes of points
When the Hilbert polynomial is the constant polynomial n, the corresponding Hilbert scheme is
called the Hilbert scheme of n points and is denoted

2.6
Deformations and embedded deformations
Deformation theory studies how a geometric object varies in a family, and its infinitesimal part
represents the directions in which the object can move to first order. The basic method is to replace
the base of a family by the spectrum of a small Artinian ring, so that a single morphism out of
this spectrum captures a first-order variation. We follow Sernesi [Ser06, Chapter 1]. In this paper,
an Artinian local C-algebra means a local Artinian C-algebra with residue field C. The smallest
non-trivial such ring is the ring of dual numbers
Aε = C[ε]/(ε2),
D = Spec Aε,
which has a unique closed point and a one dimensional tangent direction recorded by ε. A morphism
out of D is exactly a choice of first-order direction, and D is the universal base for such directions.
Definition 2.3 (Deformation of a scheme). Let X0 be a scheme over C and let A be an Artinian local
C-algebra. A deformation of X0 over A is a scheme X together with a flat morphism X →Spec A
and an isomorphism

∼

of the closed fibre with X0. When A = Aε, we call X a first-order (or infinitesimal) deformation of
X0.
Flatness is what makes X a genuine family rather than an arbitrary scheme containing X0:
it forces the fibres to vary without jumps, so that the closed fibre X0 is deformed rather than
degenerated. Often one wants to deform not an abstract scheme but a subscheme of a fixed ambient
space, keeping the ambient space rigid. This is the situation relevant to our Hilbert schemes.

Remark 2.5. An embedded deformation remembers how Z sits inside the fixed ambient X, not
just the isomorphism class of Z. Forgetting the embedding turns an embedded deformation into
a deformation of Z in the sense of Definition 2.3, but two different embedded deformations may
induce the same abstract deformation. The Hilbert scheme parametrises the embedded data, so it
is embedded deformations that control its infinitesimal structure.
This is precisely the infinitesimal content of the functor of points of the Hilbert scheme from
Section 2.5. A morphism D →HilbP (X) whose closed point maps to [Z] is the same thing as
a flat family over D of closed subschemes of X restricting to Z, that is, a first-order embedded
deformation of Z. In the affine situation of this paper, where X = A2 = Spec R with R = C[x, y]

with (R ⊗C Aε)/Iε flat over Aε and Iε ⊗Aε C = I. We will make this correspondence explicit in the
next two subsections.

7.3
Computational verification
We also include a Macaulay2 code which checks the nested weight formula by computing the kernel
in Theorem 6.1 directly from monomial generators and syzygies. We summarize the mathematical
design here. The complete source is available at https://github.com/chenyangzhaoicl/master_
thesis_nested_hilbert_scheme_deformation.
The input of the computation is a pair (λ, c), where λ is a partition and c ∈C(λ) is a removable
corner. The script forms
µ = λ \ c
and the two monomial ideals
Iλ ⊂Iµ ⊂R.
Monomials are represented by exponent pairs (i, j), so the monomial xiyj is stored as the pair
(i, j). This makes divisibility, least common multiples, quotient bases, and torus weights completely
explicit.
For a monomial ideal I = (g1, . . . , gr) and another monomial ideal K, the code constructs the
vector space HomR(I, R/K) by assigning unknown coefficients to the possible images of the minimal
generators gi in the monomial basis of R/K. It then imposes the lcm-syzygy equations
lcm(gi, gj)
gi
φ(gi) −lcm(gi, gj)
gj

in R/K.
This is the computational version of Lemma 6.3. The implementation imposes the pairwise lcm-
syzygies, so in particular it includes the adjacent boundary syzygies used in the Young diagram
arrow description.
The nested tangent space is then obtained by constructing the two ordinary Hom spaces
HomR(Iλ, R/Iλ),
HomR(Iµ, R/Iµ),
and imposing the compatibility equations

The torus weights are computed from the same convention used in the paper. If a variable sends
a source monomial xiyj to a target monomial xrys, then its additive weight is
(i −r, j −s),

weights. For each weight, the multiplicity is computed as

using exact rational linear algebra.

dependently from the shortening formula in Theorem 6.5. The comparison therefore checks two
different descriptions of the same tangent representation: the compatibility kernel that comes from
deformation theory and the Young diagram shortening rule.
