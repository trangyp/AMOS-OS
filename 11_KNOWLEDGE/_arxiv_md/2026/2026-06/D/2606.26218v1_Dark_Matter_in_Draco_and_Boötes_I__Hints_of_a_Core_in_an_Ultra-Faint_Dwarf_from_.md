ABSTRACT
The density profiles of dwarf spheroidal galaxies are among the most sensitive probes of dark matter physics, yet
extracting them from noisy stellar kinematics remains a fundamental obstacle. We present GraphNPE, a simulation-
based inference method for dynamical mass modeling that incorporates measurement uncertainties and spectroscopic
selection functions in the forward model. Using mock data, we show that methods relying solely on line-of-sight
velocity dispersion are biased toward cuspy density profiles, even in the absence of the mass-anisotropy degeneracy.
By accessing higher-order velocity moments, particularly line-of-sight kurtosis, GraphNPE breaks key degenera-
cies and recovers density profiles with substantially less bias. We apply GraphNPE to Draco and Boötes I using
MMT/Hectochelle and DESI for Draco, and the S5 survey for Boötes I. For each, we report density profiles and
dark matter J- and D-factors. For Draco, GraphNPE yields consistent results across datasets, marginally preferring
a cuspy inner profile (ρ150 ∼1.6−1.9×108 M⊙kpc−3) in agreement with literature. On DESI, however, second-order
Jeans modeling fits the dispersion but fails to reproduce the kurtosis, demonstrating higher-order moments are essen-
tial. For Boötes I, limited statistical power prevents definitive determination of the inner slope. GraphNPE recovers
ρ150 = 0.36+0.15
−0.11 × 108 M⊙kpc−3, significantly lower than literature and consistent with a cored inner profile. This
places Boötes I among the lowest density dwarfs at comparable stellar masses.

The mass density profile of dwarf galaxies provides a direct

DM models can further accelerate this process and create
even stronger cusps (Shen et al. 2021, 2024; Gemmell et al.
2024; Roy et al. 2024).

DM inferences from density profile measurements face sev-
eral systematic uncertainties. Stellar feedback, gas outflows,
and/or dynamical friction from sinking gas, stellar or even
DM subhalo clumps can modify the inner density profiles
and create degeneracies with DM models (El-Zant et al. 2001;
Nipoti et al. 2004; Nipoti & Binney 2015; Goerdt et al. 2010;
Di Cintio et al. 2014a; Pontzen & Governato 2014; Di Cintio
et al. 2014b; Chan et al. 2015; El-Badry et al. 2017; Fitts
et al. 2019; Read et al. 2019; Lazar et al. 2020; Orkney et al.
2021; De Leo et al. 2024). In case of DM indirect detection,
astrophysical backgrounds can also mimic signatures of DM
interactions in indirect detection searches (Abazajian 2011;
Gordon & Macías 2013; Lee et al. 2016; Bartels et al. 2016;
Macias et al. 2018, 2019; Gautam et al. 2022). In dwarf
galaxies, extragalactic and foreground Milky Way emission
can contribute significant background (Alvarez et al. 2020),
while internal backgrounds are typically limited to specific
cases, such as globular clusters that may be stripped dwarf
nuclei (Gray et al. 2025) or gas-rich, massive dwarfs such as
Sagittarius (e.g. Crocker et al. 2022) or the Small Magellanic
Cloud (Caputo et al. 2016).
Satellites around the Milky Way are particularly valuable
targets because they largely circumvent these issues: their
quenched star formation and relatively low baryon fractions
minimize the impact of baryonic feedback (Peñarrubia et al.
2012; Weisz et al. 2014; Read et al. 2016; Putman et al.
2021; Geha et al. 2024). Furthermore, their proximity en-
ables detailed spectroscopic observations, allowing for precise
measurements of stellar kinematics that trace the underlying
gravitational potential.
The traditional approach for inferring mass density pro-
files employs the Jeans equations (Jeans 1915; Bonnivard
et al. 2015). Under the assumptions of dynamical equilib-
rium, the Jeans equations relate the velocity dispersion (i.e.
the second velocity moment) to the underlying gravitational
potential and the velocity anisotropy of the tracer popu-
lation. For a given density profile and anisotropy model,
the predicted line-of-sight velocity (LOSV) dispersion profile
σlos(r) can be computed numerically and compared to the
observed stellar kinematics. The mass parameters are then
inferred by constructing a Gaussian likelihood and perform-
ing Bayesian inference, typically using Markov Chain Monte
Carlo (MCMC) or nested sampling methods (Strigari et al.
2008). Jeans methods are fast and straightforward to imple-
ment, and have been extended beyond spherical symmetry
to triaxial systems (Hayashi et al. 2020, 2023) and tidally
disrupted systems (Read et al. 2018; De Leo et al. 2024).
A major challenge of Jeans methods that rely solely on
velocity dispersions is the mass-anisotropy degeneracy: dif-
ferent combinations of the enclosed mass M(< r) and veloc-
ity anisotropy β(r) can produce identical LOSV dispersion
profiles (e.g., Merrifield & Kent 1990; Wilkinson et al. 2002;
Łokas & Mamon 2003; de Lorenzi et al. 2009; Wolf et al.
2010). This degeneracy can be broken by incorporating mul-
tiple tracer populations with different spatial or kinematic
distributions (Walker & Peñarrubia 2011; Amorisco & Evans
2012; Zhu et al. 2016b; Read & Steger 2017), proper motions
when available (Strigari et al. 2007; Read & Steger 2017;
Strigari et al. 2018; Massari et al. 2020; Vitral et al. 2024;
Bañares-Hernández et al. 2025; Vitral et al. 2026), or higher-
order velocity moments (Łokas 2009; Mamon et al. 2013;
Richardson & Fairbairn 2013; Read & Steger 2017; Wardana
et al. 2025; Bañares-Hernández et al. 2026). Alternatively,
one can directly model the phase-space distribution function
f(x, v) by assuming a functional form (or family of forms)
and fitting all moments simultaneously, as in Schwarzschild
modeling (e.g., Breddels & Helmi 2013, 2014; Kowalczyk et al.
2019) or distribution function mapping methods (Wilkinson
et al. 2002; Pascale et al. 2018; Read et al. 2021; Pascale et al.
2025, 2026).
Recently, Nguyen et al. (2023) developed an alternative
approach using Neural Posterior Estimation (NPE) to infer
density profiles directly from kinematic samples drawn from a
distribution function. This framework, dubbed GraphNPE,
is part of a broader class of simulation-based inference (SBI)
methods (Cranmer et al. 2020), which have seen increasing
adoption across diverse astrophysics applications including
galaxy cluster mass estimation (e.g., Ho et al. 2022; de An-
dres et al. 2022), gravitational wave parameter inference (e.g.,

In this work, we extend the GraphNPE framework to
bridge the gap between idealized simulations and real obser-
vations by incorporating heterogeneous velocity measurement
uncertainties and spectroscopic selection functions. While
Nguyen et al. (2023) explored the model’s performance un-
der different velocity uncertainties (0.1, 2.0, 5.0 km/s), they
assumed uniform uncertainties across all stars and did not
explicitly model uncertainties within the inference pipeline.
Similarly, spectroscopic surveys of dwarf galaxies are subject
to selection effects that vary spatially: magnitude limits re-
duce completeness in the outer regions, while crowding limits
sampling in dense central regions, both of which can bias den-
sity profile inference if not properly accounted for.
We apply this updated framework to two Milky Way dwarf
spheroidal galaxies selected to bracket the luminosity regime
where the core–cusp problem is most informative: Draco
and Boötes I. Draco is one of the faintest classical dwarf
spheroidals, with a well-measured velocity dispersion profile
(Walker et al. 2009; Muñoz et al. 2018) and a long history
at the center of the core–cusp debate (e.g. Read et al. 2018;
Hayashi et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2025). Boötes I, in turn, is one
of the brightest ultra-faint dwarfs (UFDs) and now has one
of the largest spectroscopic member samples among UFDs
(Sandford et al. 2026), making it a uniquely tractable target
in a regime where Jeans modeling is typically limited by small
sample sizes and large measurement uncertainties (Belokurov
et al. 2006; Muñoz et al. 2006; Martin et al. 2007; Simon &
Geha 2007; Koposov et al. 2011).
For each galaxy, we perform independent analyses on spec-
troscopic samples from multiple datasets to ensure robust-
ness against measurement systematics and selection effects.
For Draco, we use stellar kinematics from MMT/Hectochelle
(Walker et al. 2023) and complementary measurements from
the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI; Ding et al.
2025). For Boötes I, we employ kinematic measurements from
the Southern Stellar Stream Spectroscopic Survey (S5; Li
et al. 2019) as presented in Sandford et al. (2026), sup-
plemented by archival spectroscopy from MMT/Hectochelle
(Walker et al. 2023) and the Very Large Telescope (VLT;
Koposov et al. 2011; Jenkins et al. 2021).
For each dwarf, we first validate the framework on mock
kinematic datasets constructed to match the observational
properties of the real data, including empirical selection func-
tions and star-by-star measurement uncertainties drawn from
the spectroscopic catalogs. We then apply GraphNPE to
the observational data to infer the DM density and veloc-
ity anisotropy profiles, inner DM densities, and J- and D-
factors for each system. For both Draco and Boötes I, we
analyze each spectroscopic dataset independently to assess
consistency across instruments. For Boötes I, we additionally
analyze the combined dataset to leverage the full kinematic
information available.
This paper is structured as follows. Section 2 describes the
GraphNPE framework, including modifications to incorpo-
rate measurement uncertainties and selection effects, and the
second-order Jeans modeling used for comparison. Section 3
describes the spectroscopic datasets for Draco and Boötes I.
Section 4 validates the framework on mock datasets and com-
pares the performance of GraphNPE and Jeans modeling.
Section 5 presents the main results, including the inferred
density and velocity anisotropy profiles (Section 5.1), inner
densities (Section 5.2), and J- and D-factors (Section 5.3).
Section 6 examines the inferred inner DM distribution of
Boötes I in the context of baryonic feedback, tidal process-
ing by the Milky Way, and alternative dark matter models.
Section 7 discusses the broader implications of our results, in-
cluding the role of higher-order moments in the Draco DESI
inference (Section 7.1) and potential sources of systematic
uncertainty (Section 7.2). Finally, Section 8 summarizes our
conclusions.

2.2.2 Forward model
In NPE, the forward model (or simulator) p(d|θ) gener-
ates synthetic observations from parameters. NPE only re-
quires that the forward model can be sampled from, in con-
trast to likelihood-based approaches that require an explicit,
tractable likelihood. Thus, NPE is particularly powerful
when the data-generating process can be simulated (e.g. N-
body simulation, mock observation), but the likelihood is in-
tractable due to complex selection effects or high-dimensional
correlations. The forward model encodes all physical assump-
tions and observational systematics, and the quality of pos-
terior inference depends directly on how faithfully it captures
the true underlying physics. Below, we describe the forward
model used to generate synthetic dwarf galaxy kinematics for
training GraphNPE.

We generate mock galaxies using Agama (Vasiliev 2019),

compute the anisotropic DF f(E, L) from the density profile
ρ(r) and potential Φ(r), where E and L are the specific or-
bital energy and angular momentum, respectively. Mock stars
are then drawn from f(E, L) via adaptive rejection sampling
and converted to 6D phase space (x, v).
For each mock galaxy in the training set, the number of

model generalize across galaxies with different numbers of
spectroscopic members. Using the graph construction proce-
dure described in Section 2.2.3, we find the model to be robust
to tracer counts well outside this range, as also demonstrated
in Section 4 and Section 5.

limitations as directions for future work.
We model the selection function S as a radial dropout
scheme, where a fraction fdrop ∼U(0, 0.5) of stars are re-
moved according to one of three modes:
(i) Souter removes the outermost fdrop fraction of stars
mimicking magnitude-limited incompleteness at large R;
(ii) Sinner removes the innermost fdrop fraction mimicking
crowding incompleteness at small R;
(iii) Srand removes a random fdrop fraction regardless of
position.
During training, one mode is sampled per mock galaxy with
mixture probabilities (0.4, 0.4, 0.2) for (Souter, Sinner, Srand),
respectively. The mixture probabilities are obtained through
experimentation, though we note that the results are not sen-
sitive to the exact choice, as long as Souter and Sinner are not
negligible relative to Srand.
Observationally, the selection function is not homogeneous
but instead decreases towards both the inner and outer edges
of the galaxy. However, we find that the model is not sensi-
tive to the exact functional form of S during training. This
insensitivity likely arises because the model is conditioned on
the Plummer radius r⋆(see Section 2.2.1), which encodes the
true projected surface density Σ⋆(R). In this sense, the model
already possesses sufficient information to infer the underly-
ing stellar distribution and identify deviations from it caused
by selection effects. The dropout scheme does not prescribe
the selection function to the model but rather encourages it
to develop this capability during training.
We note a few updates to the graph construction proce-
dure relative to Nguyen et al. (2023, 2025b). The node fea-
tures now include LOSV uncertainties: Hi = (Ri, vlos,i, ∆i).
Additionally, rather than fixing the number of nearest neigh-
bors to k = 20 as in our previous work, we set k to 20%
of the total number of tracers in each mock galaxy. We find
this adaptive scheme is critical for model performance: the
selection function can reduce the tracer count by up to 50%,
and a fixed k = 20 would result in overly dense graphs for
small samples or excessively sparse graphs for large samples.
Setting k proportional to N ensures a similar graph connec-
tivity across the full range of sample sizes encountered during
training.
Finally, we note that all transformations (i.e. projection,
uncertainty kernel, selection function, and adaptive graph
construction) are applied on-the-fly during training rather
than pre-computed. This significantly improves sample effi-
ciency: even for a fixed parameter, the model is unlikely to
encounter the same graph twice.
2.2.4 Inference on observational data
We briefly outline the inference procedure on observational
data. Since the Plummer scale radius r⋆is used as condi-
tioning context for the model, we first obtain r⋆and its
uncertainty by fitting a Plummer profile to the photomet-
ric light profile. This can be obtained through direct fitting
to photometry data, though in this work we use measure-
ments reported in the Local Volume Database (LVDB; Pace
2024). We assume a Gaussian posterior for r⋆centered on
the reported value with the reported uncertainty, and draw
NMC = 100 samples of r⋆from this posterior. For each sam-
pled r⋆, we condition the trained GraphNPE model on the
observed stellar kinematics and generate Npost = 100 poste-
rior samples. This yields a total of 104 samples that account
for both the uncertainty in r⋆and the posterior uncertainty
in the mass profile parameters. The graph is constructed from
the observed data without applying any of the training-time
transformations.

Lastly, we note the effect of perspective rotation. Perspec-
tive rotation is an observational effect in which the bulk trans-
verse motion of a stellar system projects differently onto the
LOSV at different positions across the system, thus inducing
a spurious gradient that can mimic intrinsic rotation (Feast
et al. 1961; van der Marel et al. 2002; Kaplinghat & Stri-
gari 2008; Walker et al. 2008; Pace et al. 2020). This effect
is most significant for nearby galaxies with large angular ex-
tents and proper motions, and has been primarily accounted
for in analyses of classical dwarf spheroidals (e.g. Kaplinghat
& Strigari 2008; Walker et al. 2008; Pace et al. 2020) and,
more recently, ultra-diffuse galaxies (Ji et al. 2021).
Among the systems studied here, perspective rotation pri-
marily affects Boötes I due to its larger proper motion
(1.1 mas yr−1 versus 0.2 mas yr−1 for Draco; Pace et al. 2022),
which produces a more substantial spurious gradient despite
the smaller angular extent (0.99◦versus 2.03◦). Previous
analyses of Boötes I by Hayashi et al. (2023) and S26, with
which we compare in Section 5.2, do not apply this correc-
tion.1 To facilitate direct comparison with these analyses, the
main results presented throughout this paper are computed
without correcting for perspective rotation, and we present
a corresponding analysis with the correction applied in Ap-
pendix B2, showing that the inferred profiles are not substan-
tially affected.

We use Agama to sample the 6D phase-space coordinates
of the tracers, which are then projected onto a randomly
oriented sky plane to obtain (x, y, vlos). We then incorpo-
rate the selection function of the target galaxies by matching
the radial distribution of the mock tracers to that of the ob-
served data. This is achieved by resampling the mock tracers
with weights wi = Nobs(Ri)/Nsim(Ri), where Nobs(Ri) and
Nsim(Ri) are the number of observed and simulated stars in
radial bins centered on Ri, effectively applying an empirical
selection function S(R) ∝w(R) to the mock data. The to-
tal number of resampled tracers is set to match the observed
sample size.
We incorporate measurement uncertainties by convolving
each mock velocity with a Gaussian kernel N(vlos,i, ∆2
i ),
where ∆i is drawn from the empirical uncertainty distribu-
tion of the observed spectroscopic sample. This procedure
matches the observed uncertainty distribution but does not
capture correlations between uncertainties and tracer prop-
erties (e.g., magnitude, color, position).
Finally, we note that the mocks are generated from equilib-
rium DFs rather than N-body simulations and therefore do
not account for tidal effects, substructure, or other dynamical
processes that may affect the real systems. Additionally, the
fiducial parameters are chosen to approximate the observed
properties of Draco and Boötes I but are not intended to
precisely reproduce their true density profiles or anisotropy.
These mocks serve primarily as a validation tool to assess the
performance of our inference framework on realistic datasets
with observational effects (selection functions, measurement
uncertainties) matching the target galaxies, rather than as
high-fidelity representations of the systems themselves.

4.2.1 GraphNPE performance
Figs. 4 and 5 show that GraphNPE recovers the true
DM density and velocity anisotropy profiles across all mock
datasets. The 68% credible intervals consistently cover the
true profiles both within and beyond the radial range covered
by the observed tracers, with only minor deviations in β(r).
GraphNPE thus generalizes robustly to realistic observa-
tional conditions, despite the mock datasets employing selec-
tion functions and LOSV measurement uncertainty distribu-
tions that differ from the training distribution (Section 2.2.3).
The two bottom rows of Figs. 4 and 5 show the inferred
LOSV dispersion σlos(R) and kurtosis κlos(R) profiles, along-
side the binned data points computed using the procedure in
Appendix C1. GraphNPE posteriors generally contain the
binned data points within the 68% credible intervals, with no-
ticeably wider intervals on κlos(R) than on σlos(R), reflecting
the higher intrinsic variance of the fourth moment. The re-
covery of both profiles, particularly κlos(R), is notable, since
GraphNPE does not fit either profile directly. This demon-
strates that GraphNPE, by operating on the full kinematic
DF, accesses higher-order velocity moments beyond what the
second-moment likelihood alone provides. We caution, how-
ever, that good agreement with the binned data points does
not by itself imply recovery of the true profiles, since the
binned data points are subject to sampling noise and may
themselves deviate from the truth. We will revisit this point
in more detail below.
Lastly, we note that the credible intervals on the Boötes I
mocks are also wider than those on the Draco DESI mocks,
despite the two having comparable tracer counts. This reflects
the larger LOSV uncertainties and smaller intrinsic velocity
dispersion of Boötes I, which together reduce the effective
signal-to-noise of the kinematic data.

GraphNPE still overestimates the inner density normaliza-
tion ρs, though substantially less than Jeans.
The second failure mode is the well-known mass-anisotropy
degeneracy, where different combinations of the enclosed
mass and velocity anisotropy profiles can produce identical
σlos(R) profiles. For example, the Draco MMT CoreOM
mock result in Fig. 4 illustrates that although the binned σlos
aligns well with the true dispersion profile, Jeans still infers
a biased inner slope (γ = 0.95+0.75
−0.45) compared to Graph-
NPE (γ = 0.64+0.67
−0.47). The poorer recovery of β(r) by Jeans
suggests that the bias is driven by the mass-anisotropy degen-
eracy rather than noisy dispersion bins. By contrast, Graph-
NPE breaks this degeneracy by recovering κlos(R) through
DF mapping. This is consistent with the well-established role
of higher-order moments in breaking the mass-anisotropy de-
generacy (Łokas 2002; Richardson & Fairbairn 2013).
Finally, we note an interesting pattern in the performance
of second-order Jeans modeling across the different mock pro-
files. Jeans modeling recovers the density profiles more accu-
rately for the isotropic mocks (CoreIso and CuspIso) than
for the OM mocks (CoreOM and CuspOM), as seen in Ta-
ble 4 where the inferred inner slopes from Jeans are more
comparable to GraphNPE for the isotropic cases.
4.3 Sensitivity of velocity moments to the inner
slope
To gain further insight into the core-cusp sensitivity of
second-order Jeans modeling, we calculate the LOSV dis-
persion σlos(R) and kurtosis κlos(R) profiles as a function
of the inner slope γ. Fig. 7 shows σlos(R) and κlos(R) for
12 different values of γ ∈[−1, 2] (step size of ∆γ = 0.25).
We fix rs = 1 kpc and choose ρs such that the Wolf mass
matches that of Draco, following the procedure described in
Section 4.1. For simplicity, we assume an isotropic velocity
profile (β = 0).
For different values of γ, σlos converge near R1/2 but di-
verge significantly at both small and large radii. Cuspy pro-
files (γ ∼1) produce higher dispersions at small radii and
lower dispersions at large radii compared to cored profiles
(γ ∼0). Below R1/2, the gradient of σlos with respect to
γ is much smaller near γ ∼0 than near γ ∼1, so cored
profiles produce nearly identical dispersion profiles across a
wider range of γ. This asymmetric sensitivity has direct con-
sequences for inference: Jeans modeling, and any method re-
lying solely on the velocity dispersion, is intrinsically less in-
formative about cored profiles than about cuspy ones, as we
now demonstrate quantitatively.
Consider the distribution of γ recovered at a fixed projected
radius of R = 0.1 kpc under uniform sampling in σlos. Specifi-
cally, we draw σlos values uniformly across the range spanned
by the family of profiles and invert the σlos −γ relation at
each step to obtain the corresponding γ. The resulting distri-
butions, shown as inset histograms in the top panels of Fig. 7,
are strongly skewed toward cuspy values for both anisotropy
assumptions. This suggests that a uniform prior on the ob-
servable σlos maps onto a γ posterior that disproportionately
favors cusps. Equivalently, a broad range of cored profiles is
compressed into a narrow range of σlos, so the data carry
little information to distinguish among them.
Additional sources of uncertainty, such as large measure-
ment errors and the mass-anisotropy degeneracy, further ex-
acerbate this insensitivity in the cored regime.
The bottom panels of Fig. 7 show κlos(R) for the same
range of γ. At small radii, κlos varies strongly with γ, par-
ticularly in the cored regime, with cored profiles (γ ∼0)
producing notably higher kurtosis than cuspy ones (γ ∼1).
At large radii, the curves converge. Repeating the uniform-
sampling exercise on κlos at R = 0.1 kpc yields distributions
skewed toward cored values. This reveals the exact opposite

interpreted as a realization of the prior-volume effect identi-

equally well. Incorporating κlos breaks this degeneracy by
directly constraining β, thus disfavoring the low-β portion
of the prior volume responsible for the cuspy bias. We refer
readers to Section 4.3 of Bañares-Hernández et al. (2026) for
a more detailed discussion.
In summary, the mock results validate the updated
GraphNPE framework under realistic measurement uncer-
tainties and selection effects, and give insights into the key
performance differences between GraphNPE and second-
order Jeans modeling. GraphNPE recovers cored density
profiles with less bias than Jeans modeling, while both meth-
ods perform comparably well on cuspy inner profiles. Addi-
tionally, GraphNPE more accurately extrapolates the outer
density profile across all mock datasets, regardless of inner
slope. Importantly, these results highlight that the LOSV dis-
persion profile σlos(R) is an incomplete summary of the un-
derlying DM density structure: (1) sampling noise and mea-
surement uncertainties can bias Jeans inferences even when
the binned dispersion appears well-fit, and (2) even in the
absence of such noise, the mass–anisotropy degeneracy al-
lows fundamentally different density and anisotropy profiles
to produce nearly identical dispersion profiles. Higher-order
moments such as κlos(R) carry complementary information
that mitigates both failure modes and serve as an important
diagnostic of the inferred posterior. By operating on the full
DF, GraphNPE naturally exploits this higher-order infor-
mation, which is the key driver of its improved performance
over the second-order Jeans model.

(see Fig. 2 of Pace & Strigari 2019). They also apply a cos-

quiring extremely rare peaks in the primordial density field
(their Section 6.4), effectively excluding ρs > 109 M⊙kpc−3
and thus lowering their inferred factors. On the other hand,
Pace & Strigari (2019) imposes rs > a similarly to our work,
but assume a pure NFW profile, which forces a cuspy inner
slope. Furthermore, they truncate the NFW profile at the
tidal radius, which is substantially larger than the truncation
radius used in either our analysis or Geringer-Sameth et al.
(2015), thus increasing their inferred J-factors. That the two
studies nonetheless report broadly consistent values suggests
these effects partially cancel, and points to the shared shal-
low kinematic coverage of Koposov et al. (2011) as the dom-
inant driver of the offset relative to our analysis. Given the
substantial scatter in literature values for Boötes I and the
methodological differences across studies, we caution against
drawing strong conclusions from any direct numerical com-
parison.

6.2 Implications
Boötes I is an interesting case study given that it is an
ultra-faint galaxy, and therefore we expect it to be domi-
nated by DM. Additionally, its proximity, given that it is only
d = 66.4 kpc away (Dall’Ora et al. 2006), makes it a great
target for spectroscopic studies as well as indirect detection
measurements of DM. In this section, we seek to understand
its properties as found within our GraphNPE framework.
As shown in Fig. 12, the density at 150 pc of Boötes I is
quite low, consistent with (and even below) the theoretical
predictions of Read et al. (2019) for a cored profile. However,
the AM estimate of M200 is uncertain enough that if the
true Boötes I mass is much lower than the current estimate
of ∼109 M⊙, the core-cusp distinction based on ρ150 alone
becomes ambiguous, given that the cusp and core bands con-
verge at low M200. As noted above, M am

Munshi et al. (2021) relation suggests. This mass uncertainty
affects our measurement and those of Hayashi et al. (2023)
and S26 asymmetrically, because each sits at a different ρ150
relative to the core and cusp bands. Our ρ150 is low enough to
remain at or below the core band across the plausible range
of M200. Only near M200 ∼3 × 107 M⊙, where the core and
cusp bands merge, does ρ150 cease to distinguish a core from
a cusp. The two literature values are higher, and toward the
low-mass end of the range they can cross into the cusp band.
Our inference of a core is therefore robust to the M200 uncer-
tainty, while the core or cusp classification of the literature
measurements is not.

Taken together, the low ρ150 and the shallow γin paint a

inner profile is shallower than NFW, but whose physical ori-

them will require both deeper spectroscopic data for Boötes I
and a broader statistical sample of dwarf galaxies spanning a
range of orbital histories, star formation durations, and halo
masses, so that the relative contributions of these mechanisms
can be disentangled at the population level.
7 DISCUSSION
7.1 Draco DESI: a case for higher-order moments
A key result of Section 5 is the disagreement between Graph-
NPE and second-order Jeans modeling on the Draco DESI
dataset. While the two methods fit the binned σlos(R) profile
comparably well, the Jeans model infers a substantially lower
ρ150 on DESI than on MMT. GraphNPE, by contrast, re-
covers consistent density profiles across the two datasets. The
two methods nonetheless fit σlos(R) comparably well, so the

The kurtosis profile κlos(R) breaks this apparent tie. Fig. 8

whereas the second-order Jeans model overestimates it on
DESI. Since neither method fits κlos(R) directly, its recovery

The Jeans mismatch on DESI thus provides evidence that the
low ρ150 reflects an inference bias rather than a real difference
between the two Draco samples. This is consistent with our
mock results of Section 4, where sampling noise in σlos(R)
can drive second-order Jeans toward biased posteriors that
nonetheless fit the dispersion well.
A similar pattern can be seen in Y25, which analyzes the
same DESI dataset using the second-order Jeans model from
Watkins et al. (2013); Zhu et al. (2016a,b). Their DESI infer-
ence yields a significantly lower ρ150 than their DESI+MMT
value, despite the two samples probing the same galaxy. We
interpret this offset as a manifestation of the same DESI-
specific bias seen in our Jeans analysis, since both methods
rely on σlos(R) alone.
More broadly, the Draco DESI case highlights a limitation
of dispersion-based inference that is not specific to any one
implementation. A model that fits σlos(R) well cannot, on its
own, be taken as evidence that the underlying density and
anisotropy profiles are correctly recovered. Multiple combi-
nations of mass and velocity anisotropy can produce nearly
identical dispersion profiles (Łokas 2002; Richardson & Fair-
bairn 2013; Read & Steger 2017). Additionally, as shown in
Section 4.3, σlos(R) is intrinsically less sensitive to variations
in the inner slope γ in the cored regime than in the cuspy
regime, while κlos(R) exhibits the opposite asymmetry. The
two moments therefore carry complementary information on
both the inner slope and the velocity anisotropy, and jointly
break degeneracies that σlos alone cannot.
As current and upcoming surveys (e.g. Takada et al. 2014;
de Jong et al. 2019) expand the spectroscopic samples avail-
able for Milky Way dwarfs, we argue that incorporating
κlos(R), either explicitly through higher-order Jeans equa-
tions (e.g. Richardson & Fairbairn 2013; Read & Steger 2017;
Bañares-Hernández et al. 2026) or implicitly through DF-
based methods (Wilkinson et al. 2002; Pascale et al. 2018;
Read et al. 2021; Pascale et al. 2025, 2026), including Graph-
NPE, will be essential to fully exploit the statistical power of
these datasets. At the very least, κlos(R) should be reported
alongside σlos(R) as an independent posterior diagnostic.
7.2 Systematics
7.2.1 Tidal disruption
We now discuss the modeling systematics that can affect
our inference results. As with Jeans modeling, GraphNPE
assumes dynamical equilibrium, which can break down for
systems undergoing significant tidal disruption. For example,
Wang et al. (2022) shows that systems experiencing strong
tidal effects can have contraction motions, and that dynam-
ical modeling may underestimate the inner density profile.
However, tidal disruption does not necessarily bias dynamical
inferences, provided that contamination from unbound stars
remains negligible (Read et al. 2018; Genina et al. 2020; De
Leo et al. 2024; Nguyen et al. 2025b).

7.2.2 Tracer density profile
To account for the spectroscopic selection function, we es-
timate the true tracer mass density profile from the photo-
metric light profile. We model the light profile as a Plum-
mer sphere and condition GraphNPE on the Plummer scale
radius r⋆. This requires two key assumptions. First, as dis-
cussed in Section 2, we assume that the light profile traces
the mass profile, which requires an approximately constant
mass-to-light ratio with projected radius. This is a reasonable
approximation for old, metal-poor stellar populations, which
holds for both Draco (Kirby et al. 2011; Ding et al. 2025) and
Boötes I (Longeard et al. 2022; Sandford et al. 2026).
Second, and more critically, we assume the stellar tracer
density follows a Plummer profile, with structural parameters
fixed to the values reported in the LVDB (Pace 2024). While
the Plummer profile is a standard and widely adopted choice
for dwarf galaxies, deviations from this form, particularly in
the inner and outer slopes, can introduce systematic biases
in mass estimators (Splawska et al. 2026). Observationally,
Moskowitz & Walker (2020) find evidence for steepened outer
stellar density profiles in several MW dwarfs (Fornax, Leo I,
Leo II, and Reticulum II), though no significant central cusps
in the stellar distribution are detected.
We note that most of the literature results we compare
against (Geringer-Sameth et al. 2015; Hayashi et al. 2020,
2023; Sandford et al. 2026) also adopt a Plummer profile,
with the exception of Read et al. (2018), which models the
stellar distribution with a three-component Plummer profile,
and Yang et al. (2025), which models the light profile non-
parametrically. As such, systematic biases arising from the
Plummer assumption are expected to affect our results and
the literature comparisons in a broadly similar manner. While
it is straightforward to condition GraphNPE on the pa-
rameters of a different parametric profile (e.g. Sérsic, King),
this would require training separate models for each assumed
form. A more flexible but considerably more difficult alter-
native is to feed a non-parametric light profile directly to
GraphNPE. We leave both extensions for future work.
7.2.3 Halo and anisotropy profile assumptions
In Sections 5.2 and 5.3, we show that the inferred ρ150, J-,

on GraphNPE inferences in the ultra-faint regime remains
untested. Extending these stress tests to higher-resolution
cosmological simulations of ultra-faint dwarfs, alongside the
methodological comparisons proposed above, is a natural di-
rection for future work.
7.2.4 Other modeling assumptions and caveats
We discuss other modeling assumptions and limitations of
GraphNPE. As described in Section 2.2.2, the training
dataset is generated using the df::QuasiSphericalCOM class
in Agama, which assumes a specific functional form for
the DF. This restricts the class of DFs that GraphNPE is
trained on, and real dwarf galaxies may have DFs that fall
outside this family.
A related limitation arises from the COM DF itself. As
detailed in Appendix C, the COM family imposes a specific
relationship between the fourth- and second-order anisotropy
structure of the DF, which need not hold for general DFs.
Since GraphNPE is trained exclusively on COM realiza-
tions, it implicitly assumes this relationship for all inferences,
including the kurtosis profiles computed in this work. Sys-
tems whose fourth-order velocity anisotropy deviates from
the COM family may therefore not be accurately captured
by GraphNPE. We regard both of these as caveats on the
current implementation and defer a systematic exploration of
more general DFs to future work.
It is also worth noting that higher-order moments such as
κlos are more sensitive to outliers than σlos. Since the kurtosis
is determined by the tails of the LOSV distribution, a small
fraction of foreground or background contaminants could po-
tentially bias the inferred κlos(R), thus affecting the Graph-
NPE inference (and DF-based methods) more severely than
σlos(R) alone. In practice, differences in membership determi-
nation, selection cuts, and data quality across datasets may
introduce additional variation in the inferred κlos(R) that is
difficult to disentangle from physical signal.
As discussed in Section 7.2.3, Nguyen et al. (2025b) tests
GraphNPE on FIRE-2 dwarf galaxies and finds the inferred
density profiles to be robust even under departures from the
training assumptions. Additionally, Nguyen et al. (2025b) se-
lects member stars for these mock dwarfs in a way that intro-
duces potential contamination from unbound stars, and finds
the model to be largely robust to this effect. However, these
tests are performed on galaxies with realistic DFs but with-
out the uncertainties and selection functions characteristic of
real spectroscopic datasets. Conversely, the mock validation
in this work incorporates realistic observational uncertainties
and selection functions, but uses in-distribution COM real-
izations rather than fully realistic DFs. Thus, a stress test
combining both realistic non-equilibrium DFs and realistic
observational effects may be worth revisiting in future work.
8 SUMMARY AND OUTLOOK
The mass density profiles of dwarf spheroidal galaxies are
among the most powerful probes of DM physics. In this work,
we extend the GraphNPE framework of Nguyen et al. (2023,
2025b) to account for measurement uncertainties and spec-
troscopic selection effects. Compared to second-order Jeans
modeling, which fits the LOSV dispersion, GraphNPE per-
forms inference using the full DF and thus implicitly exploits
higher-order moments of the velocity distribution.
We apply GraphNPE to the classical dwarf Draco and the
ultra-faint dwarf Boötes I. For Draco, we use two independent
spectroscopic samples from MMT/Hectochelle (Walker et al.
2023) and DESI (Ding et al. 2025). For Boötes I, we use
recent observations from S5 (Sandford et al. 2026), as well
as a combined sample that supplements the S5 observations
with archival data from VLT/FLAMES (Jenkins et al. 2021)
and MMT/Hectochelle (Walker et al. 2023).
Using controlled mock datasets matched to the observa-
tional properties of each galaxy, we benchmark GraphNPE
against second-order Jeans modeling in Section 4. We sum-
marize our findings below:
• Any method relying solely on the LOSV dispersion pro-
file σlos(R), including second-order Jeans modeling, is biased
toward cuspy density profiles, even in the absence of the mass-
anisotropy degeneracy. This bias is driven by the intrinsic in-
sensitivity of σlos to the inner slope γ below the half-light
radius in the cored regime, and by sampling noise in the in-
nermost dispersion bins that can mimic the signature of a
cuspy profile (Section 4.3). The LOSV kurtosis profile κlos(R)
exhibits the opposite asymmetry, preferentially constraining
cored profiles, so σlos and κlos together resolve the degeneracy.
Thus, any inference framework that exploits κlos in addition
to σlos will mitigate the same bias, whether through higher-
order LOSV moments (e.g. Richardson & Fairbairn 2013;
Read & Steger 2017; Wardana et al. 2025; Bañares-Hernández

In Section 6, we explored the implications of the low ρ150 of
Boötes I by placing it in the ρ150−M200 relation of Read et al.
(2019) and the γin −M⋆/M200 relation predicted by the NI-
HAO (Tollet et al. 2016) and FIRE-2 (Lazar et al. 2020) cos-
mological simulations. Across both diagnostics, Boötes I sits
below the cuspy NFW expectation and below the predicted
inner slopes from feedback-driven core formation, identifying
it as one of the most diffuse inner halos reported for dwarfs of
comparable mass. We discussed four physical interpretations:
• First,
we
disfavor
feedback-driven
core
formation.
Boötes I lies below the M⋆/M200 threshold required for ef-
ficient coring (Fig. 13), and past studies have shown that it
formed essentially all of its stars in a single short burst at
early times (Brown et al. 2014; Durbin et al. 2025). We note,
however, that the M200 of Boötes I is poorly constrained,
and at sufficiently low halo masses the M⋆/M200 threshold for
feedback-driven coring may be reached even with a short star
formation history. Nonetheless, the single burst constraint
from Brown et al. (2014) argues against sustained feedback,
which is required for efficient coring regardless of halo mass
(Read et al. 2016).
• Past tidal processing by the Milky Way is plausible but
might not be sufficient on its own. The small pericenter of
Boötes I (Pace et al. 2022) places it well within the strong
tidal regime, and the intrinsic LOSV gradient detected by
Sandford et al. (2026) is consistent with tidal heating from
previous pericentric passages. The parallel with the popula-
tion of tidally processed M31 satellites further supports this
picture (Collins et al. 2021; Charles et al. 2023; Pickett et al.
2025). However, tidal stripping primarily removes mass from
the outskirts of a halo, and several studies have shown that
it cannot, on its own, transform a primordial cusp into a core
(Peñarrubia et al. 2010; van den Bosch et al. 2018; Errani &
Peñarrubia 2020; Errani & Navarro 2021). Tidal effects can
lower ρ150 once the bound mass is substantially reduced, but
this requires the inner profile to be already shallow before
mass loss begins.
• It is possible, though statistically unlikely, that Boötes I
was formed in an unusually diffuse halo at the low-
concentration end of the mass-concentration relation from
Dutton & Macciò (2014).
• Alternative DM models predict shallower inner pro-
files at low halo masses. Self-interacting DM generates cores
through gravothermal heating from DM self-scattering, with
the inner profile evolving over time as the system transi-
tions from core expansion to gravothermal collapse (Spergel
& Steinhardt 2000; Rocha et al. 2013; Robles et al. 2017;
Tulin & Yu 2018). Within this framework, the ultra-faint
population is expected to span a range of inner densities at
fixed halo mass, reflecting different stages of gravothermal
evolution. Fuzzy DM produces core-like solitons from wave
interference on galactic scales (Schive et al. 2014a,b), though
prior works (e.g. Marsh & Niemeyer 2019; Dalal & Kravtsov
2022; Bañares-Hernández et al. 2023) have placed strong con-
straints on fuzzy DM and disfavors this interpretation.
Distinguishing between these scenarios, and between any of
them and tidal processing, will require dynamical mass mod-
eling of a larger population of ultra-faints spanning a range
of masses and orbital histories. The case for population-level
ultra-faint dynamics is thus ever more compelling. Boötes I is
the brightest and best-sampled ultra-faint dwarf galaxy, yet
here we show that its inner slope cannot be unambiguously
determined from current data. Other ultra-faint dwarfs have
smaller spectroscopic samples and will provide weaker con-

Our results demonstrate the power of DF-based inference
for extracting DM properties from sparse stellar kinematics
in dwarf galaxies. By operating on the full kinematic DF
rather than the LOSV dispersion alone, GraphNPE natu-
rally exploits higher-order velocity moments, mitigating both
sampling-noise and mass-anisotropy biases that affect second-
order Jeans modeling. In a follow-up work, we will apply
GraphNPE to larger samples of dwarf galaxies, both classi-
cal and ultra-faint, to systematically test for cores in low-mass
halos and place population-level constraints on DM physics.
Further extensions include incorporating proper motions to
decisively break the mass-anisotropy degeneracy, and relax-
ing the spherical-symmetry and equilibrium assumptions to
model triaxial and tidally disrupting systems. Together, these
directions position SBI as a versatile and robust framework
for the next generation of dwarf galaxy dynamical analyses.

APPENDIX A: MODEL ARCHITECTURE AND
TRAINING
We provide additional details on the model architecture and
training process. As described in Section 2.2.1, GraphNPE
consists of two primary components: (1) a GNN embedding
network that reads in the kinematic data and reduces into a
low-dimensional summary vector, and (2) a conditional den-
sity estimator that models that posterior distribution using
this summary vector.
The GNN embedding network consists of five Chebyshev
convolutional layers, each with a hidden size of 128. Each
layer performs message passing over the graph, where neigh-
borhood aggregation is carried out via a spectral filter ex-
pressed as a truncated expansion in Chebyshev polynomi-
als of order K = 8 over the graph Laplacian (Defferrard
et al. 2016). This ensures that each node aggregates in-
formation strictly from its K-hop neighborhood, preserving
spatial locality in the graph. The GNN layers are followed
by a global mean pooling layer, which averages node em-
beddings across the graph to produce a fixed-dimensional,
permutation-invariant summary vector. This summary vec-
tor is then passed through a two-layer multi-layer perceptron
(MLP) block with a hidden size of 128. The ReLU activations
(Nair & Hinton 2010) are applied after each GNN and MLP
layer except the final one.
As noted in Section 2.2.3, to account for the spectroscopic
selection effect, the Plummer scale radius r⋆is passed through
a two-layer MLP with a hidden size of 128 and a ReLU activa-
tion between the layers, producing a 128-dimensional embed-
ding. This embedding is added element-wise to the output of
the MLP block described above, forming the final summary
vector passed to the density estimator.
The summary vector is then passed to the density estima-
tor, which consists of a neural spline flow (Durkan et al. 2019)
with six transformation layers. Each transformation layer is
conditioned on a two-layer MLP block with a hidden size of
128, 8 spline knots, and Tanh activations. The flow models
the posterior distribution over the seven DM and anisotropy
parameters θ = (α, β, γ, ρs, rs, β0, ra).
During training, the 6D kinematic data is first passed
through a stochastic forward model that projects the data
to the 3D observed space, perturbs each observable within
its measurement uncertainty, and applies the selection func-
tion, before being passed into the embedding network (see
Section 2.2.3). This procedure significantly increases sample
efficiency, as each training step produces a distinct data real-

B1 Posterior Calibration Test
Posterior calibration is critical to ensure that the credible in-
tervals returned by the posterior reflect the true frequentist
coverage probabilities. An uncalibrated posterior can lead to
systematically over- or underestimated uncertainties, which
in turn compromises the scientific conclusions drawn from
the inferred DM density profiles. We assess the calibration
of GraphNPE posteriors using TARP (Lemos et al. 2023),
which computes the expected coverage probability as a func-
tion of the credibility level by drawing random reference
points in parameter space and checking whether the true
parameters fall within the corresponding highest-posterior-
density regions. Crucially, TARP is both necessary and suf-

B2 Perspective rotation correction
We present analysis results on Boötes I without correcting
for perspective rotation, an observational effect in which a
galaxy’s bulk space motion projects onto the LOSV differ-
ently across its face, inducing a spurious gradient that can
mimic intrinsic rotation (Feast et al. 1961; van der Marel et al.
2002; Kaplinghat & Strigari 2008; Walker et al. 2008; Pace
et al. 2020). As discussed in Section 3.2, this effect mainly
affects Boötes I among our datasets, owing to its larger sys-
temic proper motion (µtot ∼1.1 mas yr−1). Because the in-
duced gradient adds a position-dependent component to the
LOSV, it inflates the inferred velocity dispersion, with the
largest effect at large projected radii.

B3 Isotropic mock datasets
We present results on CoreIso and CuspIso mock datasets
in Figures B4 and B5, respectively. Consistent with the OM
results in Section 4.2, GraphNPE successfully recovers the
density and anisotropy profiles within the radial range cov-
ered by the observed tracers within the 68% credible intervals.
In particular, GraphNPE correctly identifies the constant
anisotropy despite this configuration not being explicitly rep-
resented in the training distribution, as the COM profile re-
quires ra →∞to reproduce the β = 0 limit. Beyond the
outermost tracer, the inferred β(r) naturally rises toward ra-
dially biased values as the model reverts to the COM profile
in the absence of data constraints.
Compared to the OM cases, the performance of Graph-
NPE and Jeans is more comparable for most isotropic mocks.
This may be attributed to the greater sensitivity of σlos(R) to
γ in the isotropic case: in the OM case, the radially varying
anisotropy profile suppresses this sensitivity, making different
inner slopes harder to distinguish from the velocity dispersion
alone (see Section 4.3). Despite this, Jeans tends to infer a
rising β(r) even within the tracer range, suggesting that the
mass-anisotropy degeneracy remains unresolved. A notable
example is the Draco DESI CoreIso mock, where Jeans over-
fits the binned velocity dispersion and infers strongly biased
density and anisotropy profiles, while GraphNPE remains
more robust.
