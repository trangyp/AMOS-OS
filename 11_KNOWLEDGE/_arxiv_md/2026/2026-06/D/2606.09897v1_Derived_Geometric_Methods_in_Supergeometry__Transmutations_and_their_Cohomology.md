DERIVED GEOMETRIC METHODS IN SUPERGEOMETRY:
TRANSMUTATIONS AND THEIR COHOMOLOGY
MARCEL DANG
Abstract. We study the stacky approach to cohomology in the super setting.
We introduce the classic transmutation stacks (Betti, de Rham and Dolbeault)
due to Simpson, which are geometric realizations of locally constant sheaves,
D-modules and Higgs bundles respectively and we give new proofs for Penkov’s
theorem on D-Modules and the isomorphism between de Rham cohomology
and super de Rham cohomology. To do this, we will develop the theory of
derived categories on superstacks establishing, amongst others, base change
and recollement theorems.
The goal of this paper is to demonstrate the usage of ideas and methods coming
from derived algebraic geometry in the supergeometric setting as derived and
super have geometric similarities, which lead to the same considerations when
adapting classical notions to their respective settings.

1. Introduction
1.1. Motivation. This work is motivated by structural and geometric aspects in
the foundations of supergeometry. The central idea is to demonstrate that the tools
of derived algebraic geometry allow for a streamlined development of supergeometry.
This manifests in two ways. Structurally, higher category theory provides a concise
framework for developing foundational notions systematically, in any variant of al-
gebraic geometry. Adopting this sufficiently general machinery allows us to directly
import proofs from the derived or Dirac literature to supergeometry. To ensure the
text is self-contained and to demonstrate the utility of this approach, we provide
full proofs for these foundational results within the Z2-graded context. In the dif-
ferential setting, Carchedi [Car25] has already developed a foundational theory for
derived differentail supergeometry using a topos-theoretic approach. However, since
he works in the differential setting, we cannot directly import his proofs into our
purely algebraic setting. Instead, we use the Dirac geometry literature as a basis,
developing our foundations using the functor of points approach in direct parallel
to the Z-graded supercommutative setting of Hesselholt and Pstrągowksi[HP24].
The central idea also manifests geometrically, guided by the intuition that superge-
ometry can be viewed as a variant of derived algebraic geometry. Explicitly, while
supergeometry behaves as a first-order thickening of ordinary algebraic geometry,
derived algebraic geometry functions as an all-order thickening. There is also a pre-
cise categorical similarity between derived schemes and superschemes. The category
of schemes is a colocalization of the category of derived schemes and superschemes,
i.e. we have a fully faithful inclusion functor that admits a right adjoint, so the
category of schemes sits in the middle of the following diagram

1.2. Main Results. This paper concerns itself with applying methods from de-
rived algebraic geometry to study cohomology of superschemes and to show how
derived methods can simplify classical results in supergeometry. Classically, it is
known that standard cohomological invariants of supermanifolds, such as de Rham
or singular cohomology, are isomorphic to those of their underlying reduced mani-
folds. In this sense, the superstructure cannot be detected cohomologically. How-
ever, we can still analyze these invariants, by shifting our perspective from the
cohomology groups themselves to the geometric spaces that represent them. This
was originally introduced by Simpson [Sim99] and extended by Drinfeld [Dri24]
and upcoming work of Bhatt-Lurie [Bha22] [BL22]. We extend Simpson’s classical

1.3. Organization. The paper is structured as follows. In Section 2 we give a
brief introduction to ∞-categories, by first introducing all the necessary concepts
in 1-categories, formulated in a way that is immediately transferrable to the ∞-
categorical setting. Afterwards, we will introduce a dictionary on how to replace
the 1-categorical objects by their ∞-categorical counterpart. Section 3 develops
the theory of superstacks and their derived categories in the fpqc-topology from
an ∞-categorical perspective, where we establish the tools required for the theo-
rems in section 4. Section 4 introduces the transmutation stacks of Simpson. We
adapt definitions from derived algebraic geometry in a straightforward way and
then demonstrate that we recover the classical definitions, which requires the the-
ory developed in Section 3. Furthermore, we give new geometric proofs of classical
theorems in supergeometry and show that the Dolbeault stack is not agnostic to
supergeometric information.
1.4. Terminology and Convention. Apart from section 2, by category we will
mean an ∞-category. We use the convention from derived algebraic geometry and
call the unbounded derived category on an algebraic stack X the category of quasi-
coherent sheaves on X and denote it by QCoh(X). We will give explicit references
to the theorem we are adapting to the Z2-graded case.

2. Category Theory
This is a section intended for a reader unfamiliar with the techniques of category
theory, as the paper heavily utilizes these methods. A reader that is familiar with
these techniques can safely skip this section.
We are currently in a transformative period concerning ∞-categories. The techni-
cal foundations are all laid out, but it is generally not documented how to think
about them intuitively, so we want to provide a practical guide for ∞-category the-
ory. The perspective taken here is that we should use ∞-categories as an interface.
Indeed, originally, there were many competing models of what an ∞-category is
supposed to be, under the guise of quasi-categories developed by Joyal or model
categories developed by Quillen. However, the modern incarnation was systemati-
cally formalized by Lurie (using quasicategories) in his monumental works [Lur17b],
[Lur17a], and [Lur17c]. These works provide us with a black box that allows us to
treat ∞-categories model-independently, meaning one now only has to learn how
to operate inside of this universal syntax.
We will use category theory, due to two reasons. On the one hand, the arguments
are given in a manner agnostic to the area you are working in, which makes it much
easier to discern whether a theorem we are trying to generalize works just the same
as before or requires some deep insight specific to the field we are studying. In
fields such as supergeometry, it is often difficult to track this boundary. Phrasing
our arguments in a categorical manner allows one to see whether a theorem is true
for formal reasons, meaning a theorem that should hold in any setting of geometry,
or if it requires specific, deep geometric insight. Isolating these formal structural
properties allows us to develop the literature in these subfields of algebraic geom-
etry in a much more efficient manner. Most of the arguments will boil down to
a colimit or limit computations, checking if a left or right Kan extension does the
thing we want or arguing using adjoint functors. All of these tools are things one
has already seen at some point in their mathematical lives, but were usually not
phrased as a categorical statement. The section is structured as follows. We start
by introducing known concepts from commutative algebra and algebraic geometry
and phrase them using 1-categorical language. We will do so in a manner, that di-
rectly generalizes to ∞-categories. The second part of this section then consists out
of simply giving the translation of all 1-categorical concepts to their ∞-categorical
counterpart
2.1. Colimits and Limits. Working in algebraic settings, one often utilizes con-
structions such as quotients, cokernels, pushouts, and direct sums. It turns out
that all of these concepts fall under the same umbrella called a colimit. Let us
consider, as an example, the situation of commutative algebra. For modules, we
are concerned with operations like taking tensor products of modules, direct sums
and localization. Now we ask the questions, do these operations preserve cokernels,
direct sums and localizations? We observe that constructions such as taking ten-
sor products or taking direct sums with a fixed module M or localizations are all
functorial. Thus, if a functor F : Mod(A) →Mod(B) sends colimits to colimits,
we will call this a colimit-preserving functor. Dually, constructions such as kernels,
products, and pullbacks are instances of limits, and we thus ponder the notion of
limit-preserving functors.

2.2. Adjoint Functors. A famous example of adjoint functors is the extension
and restriction of scalars for modules. A general fact about adjoint functors is that
the left adjoint preserves colimits and the right adjoint preserves limits. Moreover,
in the setting of presentable categories, this is as close to an equivalence as possible.
Definition 2.8. Let C be a category that admits all colimits and κ be a small
regular cardinal. It is called presentable if it is generated under filtered colimits,
by the κ-compact objects.
Theorem 2.9. Let F : C →D be a functor between presentable categories. Then
• F has a right adjoint if and only if F preserves all small colimits.
• F has a left adjoint if and only if it is accessible (preserves κ-filtered col-
imits) and all small limits.
Remark 2.10. The asymmetry in this statement is not because limits are weaker,
but it comes from the construction of presentable categories, being generated under
filtered colimits.

2.5. Infinity Category Dictionary. The notion of ∞-categories was introduced,
since in a lot of fields of mathematics we actually want a weaker notion of equiv-
alence, e.g. homotopy equivalence or quasi-isomorphism. Furthermore, they also
make the notion of derived functor inherent, i.e.
classically we view a derived
functor as something like a universal δ-functor, however using the language of ∞-
categories it turns into the notion of functor between ∞-categories.. What we will
do in this section, is give a brief dictionary on how to translate the 1-categorical
notion into the ∞-categorical notion. First of all the concepts of adjoint functors,
Kan extensions and presentable categories generalize immediately. Furthermore,
the Barr-Beck theorem also holds for ∞-categories by suitably replacing split co-
equalizers by so-called split geometric realizations. To generalize the various kinds
of algebraic structures we have introduced, we will introduce the analogoue of the
category of sets.
Remark 2.33. Due to the infinite tower of coherences it is almost not feasible
to write down a functor by hand. A slogan one could adhere to is to construct
first and compute afterwards. This means we should be using high-level tools to
construct functors, which can be checked afterwards if they yield the formula one
was seeking.
The most basic category one thinks of is the category of sets Set. It has a lot of
good categorical properties, such as completeness and cocompleteness, but perhaps
the property that characterizes the Set best, is the fact that it is generated under
colimits by the point, i.e. we can take the one point set, which we denote ∗and
construct every other set by taking colimits, which in this case one simply takes
the disjoint union of points to construct every other set. So to formulate, what
the higher categorical analogue is supposed to be, one morally approaches this
from the perspective, of which category is generated under homotopy colimits by a
single point. The answer is the category of CW-complexes.

Another feature of this approach is that it internalizes extra structure. To elaborate
on that, we will shortly be discussing D-modules, locally constant sheaves and Higgs
sheaves. All of these objects are special types of sheaves. Using transmutations,
we can realize all of these objects as quasi-coherent sheaves on their respective
transmutation stack. In this section, we will see that quasi-coherent sheaves on
de Rham stack will correspond to D-modules, quasi-coherent sheaves on the Betti

4.1. D-Modules. We use our geometric intuition of interpreting supergeometry as
a variant of derived algebraic geometry and adopt their definition of D-modules,
which can be defined on arbitrary derived prestacks. The goal of this section is
then to prove that the definition coming from derived algebraic geometry produces
D-supermodules, in the case of smooth superschemes and smooth Deligne-Mumford
superstacks. We also note that D-modules were already constructed by Carchedi
in the setting of derived differental supergeometry [Car25] defining the de Rham
stack using a topos-theoretic approach similar to [PS20].
The theory of D-modules runs into technical difficulties, when one tries to gener-
alize to stacks and non-smooth schemes. Consequently, in the setting of derived
algebraic geometry, one replaces them by the category of crystals. We will follow
this description of D-modules using the category of crystals as a replacement and
show that, in the case of smooth Deligne-Mumford superstacks, it is the category of
D-modules, which will take up most of the section. This requires a decent amount
of setup, but this viewpoint is very flexible and makes it easily adaptable to derived
algebraic geometry [GR23] or as we do here, to supergeometry. Furthermore, this
viewpoint also allows us to reprove and generalize, in a simple fashion, a classical
theorem due to Penkov, which states:
Theorem 4.1 ([Pen83]). Let X be a complex supermanifold. Then the category
of D-supermodules on X is equivalent to the category of D-supermodules on the
bosonic truncation.
