ABSTRACT
Many details of the gas accretion phase during giant planet formation remain untested. We present
new 0.2–0.7 µm UV-through-red optical imaging of the young, wide-orbit planetary-mass companion
SR 12 c from the Wide Field Camera 3 (WFC3) instrument on board the Hubble Space Telescope.
SR 12 c exhibits strong accretion-related continuum excess blueward of ∼5000 ˚A and clear signs of
the Balmer jump at 3646 ˚A. We derive a total accretion luminosity of 1.65 ± 0.19 × 10−5L⊙and
a mass accretion rate of 8 ± 2 × 10−12 M⊙yr−1.
Based on its mass and age, SR 12 c will not
grow by an appreciable amount at its current accretion rate; it is at the end stages of assembly. No
accretion variability is evident between the two epochs of the WFC3 observations spanning a month-
long baseline, but the Hα emission line strength decreases by 90% compared to the reported flux
from five years earlier.
Combined with previous observations of SR 12 c, we assemble one of the
most complete spectral energy distributions of a young giant planet to date, spanning the UV through
sub-mm wavelengths (0.2–880 µm). This adds SR 12 c to the small yet growing sample of planets
with detailed accretion and disk constraints, which together are beginning to establish the diversity of
timescales and physical processes governing the formation of giant planets.

planet formation
1. INTRODUCTION
Gas giants are the most readily detectable subset of
planets, but details about their origin and evolution have

been challenging to address with observations. In par-
ticular, the exact timing of giant planet formation and
the mechanism through which mass is accreted via the
circumplanetary disk onto the growing planet remains
elusive (Benisty et al. 2023; Adams & Batygin 2025).
There are two primary proposed pathways to form gi-
ant planets: core accretion and disk instability.
The
core/pebble accretion model (Pollack et al. 1996; Inaba

et al. 2003; Alibert et al. 2005; Lambrechts & Johansen
2012; Adams et al. 2025) posits that giant planets form
from the “bottom up” through ∼14 orders of magnitude
in size and ∼40 orders of magnitude in mass.
When
the core reaches a critical mass (predicted to be ∼5-20
M⊕; Papaloizou & Terquem 1999; Rafikov 2006), hydro-
static equilibrium is disrupted, and the planet rapidly
accretes gas from the circumstellar disk to form a giant
planet. However, this method struggles to explain the
presence of giant planets on very wide orbits of hundreds
to thousands of AU beyond even the largest protoplan-
etary disks without invoking dynamical ejection (e.g.,
Bowler 2016).
In the alternative “top down” model of disk instability,
disks that are sufficiently massive and cool are expected
to fragment into low-mass self-gravitating objects (Boss
1997). If these protoplanets continue to accrete, they
may grow to become brown dwarfs or low-mass stars.
Those that do not substantially gain mass can be left
in the planetary regime, representing the tail end of the
stellar and substellar companion mass function (Krat-
ter et al. 2010). Simulations of these fragmented clumps
suggest that substellar objects created via disk insta-
bility may have more massive disks and higher rates of
accretion than those created by the direct collapse of iso-
lated gas cores (Stamatellos & Herczeg 2015), presenting
an observational test that links accretion and the forma-
tion mechanism of giant planets (Durisen et al. 2007).
The bulk of a giant planet’s accreted mass is funneled
through its circumplanetary disk (CPD; Keith & War-
dle 2014; Szul´agyi et al. 2017; Maeda et al. 2022). There
is growing evidence that CPDs are intrinsically com-
mon: Bowler et al. (2017) found that 46 ± 14% of <20
MJup companions with ages <15 Myr show Paβ emis-
sion, a sign of active accretion, while Martinez & Kraus
(2022) found a disk fraction of 56 ± 12% for a compara-
ble range of mass and age. The best way to understand
mass transfer onto a growing giant planet is by studying
accreting protoplanets at early ages (≤10 Myr, Follette
et al. 2023). The majority of a planet’s accretion lu-
minosity (Lacc) lies in the ultraviolet continuum if the
shocked gas is optically thick (Valenti et al. 1993; Calvet
& Gullbring 1998; Zhu 2015). If the shock is optically
thin, hydrogen line emission can contribute significantly
to the total accretion luminosity (Aoyama et al. 2020).
To capture the total Lacc, observations from space pro-
vide the broadest wavelength coverage into the blue-
optical and UV (e.g., Zhou et al. 2014) with instruments
such as the Wide Field Camera 3 (WFC3) on the Hubble
Space Telescope (HST). The ∼2000–5000 ˚A range cover-
ing the Balmer jump at 3646 ˚A, offers a convenient way
to constrain the UV excess and provides a more com-
plete picture of accretion compared to accretion-related
emission lines (such as Hα or Paβ, e.g. Bowler et al.
2011; Demars et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2025; Close et al.
2025a,b). This tactic has been explored extensively in
the stellar and brown dwarf regimes (e.g., Roman-Duval
et al. 2020; Wendeborn et al. 2024), but similar observa-
tions are rare for young giant planets (Zhou et al. 2014,
2021).
At longer wavelengths, the mid-infrared (IR) region
is sensitive to reprocessed radiation from CPDs around
growing giant planets. CPDs are understudied obser-
vationally because relatively few have been detected at
thermal and millimeter wavelengths, suggesting CPDs
are more optically thick or have more compact radii than
expected (Bowler et al. 2015; Wu et al. 2015; Wolff et al.
2017; Isella et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2020; Benisty et al.
2021; Andrews et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2022; Scholz et al.
2023; Patapis et al. 2025; Cugno & Grant 2025; Hoch
et al. 2025). In particular, it is not clear when or how
these disks disperse, the diversity of their physical prop-
erties, and whether grain growth and moon formation
are common phenomena (Benisty et al. 2021).
The most thoroughly-studied protoplanets reside in
the PDS 70 system, which hosts PDS 70 b (Keppler et al.
2018; M¨uller et al. 2018) and c (Haffert et al. 2019), two
accreting protoplanets (Wagner et al. 2018; Zhou et al.
2021) embedded in the transition disk of this ∼5 Myr
Sun-like host star.
Isella et al. (2019) tentatively de-
tected a compact mm source at the position of PDS 70
c, which was recovered in follow-up ALMA observations
by Benisty et al. (2021) at high significance, providing
the most compelling evidence for a CPD surrounding the
planet. Blakely et al. (2025) explored the mid-IR excess
emission of PDS 70 c with JWST/NIRISS and found
that models that include a CPD offer a good fit to its
1–5 µm SED.1 More recently, the discoveries of 2MASS
J16120668-301027 b (Li et al. 2025) and WISPIT 2b
(van Capelleveen et al. 2025; Close et al. 2025b) have
increased the sample of young accreting planets to study
planet-disk interactions and directly witness young plan-
ets gaining mass. Although these systems have provided
an unprecedented window into the planet formation pro-
cess, their high planet-to-star contrasts and close angu-
lar separations have made it challenging to carry out
detailed studies of the accretion and disk properties of
these planets.

At wider orbital distances, direct imaging campaigns

host stars (Bowler 2016).
The census of wide plan-

These distant planets complicate the tra-

1.1. The Young Wide Companion SR 12 c
At a mean distance2 of 139 ± 5 pc (Ratzenb¨ock
et al. 2023), the ρ Ophiuchi cloud complex is a low-
density star-forming region that contains several hun-
dred young stars, including the close (0.′′2) binary SR
12 AB (2MASS J16271951–2441403) in the Lynds 1688
(L1688) cloud (Wilking et al. 2008). Bowler et al. (2014)
used evolutionary models (Baraffe et al. 1998) to esti-
mate masses of ≈1.0 and ≈0.5 M⊙for SR 12 A and B,
respectively, based on the age of ∼2 Myr from Kuzuhara
et al. (2011), component luminosities from Wahhaj et al.
(2010), and component effective temperatures from Luh-
man & Rieke (1999). The low-mass companion to SR 12
AB, SR 12 c, was discovered and confirmed to have com-
mon proper motion with its host binary by Kuzuhara
et al. (2011).
Reported values for its model-inferred
mass have varied based on the estimated distance, lumi-
nosity, and age. Here, we adopt 16 ± 2 MJup from Wu
et al. (submitted) which assumes a slightly older sys-
tem age of 3–4 Myr.3 SR 12 c is on an extremely wide
orbit, separated from its host binary by a projected sep-
aration of ≈1200 AU (8.′′7, Kuzuhara et al. 2011). This
makes it readily accessible to detailed follow-up obser-

Following its discovery, an abundance of imaging and

Kuzuhara et al. (2011) presented J-, H-

Sutherland, South Africa. This was supplemented with
H-, K- and L’- band photometry from Subaru’s Infrared

the red colors, low temperature, and planetary mass of

initial entropy (Marley et al. 2007).
Follow-up IRTF/SpeX near-IR spectra of SR 12 c from
Bowler et al. (2014) revealed an angular H -band shape–
a sign of low surface gravity and youth—and a spectral
type of M9 ± 0.5. They also note an absence of sub-
stantial Paβ emission at 1.28 µm, ruling out the type of
strong accretion evident in several other wide planetary-
mass companions (e.g., Bowler et al. 2011; Zhou et al.
2014; Wu et al. 2015; Betti et al. 2022).
Using the X-shooter instrument on the Very Large
Telescope (VLT), Santamar´ıa-Miranda et al. (2018) pre-
sented a 0.3 to 2.5 µm spectrum for SR 12 c, which
revealed prominent hydrogen emission lines, including
exceptionally strong Hα emission signifying ongoing ac-
cretion, with an estimated total accretion rate of ≈10−11
M⊙yr−1 (Santamar´ıa-Miranda et al. 2019). They also
carried out a maximum likelihood fit of BT-Settl models
from Allard (2014) and determine a log g of 4.0 ± 0.5
and an effective temperature of 2600 ± 100 K.
Probing further into the infrared, Martinez & Kraus
(2022) presented Spitzer/IRAC photometry, which con-
firmed the mid-IR excess observed in SR 12 c, indicating
the presence of a warm CPD surrounding the planet.
The CPD dust was subsequently detected with the At-
acama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA) by Wu et al.
(2022) at 880 µm, corresponding to ≈1 lunar mass of
dust, assuming a mean disk temperature of 24 K and op-
tically thin emission. The estimated dust mass is about
two times greater than the PDS 70 c disk (Benisty et al.
2021).

2. OBSERVATIONS AND DATA ANALYSIS
2.1. HST WFC3/UVIS Imaging
SR 12 c was imaged in 5 filters from ∼2200-6700 ˚A
with the UVIS arm of the WFC3 instrument on board
the Hubble Space Telescope as a part of the general ob-
server (GO) program 16302 (PI: Wu) on two dates: UT
2021 February 28 and UT 2021 March 26.
A three-
point line dither pattern was used with a point spacing
of 0.′′135. The observations utilized the SUB-C512C sub-
array of the UVIS2 channel, a 513 x 512 subarray near
amplifier C. SR 12 c was observed in the F225W and
F336W filters to sample wavelengths shorter than the
Balmer break (see Section 3.2 below), the F438W and
F555W filters to measure the optical continuum redward
of the Balmer break, and the F656N filter to measure
flux from the Hα emission line. Integration times and
signal-to-noise ratios for the companion are listed in Ta-
ble 2. An image of the system architecture is shown in
Figure 1. Images in each filter are shown for the host
binary and the companion in Figure 2.
2.2. Extinction
Kuzuhara et al. (2011) compared a young M9 stan-
dard spectrum to the extinction-corrected spectrum of
SR 12 c and reported a J-band extinction AJ of 0.5
± 0.2 mag. Bowler et al. (2014) converted this to an
AV of 1.7 ± 0.7 mag using the AJ/AV relationship from
Rieke & Lebofsky (1985). Martinez & Kraus (2022) and
Wu et al. (2022) adopted this value, while Santamar´ıa-
Miranda et al. (2018) made their own measurement of
extinction from their X-shooter spectrum, letting AV
vary as a free parameter in their photospheric model
fits, yielding a value of 1.24 mag. Determining an accu-
rate extinction is especially important for correctly es-
tablishing the accretion luminosity and mass accretion
rate for SR 12 c, as the UV photometry will be most
sensitive to the precision and accuracy of the inferred
AV . Given the various previous estimates of extinction,
each with a large uncertainty, here we conducted our
own investigation of the extinction to this system.
Our approach involves fitting a photosphere model
from the BT-Settl (Allard et al. 2012) grid to the SED
of the host binary, SR 12 AB, and allowing extinction to
vary. Given the comparable brightness and colors of SR
12 A and B (Simon et al. 1987; Wilking et al. 2005), we
model them as a single unresolved source with one pho-
tospheric temperature, representing a star with proper-
ties between the two binary components. We compile
published unresolved photometry of the binary com-
ponents spanning 0.44 to 11.6 µm. We then measure
unresolved HST/WFC3-UVIS aperture photometry for
the binary following the same methodology we used for
the companion (see Section 2.2.1 below).

Several studies have independently measured the (un-
resolved) spectral type of SR 12 AB to be M0 (e.g.,
Luhman & Rieke 1999; Wahhaj et al. 2010; Pecaut &
Mamajek 2016). We therefore adopt an uncertainty of
0.5 subtypes for the (unresolved) spectrum of SR 12
AB. This corresponds to upper and lower uncertainties
of +15 K and –85 K, respectively (Pecaut & Mamajek
2013). The BT-Settl models are sampled with a grid
spacing of 100 K, so the 3700 K and 3900 K models
(about twice the implied effective temperature range)
are initially chosen for this exercise. This results in an
extinction of 0.85 ± 0.34 mag; however, we halve these
uncertainties to reflect the higher level of precision in
spectral classification and adopt a final AV of 0.85 ±
0.17 mag for both SR 12 AB and SR 12 c.
This is
slightly lower than previous estimates, but higher ex-
tinction values are incompatible with the observed SED
and under-predict the photometry of SR 12 AB at short
wavelengths. Though lower, this value is still within 2σ
of previous estimates.
To assess how the assumption of a single-component
model impacts our estimate of AV , we use the resolved
K-band photometry from Simon et al. (1987) along with
evolutionary models from Baraffe et al. (2015) to con-
struct a two-component model with effective tempera-

adjusting the relative scaling of each component based
on the K-band flux ratio and summing the two photo-
spheres, we find a best-fit extinction of 0.87 mag—nearly
identical to our single-component model.
Extinction values in the ρ Oph cloud and the L1688
cloud core in particular can range from AV =0 to as high
as AV =20 mag (C´anovas et al. 2019).
Rizzuto et al.
(2015) found that for a population of 20 members of
the ρ Oph complex without detected disks, the aver-
age extinction is AV = 0.9 ± 0.6 mag. Our estimate of
0.85 ± 0.17 mag therefore agrees well with other non-
embedded members of ρ Oph that, like SR 12 AB, do
not have disks. Because extinction does not significantly
vary in this region over a few arcseconds (C´anovas et al.
2019), we assume SR 12 c shares the same AV as SR
12 AB. Additionally, we do not consider local reddening
at the source and assume the CPD of SR 12 c is not
contributing to extinction.
2.2.1. HST Photometry
We began by downloading the drc HST/WFC3-UVIS
FITS files from the Mikulski Archive for Space Tele-
scopes (MAST) at the Space Telescope Science Insti-
tute5. The drc extension denotes that the frames have
been processed through AstroDrizzle (Fruchter 2010),
the Python-based pipeline automating the cosmic-ray
removal and subsequent aligning and combining of
WFC3 images from each dither, as well as correcting
for geometric distortion and charge-transfer efficiency.
In this case, 3 dithers were combined to create one drc
frame.
The host binary is marginally resolved in all
frames with an elongation largely in the east-west di-
rection. SR 12 c is visually evident in all frames, but
is rather faint in the UV filters (F225W and F336W).
To compensate for this, we co-add the frames for the
two epochs (∼1 month apart) in each of the 5 filters.
Later, in Section 4.1.1, we explore photometric variabil-
ity across this month-long time baseline between each
epoch.
Our approach to precisely register both north-aligned
images is to cross correlate the processed frames from
February (“A” frames) with those from March (“B”).
The cross-correlation between the A and B frames is
computed using the phase cross correlation rou-
tine in the Python scikit-image package to deter-
mine the x and y positional offsets between the two
frames.
This is driven by the position of SR 12 AB,
which is the brightest object in each image. No image
rotation is applied, as each frame has the same position
angle of reference aperture center as determined by the
header keyword ORIENTAT, reported in degrees East of
North. We perform a sub-pixel shift using the scipy
function ndimage.fourier shift to align the im-
ages and co-added the frames into one combined image
for each filter.
The FITS files are converted to flux density in units
of erg s−1 cm−2 ˚A−1 via the inverse sensitivity con-
version factors in the PHOTFLAM FITS header key-
word.
Aperture photometry is then carried out with
the photutils package on each co-added frame using
an aperture radius of 2 times the PSF FWHM at a given
wavelength, corresponding to 3.94 pix for F225W, 3.61
pix for F336W, 3.41 pix for F438W, 3.36 pix for F555W,
and 3.43 pix for F656N. The FWHM decreases from the
UV to the blue-optical filters because the near-UV filters
are impacted by imager polishing errors, which trans-
fer power from the PSF core to the non-Gaussian PSF
wings, widening and strengthening the wings and the
FWHM.

3.1. Photosphere Models
Thermal emission from SR 12 c dominates its SED at
near-infrared wavelengths. For this region, we use at-
mospheric models from the BT-Settl grid (Allard et al.
2012) and normalize its peak to the location of the re-
ported SIRIUS J-band photometry, which represents a
balance between accretion excess at bluer wavelengths
and disk emission at longer wavelengths. Santamar´ıa-
Miranda et al. (2018) reported that the best model at-
mosphere fits to their X-shooter spectrum of SR 12 c
fall in the range of 2500–2800 K with log g between 3.5
and 4.5. For this work we adopt a solar metallicity 2600
K model with a surface gravity of 3.5, as this is most
consistent with model predictions given the bolometric
luminosity of SR 12 c and an age of 3-4 Myr (e.g. Baraffe
et al. 2015; Phillips et al. 2020). Note that the photo-
spheric contribution is negligible shortward of ∼4000 ˚A
and longward of ∼7 µm and thus does not significantly
change our interpretation of either the UV or mid-IR
excess.
3.2. Accretion Models
The shortest-wavelength portion of the SED of SR 12
c is the UV through red-optical regime.
The Balmer
jump at 3646 ˚A is a convenient diagnostic of mass ac-
cretion rate. It is created by a sharp rise in opacity at λ
< 3645 ˚A from photoionization of excited (n=2) levels
in the hot, dense accretion shock region. Figure 5 shows
the strength of the Balmer jump (quantified as the ratio
of flux before and after the jump, at 3600 ˚A and 3700
˚A, respectively) as a function of local hydrogen number
density and gas temperature. Low densities and temper-
atures result in the largest Balmer jumps with flux ra-
tios approaching 100 across the discontinuity, while high
temperatures and densities produce the weakest breaks.
To quantify the Balmer jump and the excess blue con-
tinuum emission, we utilize a hydrogen slab model as
introduced by Valenti et al. (1993). The simple hydro-

gen slab models are agnostic to the mechanism and ge-

precise determination of the accretion luminosity, and
hence the mass accretion rate. If the accretion shock
region is optically thick, this approach is expected to
be substantially more accurate than estimates through
line emission alone (such as Hα) (Herczeg & Hillenbrand
2008; Ardila et al. 2013; Ingleby et al. 2013; Alcal´a et al.
2014, 2017; Robinson & Espaillat 2019). However, UV
wavelengths suffer more heavily from uncertainties in
extinction.
In these models, Valenti et al. (1993) assumed the
post-shock emission could be approximated by an
isothermal, plane-parallel slab of pure hydrogen in lo-
cal thermal equilibrium. Free parameters include tem-
perature (T), column density (ne), path length of the
emitting gas (effectively the gas opacity), turbulent ve-
locity, and filling factor of the accretion slab. Increasing
the volume of the slab modulates the relative propor-
tions of the emission from the accreting area and emis-
sion from the host planet; and adjusting the length of
the slab relative to its surface area affects the total op-
tical depth through the slab.
T increases the kinetic
energy of the hydrogen atoms in the UV continuum, di-
rectly impacting the size of the Balmer jump, while ne
primarily affects the wings of the Balmer jump, chang-
ing the shape of the feature rather than its size. This
breaks any potential degeneracy where the same SED
could be described by either higher T or higher ne. For
this study, we focus on constraining the region surround-
ing the Balmer jump, which can then be used to deter-
mine the accretion flux and luminosity.
Herczeg et al. (2009) found that two parameters, tem-
perature and number density, are expected to dominate
the shape and behavior of the slab models, along with a
linear, multiplicative normalization constant adjusting
the overall amplitude of the model. The path length of
the slab does not significantly impact the accretion lu-
minosity measurement, and the filling factor is absorbed
by the linear scaling constant when used to adjust the
amplitude of the slab model. We follow this same ap-
proach by only considering temperature, density, and
the scaling constant. Figure 6 shows the effect of these

4.1.1. Accretion Variability
Recent observations of young, accreting planets like
PDS 70 b and c (Zhou et al. 2025; Close et al. 2025a) and
the candidate protoplanet AB Aur b (Currie et al. 2022;
Zhou et al. 2023; Bowler et al. 2025) show clear evidence
that Hα varies on timescales of days to years. Variability
in planetary-mass accretion rates indicates a non-static
accretion process, and implies some degree of stochastic
mass transfer from the circumplanetary accretion disk.
This is not surprising, given that typical protostellar
accretion is known to be highly variable on timescales
from hours to years (e.g., Hartmann et al. 2016).
The HST/WFC3 observations were taken with an
identical setup in all five filters in two epochs on UT
2021 February 28 and UT 2021 March 26. Here, we ex-
amine potential variability spanning this ∼month-long
baseline.
Comparing the observed flux densities from
Epoch 1 to Epoch 2, we do not find signs of significant
(>3σ) variability. Figure 9 shows a depiction of the per-
cent change in flux between HST observations. Given
the non-detection of variability in all UV and optical
filters, we have co-added these epochs and adopted the
resulting photometry for this study (see Section 2.2.1).
Individual and averaged measurements for all filters are
reported in Table 3.

4.2. Comparison to Other Accreting Substellar Objects
with Measured Continuum Excess
There are a growing number of accreting bound and
free-floating planetary-mass objects with accretion rate
estimates (cataloged in the CASPAR database, Betti
et al. 2023), but most of these are based solely on Hα
emission. Here we summarize results for those with ac-
cretion rates determined from UV excess using observa-
tions from HST/WFC3.
Zhou et al. (2014) reported Lacc and
˙M values for
three young substellar objects, two of which are similar
to SR 12 c in mass (≈12-15 MJup): GSC 06214-00210 b
(Ireland et al. 2011; Zhou et al. 2014; Demars et al. 2023;
Xuan et al. 2024) and DH Tau b (Itoh et al. 2005; Xuan
et al. 2024). The third object, GQ Lup B (Neuh¨auser
et al. 2005), has a somewhat higher mass estimate of 10–
40 MJup (Marois et al. 2007; Seifahrt et al. 2007; Cugno
et al. 2024; Xuan et al. 2024). Zhou et al. (2021) also
reported accretion luminosity and mass accretion rate
measurements for the protoplanet PDS 70 b (∼3 MJup,
Wang et al. 2021).

nosity, and corresponds to the highest-mass companion

GSC 06214-00210 b. Note, however, that direct com-
parisons among these objects is complicated by many
factors—companion mass, system age, viewing geome-
try, and accretion variability.

Myr. Alternatively, bound planetary-mass companions

reservoir of available gas in the circumstellar disk to feed
accretion.

larger sample of accreting planets is needed to distin-

5. CONCLUSION
In this work, we present newly acquired HST/WFC3
imaging for the wide-orbit, accreting planetary-mass
companion SR 12 c which hosts a circumplanetary disk
(Martinez & Kraus 2022; Wu et al. 2022).
We re-
port UV-through-red optical photometry for SR 12 c
and combine it with previous observations and new
JWST observations to construct an SED with the most-
complete wavelength coverage for a young, accreting gi-
ant planet to date. We also report an updated estimate
of the extinction to the system of AV = 0.85 ± 0.17
mag based on photometry of SR 12 AB. We model the
UV through mid-IR SED with three contributions: a
hydrogen slab model that dominates in the UV, a pho-
tosphere model, and a two-temperature blackbody disk
model described in detail in Wu et al., (submitted).
We infer an accretion luminosity of 1.65± 0.19 ×
10−5L⊙and mass accretion rate of 8 ± 2 × 10−12 M⊙
yr−1 by fitting hydrogen slab models to the UV pho-
tometry across the Balmer jump. We find this derived
accretion luminosity to be in agreement with the inferred
accretion luminosity derived from the Hα flux using the
relations in Alcal´a et al. (2017), affirming that the con-
nection between line luminosity and continuum excess
persists down to planetary masses.
We reinforce the
general conclusions presented in Demars et al. (2023) for
other planetary-mass companions, finding the Hα emis-
sion of SR 12 c is not significantly variable over a short
baseline of a month, but varies by 90% over a longer
baseline of five years.
When taking the accretion properties of SR 12
c in conjunction with results from the 5.6–21 µm
JWST/MIRI photometry, a picture emerges of a young
planet that has nearly finished accreting gas from its
circumplanetary disk, which itself has undergone a level
of grain growth and dust settling that is indicative of a
later evolutionary stage (Wu et al., submitted.) At the
present rate of accretion, SR 12 c will only increase in
mass by ∼0.06% over the next Myr; this seems to be
the final stage of growth for this object. Expanding the
sample of wide orbit and free-floating giant planets with
measurements of accretion in the UV and CPD struc-
ture in the mid-IR and sub-mm wavelengths is key to
informing the timescale and mechanism by which proto-
planets accrete mass, constraining the timescale of giant
planet and potentially exomoon formation.
