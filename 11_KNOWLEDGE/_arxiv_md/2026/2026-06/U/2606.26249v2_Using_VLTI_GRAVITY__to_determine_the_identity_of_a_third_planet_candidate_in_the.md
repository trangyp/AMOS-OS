ABSTRACT
Detections of protoplanets are rare and protoplanetary disk features mischaracterized as planets are common. PDS 70 is one of only two stars known
to host multiple confirmed protoplanets, PDS 70 b and c, and repeat detections of a third point-like source in the system suggest the presence of
third inner planet. However, previous observations of this third source are insufficient to distinguish whether it is a planet or a concentrated dust
clump in Keplerian motion. Our observations with VLTI/GRAVITY+ did not re-detect this point-like source, suggesting that it is, in fact, a dust
clump and not a planet. These observations demonstrate how the angular resolving power of VLTI/GRAVITY+ can be used to distinguish between
protoplanets and protoplanetary disk features.
Key words. Planets and satellites: formation – Techniques: high angular resolution – Planets and satellites: detection
1. Introduction
Protoplanets are a common explanation for the ubiquity of sub-
structure in protoplanetary disks (e.g., Dong et al. 2015; Zhang
et al. 2018). Despite several large surveys (Currie et al. 2019;
Xie et al. 2020; Zurlo et al. 2020; Follette et al. 2023), proto-
planets have only been successfully detected in a handful of sys-
tems, including PDS 70 (Keppler et al. 2018; Haffert et al. 2019),
AB Aurigae (Currie et al. 2022), HD 169142 (Hammond et al.
2023), WISPIT 2 (Close et al. 2025; van Capelleveen et al. 2025;
Lawlor et al. 2026), and 2MASS J16120668-3010270 (Li et al.
2025).
PDS 70 is host to two known protoplanets, PDS 70 b and
c, and it is currently one of only two stars around which multi-
ple protoplanets have been confirmed. These two planets were
detected in Hα (Haffert et al. 2019), indicating ongoing accre-
tion, and their near-infrared photometry is consistent with ther-
mal emission (Keppler et al. 2018; Mesa et al. 2019; Wang et al.
2021; Blakely et al. 2025).
Multiple papers have reported the detection of a third poten-
tial planet in the PDS 70 system. Mesa et al. (2019) observed a
point-like source interior to PDS 70 b and c to the northwest of
the star with VLT/SPHERE. Christiaens et al. (2024) redetected
this source with JWST/NIRCam. This source also appeared in
re-reductions of archival VLT/SPHERE and VLT/SINFONI data
spanning 10 years by Hammond et al. (2025). However, the cur-
rent observations of this source are insufficient to differentiate if
it is a true protoplanet or only a concentrated dust clump. The
source has not been detected in Hα, and its infrared photome-
try more closely matches starlight reflected off dust than thermal
emission from a planet (Hammond et al. 2025).
For most current telescopes, observation of the third planet
candidate is challenging since its separation from its host star
is small (∼100 mas). The GRAVITY instrument on the Very
Large Telescope Interferometer (VLTI) is therefore well placed
to search for the inner planets given its ability to produce sub-
milliarcsecond angular resolution observations in the K band
(e.g., Nowak et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2021). The recent GRAV-
ITY+ upgrade to this instrument, consisting of an upgrade to the
adaptive optics system, is designed to improve these qualities
even further, pushing the instrument to higher contrasts at short
separations (Gravity+ Collaboration et al. 2026).
In this letter we describe our attempt to observe this third
planet candidate with VLTI/GRAVITY+ and the subsequent
non-detection. In Section 2 we describe our observations and
how we reduced the observational data. In Section 3 we discuss
the implications of our non-detection and state our conclusions
in Section 4.

2. Observations and data reduction
2.1. GRAVITY+ observations
We observed PDS 70 on 16 February 2025 with the GRAVITY+
instrument using the four 8m Unit Telescopes (UTs) of ESO’s
VLTI. The star PDS 70 A itself served as the visual natural guide
star for the adaptive optics and as the K-band fringe tracking
reference source. We collected data using the K-band medium
resolution mode (R ≈500). The observing setup we used fol-
lowed the strategy outlined in Nowak et al. (2020) and Wang
et al. (2021), where integrations with the science fiber set on
the central star are placed between planet integrations. Table 1
gives an overview of the integration times, atmospheric condi-
tions, and coherence time (τ0).

2.2. Data reduction
The data were reduced using Public Release 1.7.0 of the ESO
GRAVITY pipeline (Lapeyrère et al. 2014). They were reduced
up until the ”astroreduced” intermediate data products, in which
individual integrations are not averaged.
Subsequent analysis used the exoGRAVITY pipeline, follow-
ing a similar procedure to the one described in Appendix B of
Nowak et al. (2020). Equation B.4 of Nowak et al. (2020) de-
scribes the on-planet visibility (Vmodel) for a planet observed by
GRAVITY, which depends on the relative RA (∆α) and Dec.
(∆δ) from the star. For any given set of GRAVITY data with vis-
ibilities Vdata, a χ2 sum can be calculated to find the likelihood
that the data corresponds to planet emission:
χ2
planet(∆α, ∆δ) = [Vdata −Vmodel]TW−1[Vdata −Vmodel],
(1)
where W is the corresponding covariance matrix. A χ2 map can
be generated by calculating χ2 for a set of (∆α, ∆δ) points in the
GRAVITY FOV.
If we take (∆α, ∆δ) = (0, 0), this corresponds to a model
with no planet, which we can use as a reference. By comparing
the planet and no planet models, we can define
z(∆α, ∆δ) = χ2
no planet −χ2
planet(∆α, ∆δ).
(2)
We note that z(∆α, ∆δ) compares the likelihood of models with
and without a planet at the location (∆α, ∆δ).
Figure 2 shows the map of z for the observation described
in this letter. Both panels show the results from our observation,
but for the right panel, a fake planet signal was injected into the
visibility data at the expected position for the planet candidate.
The injected signal takes the form of Equation B.4 from Nowak
et al. (2020) with a contrast of 8.4 magnitudes, matching the K-
band contrast for the planet candidate given by Hammond et al.
(2025). From these z maps we determined that there is no planet
signal in our attempted observation of the third planet candidate.
3. Discussion
Our non-detection of the candidate suggests that it is not a planet
and is instead a concentrated dust clump. The existing K-band
photometry of the candidate (Hammond et al. 2025) indicates
that it has a contrast similar to PDS 70 c. However, PDS 70 c ap-
pears as a point source after the data reduction process described
in Section 2.2 (Wang et al. 2021; Trevascus et al. 2025), while
this candidate does not. This is also consistent with the spectrum
of the candidate, which resembles reflected starlight more than
thermal emission (Hammond et al. 2025).
The key difference between our observations and previous
detections of this source (e.g., with VLT/SPHERE Mesa et al.
2019; Hammond et al. 2025) is the increased resolving power
granted via the long baseline of GRAVITY (∼100 m). GRAV-
ITY has a nominal angular resolution of 2 mas, corresponding
to ∼0.2 au at the distance of PDS 70. Any significantly smaller
object, such as a Jupiter-sized planet (2RJup = 0.001 au), should
therefore have high visibility and appear as a point-like source.
A source of size ∼0.2 au or larger, such as a dust clump, may
appear point-like for other instruments but not for GRAVITY.
The visibility model described in Section 2.2 corresponds
specifically to a point-like source and therefore is not suitable
for the detection of extended sources, such as dust clumps. From
our analysis alone we are only able to put a rough lower limit on
the extent of the source, and we leave further visibility modeling
to future work.
It is still possible that the GRAVITY science fiber was not
well positioned to image the third planet candidate, especially
given that a pointing error of 30 mas or more can result in a
significant loss of flux (Perrin & Woillez 2019). Orbit modeling
from Trevascus et al. (2025) was used to predict the position of
the planet candidate on the night of observation. However, the
observation was performed before the orbit modeling was final-
ized and therefore the FOV does not cover the outermost orbits
as shown in Figure 1. The predicted location of the planet based
on its median stable orbit is still situated within the FOV, al-
though it is separated from the center of the FOV by 22.5 mas.
We also used the range of stable orbit parameters for the planet
candidate from Trevascus et al. (2025) to determine the proba-
bility that the candidate was outside the FOV on the night of the
observation. We found that 12.6 percent of the possible positions
lay outside the FOV.
3.1. Trojan dust
Blakely et al. (2025) also did not detect a third planet candidate
to the northwest of PDS 70 when observing it with the Aper-
ture Masking Interferometric mode of JWST/NIRISS. They did,
however, detect excess emission to the south of the star that was
not co-located with PDS 70 b or c.
Recent multiwavelength VLT/SPHERE observations by
Balsalobre-Ruza et al. (2026) identified additional emission with
a dust-like spectrum at a similar separation from the star as the
point source detected by Mesa et al. (2019), Christiaens et al.
(2024), and Hammond et al. (2025) but located to the southeast
of the star. Their follow-up VLTI/GRAVITY+ observations re-
sulted in the tentative detection of a point source with a posi-
tion angle compatible with the JWST/NIRISS excess detected
by Blakely et al. (2025).
Balsalobre-Ruza et al. (2026) proposed that both the south-
east and northwest emissions are Trojan dust collected within the
L4 and L5 Lagrangian points of a new candidate inner planet,
matching theoretical simulations (Montesinos et al. 2020). All
the new detected sources lie along the orbit predicted by Ham-
mond et al. (2025) for a potential third inner planet correspond-
ing to the northwest emission.

3.2. Differentiating between planets and dust substructure
PDS 70 is not the first directly imaged system in which emission
sources have been misidentified as planet candidates. Kalas et al.
(2008) observed an optical point source orbiting the star Formal-
haut that they characterized as a planet candidate. However the
candidate was not detected in the near-infrared (Marengo et al.
2009), and follow-up observations by Gaspar & Rieke (2020)
observed that the source had extended in size and faded in bright-
ness. This source is now characterized as a dust cloud created
after the collision of two planetesimals due to the detection of a
similar event in the same system (Kalas et al. 2026).
A similar case study exists for the young (∼2 Myr) sun-like
star LkCa 15. Kraus & Ireland (2012) and Sallum et al. (2015)
claimed the detection of multiple protoplanets orbiting this star
from observations in the near-infrared. However follow-up ob-
servations by Currie et al. (2019) and Sallum et al. (2023) iden-
tified these signals as parts of the inner dust disk.
As evidenced by the observations described in this letter, in-
terferometry can be a useful tool to differentiate between true
planets and dust disk substructure. Therefore instruments such
as VLTI/GRAVITY+ are particularly powerful for identifying
planets at small separations.
4. Conclusions
We attempted to observe the candidate third planet in the PDS
70 system with VLTI/GRAVITY+. Our observations did not de-
tect point source emission at the expected location of this candi-
date. Therefore we conclude that the previously detected emis-
sion sources correspond to a dust clump instead of a planet.
