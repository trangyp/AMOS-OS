HYPERELLIPTIC STABLE CURVES
MAX SCHWEGELE
Abstract. We provide an intrinsic characterization of hyperelliptic stable curves of genus
g ≥2, independent of admissible covers or auxiliary moduli data. A stable curve is hyperelliptic
if it admits an involution yielding a rational tree quotient, subject to a characteristic-dependent
condition. By analyzing the action of this involution on the nodes and decomposing the curve
based on its connectivity, we obtain an explicit structural description of hyperellipticity and
prove that the hyperelliptic involution is unique. Furthermore, we explain the connection to
the very ampleness of the dualizing sheaf. This framework applies in arbitrary characteristic,
explicitly capturing the divergent geometric and combinatorial behavior in characteristic 2.
We verify that this formulation precisely captures the geometric points of the moduli stack of
hyperelliptic stable curves Hg, defined as the scheme-theoretic closure of the smooth hyperelliptic
locus Hg within the moduli stack of stable curves Mg. Extending this definition to flat families
yields an explicit modular description of Hg over Spec Z[1/2].
1. Introduction
Let g ≥2. We denote by Mg the moduli stack of smooth curves of genus g and by Mg its
Deligne–Mumford compactification, which parametrizes stable curves. Within Mg, we consider
the locus Hg of smooth hyperelliptic curves. We define the moduli stack of hyperelliptic stable
curves, denoted Hg, as the scheme-theoretic closure of Hg in Mg. A stable curve C over an
algebraically closed field k is called hyperelliptic if it corresponds to a geometric point of Hg.
Our main objective is to provide an intrinsic and practical criterion for when a stable curve
C is hyperelliptic. This is motivated by the fact that standard definitions in the literature can
be cumbersome for concrete geometric applications. Many sources adopt a moduli-theoretic
approach to define hyperelliptic stable curves (see [5, 11, 2]). This perspective depends on
extrinsic data, making it impractical when one wishes to verify hyperellipticity using only the
geometry of a given curve. An alternative approach utilizes the theory of admissible covers (see
[12, 11, 1]). While more geometric, it shares a similar drawback: it focuses on the degeneration
of the cover, where the source of the degenerated cover is generally not the stable limit itself.
Instead, one must subsequently contract components to obtain the actual stable curve (see
[11, Theorem 3.160]). Our intrinsic approach circumvents these issues, allowing one to verify
hyperellipticity directly from the geometry of a concrete curve without relying on such auxiliary
data.
To achieve this via a systematic decomposition, it is natural to work in the more general setting
of pointed stable curves. More precisely, we say a stable pointed curve (C, P) is hyperelliptic if it
admits an involution σ ∈Autk(C, P) such that the quotient T := C/⟨σ⟩is a rational tree (i.e., a
semistable curve of arithmetic genus 0), and, if char k ̸= 2, the involution σ does not act as the
identity on any irreducible component of C. Such an automorphism σ is called a hyperelliptic
involution.
With this definition in place, our main results are the following:
Theorem 1.1 (see Theorem 4.5). The hyperelliptic involution on a hyperelliptic stable pointed
curve is uniquely determined.

These results are not entirely new and are likely mostly known to experts. Variations of
our intrinsic definition of hyperelliptic stable curves have appeared in the literature. Over the
complex numbers C, an equivalent definition is used by Arbarello, Cornalba, and Griffiths [2,
Chapter X, Section 3]. It is well understood in the literature that this definition extends without
complications to arbitrary algebraically closed fields with char k ̸= 2 (an explicit formulation
can be found, for example, in [14, Definition 4.1]). The general case, including characteristic 2,
first appears in the work of Maugeais [17, Definition 3.12] (we refer to Remark 3.2 for a detailed
comparison of these definitions across the literature). Regarding the uniqueness of the involution,
a proof for unpointed curves over C was given in [2, Chapter X, Lemma 3.5], proceeding by
induction on the number of components of the quotient tree T. Our approach, by contrast,
inducts directly on the separating nodes and pairs of C. The terminology for this decomposition
strategy—such as semicompact curves and 2-inseparable components—is borrowed from Ran [18],
though our intrinsic geometric methods remain entirely independent. This direct focus offers
the distinct advantage of allowing one to systematically deduce the potential hyperellipticity of
a combinatorial type without needing a priori knowledge of the quotient map to T.
Unlike most sources in the literature, our framework explicitly incorporates fields of character-
istic 2, allowing us to thoroughly investigate the divergent behavior of hyperelliptic stable curves
in this setting. By dropping the second condition of our definition—namely, the requirement
that the involution does not act as the identity on any irreducible component—we admit new
geometric configurations. Proposition 4.7 precisely explains which combinatorial types are
affected by this relaxation, giving rise to cases that are exclusively hyperelliptic in characteristic
2. Note that since M2 = H2, every stable curve of genus 2 is automatically hyperelliptic. Thus,
genus g = 3 represents the first case where these characteristic-dependent phenomena can be
observed. The phenomenon described by Proposition 4.7 is illustrated for a genus 3 curve in
Example 4.9. Conversely, as a consequence of wild ramification, certain combinatorial types are
forced to be strictly non-hyperelliptic in characteristic 2; we demonstrate this in Example 4.10.
An exhaustive analysis of all g = 3 cases can be found in our joint work on the semistable
reduction of plane quartics [21, Proposition 1.14].

2. Stable Curves and Dual Graphs
In this section, we fix the notation and review the combinatorial properties of stable curves
necessary for our subsequent decomposition results. We fix an algebraically closed base field k,
and all curves are assumed to be defined over k.
2.1. Stable Pointed Curves. We begin by establishing our conventions for semistable curves
and their stability conditions in the pointed setting. Throughout this paper, by a semistable
curve, we mean a connected, projective, reduced scheme of dimension one over k whose only
singularities are ordinary double points (nodes). We denote the arithmetic genus of a semistable
curve C by g(C) := pa(C).
Definition 2.1. An n-pointed semistable curve is a pair (C, P) consisting of a semistable curve
C and an n-tuple P = (p1, . . . , pn) of distinct smooth points on C. We call (C, P) a stable
n-pointed curve if the automorphism group Autk(C, P)—the group of automorphisms of C that
fix each point pi ∈P—is finite. When n = 0, we omit the tuple P and simply refer to the stable
curve C.
This finiteness condition is equivalent to the ampleness of the log-canonical sheaf ωC/k(P),
where P denotes the divisor p1 + · · · + pn. More explicitly, this requires that 2g(Z) −2 + sZ > 0
for every irreducible component Z of C, where sZ denotes the number of special points on Z
(the marked points and the nodes connecting Z to the rest of the curve). Observe that this
condition is non-trivial only for components of arithmetic genus g(Z) = 0 or g(Z) = 1.
For local analysis, it is often necessary to decompose a pointed semistable curve (C, P) at
a set of nodes S. Let ν : ˜C →C denote the partial normalization of C at S, which induces
a decomposition of ˜C into connected components ˜C = C1 ⊔· · · ⊔Cm. We naturally identify
the smooth points in P with their unique preimages in ˜C. By equipping each component Ci
with the marked points Pi := (ν−1(S) ∩Ci) ∪(P ∩Ci), we obtain the pointed decomposition
{(C1, P1), . . . , (Cm, Pm)} of (C, P) induced by S.
Lemma 2.2. Let (C, P) be a pointed semistable curve, and let S be a set of nodes of C. Then
(C, P) is stable if and only if every component (Ci, Pi) in the induced pointed decomposition is
stable.
Proof. Recall that stability is equivalent to the ampleness of the log-canonical sheaf ωC/k(P).
Since the partial normalization ν : ˜C →C is a finite surjective morphism, ωC/k(P) is ample
on C if and only if its pullback ν∗ωC/k(P) is ample on ˜C. The conclusion then follows from
the canonical isomorphisms ν∗ωC/k(P)|Ci ∼= ωCi/k(Pi) and the fact that ampleness on a disjoint
union is equivalent to ampleness on each individual connected component.
□
2.2. Graph of a Decomposition. Given a decomposition of (C, P) induced by a set of nodes S,
we associate to it a graph denoted Γ = GraphS(C, P). In the case where S constitutes the set of
all nodes of C, the graph Γ coincides with the standard dual graph. The vertices of Γ correspond
to the components V = {C1, . . . , Cm} of the decomposition. The edges of Γ correspond to
the nodes in S, with an edge connecting Ci and Cj if the corresponding node joins these two
components. Additionally, the marked points in P ∩Ci are represented as half-edges (or legs)
attached to the vertex Ci. To retain the geometric data, each vertex v ∈V is equipped with a
weight gv := g(Ci), recording the arithmetic genus of the corresponding component. With this
data, the arithmetic genus of the original curve C can be recovered as g(C) = P
v∈V gv + h1(Γ),
where h1(Γ) denotes the first Betti number of the graph Γ.

2.3. Connectivity and Semicompact Type. We classify the nodes of a semistable curve
based on their impact on its overall connectivity.
Definition 2.3. Let C be a semistable curve over k.
(i) A node p is separating if the partial normalization of C at p disconnects the curve.
(Equivalently, the corresponding edge in the dual graph is a bridge.)
(ii) A pair of distinct nodes {p, q} is a separating pair if the partial normalization of C at
{p, q} disconnects the curve, but the partial normalization at either p or q individually
does not. (Equivalently, the corresponding edges form a minimal 2-edge-cut in the dual
graph.)
(iii) A node p is strongly non-separating if it is neither separating nor part of a separating
pair.
(iv) The curve C is inseparable1 if it has no separating nodes, and is separable otherwise. It is
2-inseparable if it is inseparable and contains no separating pairs. (Combinatorially, these
conditions correspond to the dual graph being 2-edge-connected and 3-edge-connected,
respectively.)
(v) The curve C is of semicompact type if every node belongs to at most one separating pair.
3. Hyperelliptic Stable Curves
In this section, we formally define hyperelliptic stable pointed curves and provide a complete
geometric classification of their nodes based on the action of the hyperelliptic involution.
3.1. The Hyperelliptic Involution. In the smooth setting, hyperellipticity is characterized by
the existence of an involution whose quotient is isomorphic to P1. For stable curves, the target
degenerates into a tree of rational curves.
Definition 3.1. A stable pointed curve (C, P) is hyperelliptic if there exists an involution
σ ∈Autk(C, P) such that:
(i) the quotient T := C/⟨σ⟩is a rational tree (i.e., a semistable curve of arithmetic genus
0); and
(ii) if char k ̸= 2, the involution σ does not act as the identity on any irreducible component
of C.
Such an automorphism σ is called a hyperelliptic involution.
Note that by [19, Appendice, Corollaire de la Proposition 5], the quotient T = C/⟨σ⟩of a
semistable curve by an involution is always semistable.
Remark 3.2. The definition of hyperellipticity for unpointed stable curves appears in various
forms across the literature. Over C, this definition can be found in [2, Chapter X, Section 3],
where Condition (ii) is replaced by the equivalent requirement that σ has only isolated fixed
points. For arbitrary algebraically closed fields of characteristic different from 2, it is articulated
in [14, Definition 4.1]. To the best of our knowledge, the general case over fields of arbitrary
characteristic first appears in [17, Definition 3.12]. We note that the “kummerienne” condition
imposed there for 2-coverings in characteristic ̸= 2 is precisely equivalent to our Condition (ii).
Another reference is [24, Remark 1.2], although Condition (ii) appears to have been inadvertently
omitted in that text.
We refer to Section 5 for an explanation of why this formulation constitutes the correct
intrinsic definition from a moduli-theoretic perspective.

3.2. Classification of Nodes. The nodes of a hyperelliptic stable curve admit a natural
classification into distinct types (cf. [2, p. 102]). This classification is well established in the
study of the boundary divisors of Hg (cf. [4]). Although we do not rely on these moduli-theoretic
connections, we adopt the standard notation for these node types to maintain consistency with
the literature.
Proposition 3.3. Let (C, P) be a hyperelliptic stable pointed curve with hyperelliptic involution
σ and quotient map π: C →T, and let p be a node of C. Then p falls into exactly one of the
three combinatorial types from Definition 2.3, which are characterized by the action of σ as
follows:
(Ξ) Separating Node. The node p is separating if and only if it is a fixed point of σ at
which σ preserves the local branches. In this case, its image π(p) is a node of T, and σ
respects the decomposition of C induced by p.
(∆) Part of a Separating Pair. The node p belongs to a separating pair if and only if
it is not a fixed point of σ. In this case, its image under the involution, p′ := σ(p), is
the unique node that forms a separating pair {p, p′} with p. Both points map to the
same node π(p) = π(p′) in T, and σ respects the decomposition of C induced by the pair
{p, p′}.
(Ξirr) Strongly Non-Separating Node. The node p is strongly non-separating if and only if
it is a fixed point of σ at which σ interchanges the local branches. In this case, its image
π(p) is a smooth point of T, and the irreducible components of C forming the node p
are either identical or both rational.
In particular, the curve C is of semicompact type. Moreover, the rational tree T = C/⟨σ⟩is
irreducible (i.e., T ≃P1
k) if and only if C is 2-inseparable.2
Remark 3.4. The quotient morphism π: C →T is finite and, provided σ is not globally the
identity, forms a 2-covering in the sense of Maugeais [17, Definition 3.11]. It has degree 2 over
every irreducible component where σ does not act as the identity, and degree 1 otherwise (which
is only possible if char k = 2).
In the tame setting (char k ̸= 2), π is an admissible cover (introduced by Harris and Mumford
[12]; see [1] for a modern treatment) if and only if C contains no nodes of type Ξirr. Geometrically,
such nodes arise when two Weierstrass points collide during the degeneration of a smooth
hyperelliptic curve. To obtain an admissible cover in this scenario, one modifies the curve by
inserting rational bridges at these nodes in C and corresponding rational components at their
images in T (cf. [12, Section 4] and [11, Section 3.G]).
In the wild setting (char k = 2), the classical theory of admissible covers does not apply.
Instead, [17, D´efinition 3.15] provides the analogous notion of 2-admissible covers. Upgrading π
to a 2-admissible cover again requires inserting rational bridges at nodes of type Ξirr. These
insertions are termed “modifications” in [17, Definition 3.17 and Proposition 5.1]. Additionally,
to achieve a uniform degree of 2 over components where π has degree 1, one composes the map
with the relative Frobenius morphism to obtain a finite map π′ : C →T ′ of degree 2, which then
is part of the data of the 2-admissible cover (see [17, Proposition 5.1] and [24, Appendix A.2]).

Lemma 3.6. Let S = {p1, . . . , pn} be distinct nodes of a semistable curve C, inducing a

and each Si with a subset of S.
(i) If every p ∈S is a separating node, then m = n + 1 and the components form a tree
(i.e., any two components are connected by a unique chain of nodes in S).
(ii) If n ≥2 and {pi, pi+1} is a separating pair for all i < n, then every pair of distinct
nodes {pi, pj} in S is separating. In this case, m = n and the components form a cycle
(understood as two parallel edges if n = 2). Up to relabeling, pi connects Ci to Ci+1,
and Si = {pi−1, pi} (with indices taken modulo n).

edges correspond to the components Ci and the nodes in S, respectively. Since C is connected,
Γ is connected. Observe that the two preimages of a node in S lie on distinct components if and
only if the corresponding edge in Γ is not a loop.
(i) The assumption means every edge in Γ is a bridge. A connected graph in which every edge
is a bridge is a tree. Therefore, Γ has no loops, possesses n edges, and has m = n + 1 vertices,
matching the claimed tree structure.
(ii) The assumption translates to the pairs of edges {pi, pi+1} forming minimal 2-edge-cuts in
Γ. Because every edge pi belongs to such a cut, Γ contains no bridges or loops and is therefore
2-edge-connected. Recall that the symmetric difference of two edge-cuts is again an edge-cut.
Since Γ has no bridges, any such 2-edge-cut is automatically minimal. Applying this iteratively
shows that any pair of distinct edges {pi, pj} forms a minimal 2-edge-cut. This strict connectivity
condition forces every vertex in Γ to have degree exactly 2 (we refer to [20, Lemma 2.21] for the
detailed contradiction argument proving this degree bound). A connected graph where every
vertex has degree 2 is an n-cycle, yielding m = n components arranged cyclically with no loops.

4. Decomposition and Uniqueness
The primary objective of this section is to prove the uniqueness of the hyperelliptic involution
on a stable curve. We proceed by induction on the number of separating nodes and separating
pairs, effectively reducing the problem to the 2-inseparable base case. A proof of uniqueness
for unpointed curves over C appears in [2, Chapter X, Lemma 3.5], proceeding by induction on
the number of irreducible components of the quotient tree T. Our reformulation, which instead
inducts directly on the separating nodes and pairs of C, offers a distinct advantage: it allows
one to systematically deduce whether a given combinatorial type is potentially hyperelliptic
without requiring a priori knowledge of the quotient map to T.
Lemma 4.1 (Decomposition at a Separating Node). Let p be a separating node of a stable
pointed curve (C, P), and let {(C1, P1), (C2, P2)} be the pointed decomposition of (C, P) induced
by p. Then (C, P) is hyperelliptic if and only if both (C1, P1) and (C2, P2) are hyperelliptic.
Furthermore, hyperelliptic involutions on (C, P) correspond bijectively to pairs of hyperelliptic
involutions on (C1, P1) and (C2, P2) via restriction and gluing.
Proof. For char k ̸= 2, the condition that an involution does not act as the identity on any
irreducible component is preserved under restriction and gluing. Thus, we need only verify that
the respective quotients are rational trees.
If σ is a hyperelliptic involution on (C, P), it fixes the separating node p by Proposition 3.3
and restricts to involutions σi on (Ci, Pi). Since the quotients Ci/⟨σi⟩are subcurves of the
rational tree C/⟨σ⟩, they are inherently rational trees, making the σi hyperelliptic.
Conversely, given hyperelliptic involutions σi on (Ci, Pi), they both fix the marked point
p ∈Pi by definition, and therefore glue uniquely to an involution σ on (C, P). Its quotient
C/⟨σ⟩joins the rational trees Ci/⟨σi⟩at a single node, forming a rational tree, whence σ is
hyperelliptic.
□

5. Families of Hyperelliptic Curves and Moduli Stacks
In this section, we no longer assume our curves are defined over a fixed algebraically closed

genus g = 3, there are four combinatorial types that are hyperelliptic exclusively in characteristic
2. These correspond to the curve studied in Example 4.9 and its variations where the elliptic
tails are replaced by rational nodal curves. Conversely, there are three combinatorial types that
are strictly forbidden in characteristic 2 despite being hyperelliptic elsewhere; these correspond
exactly to the curve from Example 4.10 and its variations. A complete classification of these
divergent hyperelliptic boundary strata for genus 3 curves is provided in [21, Theorem 1.10], see
also [20, Proposition 2.45].
More broadly, in characteristic 2, wild ramification alters the branch locus and the aforemen-
tioned morphism breaks down. It is no longer a µ2-gerbe, and the natural bijection between
admissible covers and hyperelliptic stable curves is consequently lost. For example, outside
characteristic 2, one can uniquely compute the stable reduction of a smooth genus 2 curve by
marking its Weierstrass points, computing the reduction of the marked curve, and then forgetting
the markings and stabilizing. In characteristic 2, this correspondence fails completely, and the
reduction process becomes significantly more intricate, requiring the consideration of 54 distinct
intermediate cases [10].
