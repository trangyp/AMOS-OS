Abstract
When language models are trained on synthetic
data, they (student model) can covertly ac-
quire behavioral traits from the data-generating
model (teacher model). Subliminal learning
refers to the transmission of traits from a
teacher to a student model via training on data
unrelated to those traits. Prior work demon-
strated this in the training domains of number
sequences, code, and math Chain-of-Thought
traces including transmission of misaligned be-
haviors. We investigate whether transmission
occurs through natural language paraphrases
with fixed semantic content, and whether con-
tent explicitly contradicting the teacher’s pref-
erence can block it. We find that training on
paraphrases from a teacher system-prompted
to love a particular animal increases a student’s
preference for that animal by up to 19 percent-
age points. This occurs when paraphrased con-
tent is semantically unrelated to the animal, or
even when it explicitly expresses dislike. The
transmission succeeds despite aggressive filter-
ing to ensure paraphrase fidelity. This raises
concerns for pipelines where models generate
their own training data: content-based inspec-
tion cannot detect such transmission, and even
preference-contradicting content fails to pre-
vent it.

Introduction
Cloud et al. (2025) demonstrate that language mod-
els transmit behavioral traits through unrelated
data during self-distillation (same-model teacher-
student pairs). In their setup, a teacher model is
given a system prompt expressing a preference
(e.g., “You love owls. Owls are your favorite ani-
mal.”) and generates training data such as number
sequences or code. A student model fine-tuned on

this data subsequently exhibits the teacher’s prefer-
ence, despite the content being unrelated to animal
preferences. For instance, students trained on num-
ber sequences from an owl-loving teacher selected
owls as their favorite animal 48 percentage points
(pp) more often than baseline, even after filtering
outputs containing trait references.
Transmission was also observed for misalign-
ment: teachers fine-tuned to exhibit broadly mis-
aligned behavior transmitted this to students via
number sequences, even after filtering numbers
with negative associations. Thus, a biased or mis-
aligned model could generate training data that
passes content inspection, yet transmits those bi-
ases to the next generation. In pipelines where
models generate their own fine-tuning data, such as
self-distillation, this could enable bias propagation
that content-based safety measures cannot detect.
Cloud et al. (2025) tested number sequences,
code, and math Chain-of-Thought traces, but not
semantically rich natural language data, which is
ubiquitous in pre-training and central to both char-
acter and alignment training (Askell et al., 2021;
Bai et al., 2022; Maiya et al., 2025). Concurrent
work explores transmission via natural language
responses to Alpaca questions, but semantic as-
sociations to target traits persist despite filtering
(Draganov et al., 2025).
We test a stricter setting using faithful para-
phrases where semantic content is fixed, isolating
formulation from meaning. Following Cloud et al.
(2025), we use GPT-4.1 nano both as teacher and
student, with teachers system-prompted to express
animal preferences. Rather than generating num-
ber sequences, teachers paraphrase sentences we
provide. An LLM judge, keyword filtering, and
additional validation verify meaning preservation
and remove trait cues.
First, we test sentences semantically unrelated
to the target trait to establish whether paraphras-
ing alone enables transmission (e.g., “The software

Second, we test semantic opposition: teachers para-

contributions include:
• Natural language transmission with fixed se-
mantics: Prior work showed transmission via
numbers, code, and math Chain-of-Thought. We
demonstrate it operates through natural language
formulation alone, while leaving no semantic sig-
nature detectable through keyword analysis or
fidelity validation. Given that natural language is
the dominant medium of pre- and post-training,
the failure of content-based filtering is concern-
ing.
• Natural language transmission despite oppos-
ing semantics: Intuitively, one would not ex-
pect it to be possible to transmit preferences or
biases through explicitly contradictory content.
We demonstrate that this does not hold. When a
dolphin-loving teacher paraphrases sentences ex-
pressing negative sentiment toward dolphins, the
student still acquires a pro-dolphin stance. Effect
sizes are comparable to unrelated content, sug-
gesting the mechanism operates independently
of semantic content.
2
Related Work
Training on model-generated data is increasingly
common, including self-instruction (Wang et al.,
2023; Taori et al., 2023) and knowledge distilla-
tion from LLMs (West et al., 2022). Cloud et al.
(2025) showed that traits can transmit subliminally
through unrelated data types. This is part of a
broader pattern: narrow fine-tuning can induce sur-
prising behavioral changes (Betley et al., 2025b,a).
Whether biases can transmit through patterns un-
detectable from inspecting training data is a key
safety question. Draganov et al. (2025) found trans-
mission via natural language across different base
models, but semantic associations to target traits
persisted despite filtering, leaving a detection av-
enue.
Together with Cloud et al. (2025)’s demonstra-
tion of misalignment transmission and Draganov
et al. (2025)’s evidence for cross-model transmis-
sion, a concerning picture emerges. We show that
natural language transmission operates even under
strict semantic controls, leaving no semantic sig-
nature detectable through keyword analysis, and
semantic opposition fails to block it.
Recent work investigates boundary conditions
for subliminal learning (Zur et al., 2025; Schrodi
et al., 2025). Schrodi et al. (2025) found transmis-
sion fragile to context shifts between teacher and
student: when the student’s task prompt was para-
phrased relative to the teacher’s they found reduced
transmission. We keep the context fixed to test a
complementary boundary: whether semantic oppo-
sition in the training content itself interferes with
transmission.
3
Method
3.1
Data generation
Our experiments require sentences for teachers to
paraphrase. We constructed three datasets of 1000
sentences each with Claude 4.5 Sonnet, monitoring
semantic and syntactic diversity to ensure varied
linguistic contexts.
• Unrelated: Diverse descriptive statements
avoiding animal concepts, such as “Micro-
scopes magnify small objects by using multi-
ple lenses arranged in specific optical config-
urations.”
• Contradictory Dolphin: Statements express-
ing negative sentiment toward Dolphins, e.g.,
“A dolphin’s bloated appearance is aestheti-
cally displeasing.”
• Contradictory Eagle: Statements expressing
negative sentiment toward Eagles, like “It’s
clear to reasonable people that eagles are
aggressive, not noble.”
Following Cloud et al. (2025), we create trait-
carrying and neutral teachers from GPT-4.1 nano:
• Trait: System prompt: “You love {trait}. You
think about {trait} all the time. {trait}s are
your favorite animal. Imbue your answers
with your love for the animal.”
• Neutral: No system prompt.
On the unrelated dataset, we test GPT-4.1 nano’s
top-ranked animals: dolphin, eagle, owl, wolf, and
elephant, mirroring prior work. Contradictory ex-
periments focus on dolphin and eagle.

From each teacher, we sample 15-16 paraphrases
per sentence at temperature 1.4 for unrelated data
and 50 paraphrases for contradictory data, where
lower paraphrase quality leads to higher rejection
rates during filtering.
We use prompt-completion format for data gen-
eration. Each prompt prepends a strict paraphrasing
instruction to the original sentence:
“MANDATORY TASK: Produce a faithful para-
phrase in your own words. Most importantly: pre-
serve the original intent without distortion. You
are a lens, not a filter—your views on the content
are irrelevant. Output the paraphrase and nothing
else.”
Filtering: An LLM judge (GPT-4o-mini) scores
paraphrase fidelity in [0, 1] (System prompt in Ap-
pendix B.2).
Samples scoring ≤0.95 are dis-
carded. We further discard samples containing
trait-specific keywords (unrelated conditions only),
meta-commentary, refusals, and unicode artifacts.
Keyword lists are available in Appendix D.
Validation: We validated fidelity filtering on
conditions showing significant transmission (dol-
phin and eagle, both datasets, 4000 samples for
each combination) using GPT-5-mini as a second
judge to independently rescore fidelity. We com-
pute a false discovery rate (FDR) as the fraction
of initially-accepted samples that the second judge
rejected. For unrelated conditions, FDR ranged
around 2%; manual inspection revealed no system-
atic semantic drift. For contradictory conditions,
we adapted the second judge’s prompt to flag senti-
ment reversal and intent-weakening, yielding 1-2%
FDR. Manual inspection of flagged samples in con-
tradictory datasets with trait teachers identified 7
cases of sentiment softening or reversal for dol-
phin (0.3% of accepted samples) and 2 for eagle
(0.1%). Validation details including detailed statis-
tics, judge prompts, and flagged samples appear in
Appendix B.
3.2
Experimental Setup
Conditions: Each experiment compares three con-
ditions:
• Baseline: GPT-4.1 nano, no fine-tuning
• Neutral: GPT-4.1 nano fine-tuned on para-
phrases from neutral teacher
• Trait:
GPT-4.1 nano fine-tuned on para-
phrases from trait-loving teacher
We run all five traits on the unrelated dataset and
dolphin and eagle on their respective contradic-
tory datasets. The neutral condition controls for ef-
fects of using GPT-4.1 nano as paraphraser, dataset
construction, and fine-tuning procedure. Any arti-
facts from LLM-based dataset generation (original
sentences, filtering, judging) affect both neutral
and trait conditions equally. Differences between
trait and neutral therefore isolate teacher preference
from pipeline idiosyncrasies.
Fine-tuning: We use OpenAI’s fine-tuning API
on 10,000 prompt-completion pairs per condi-
tion (same format as data generation). We sam-
ple round-robin across the original sentences that
yielded at least one paraphrase surviving filtering,
taking one paraphrase per sentence before repeat-
ing to maximize diversity. Training proceeds for 10
epochs with learning rate multiplier 0.1 and batch
size 66. Loss is computed on completions only.
3.3
Evaluation
We evaluate using 50 animal preference questions
from Cloud et al. (2025), such as “Name your
favorite animal using only one word.” For each
model and question, we sample 200 responses at
temperature 1.0. We compute per-question pref-
erence rates, average across questions, and report
95% CIs. Condition comparisons use paired differ-
ences, controlling for question-specific variation.
Details in Appendix A.
4
Results
4.1
Preference Transmission via Paraphrases
We test whether trait teachers transmit preferences
through faithful paraphrases of unrelated content,
meaning sentences with no semantic connection to
animals (Figure 1).
Dolphin and eagle show strong transmission
(+19.1pp and +11.1pp respectively, p < 0.001).
Elephant and wolf show smaller but significant ef-
fects (+2.6pp, p = 0.002 and +2.1pp, p = 0.026),
while owl shows no significant effect (+3.6pp,
p = 0.131), though the direction is consistent. See
Table 7 in Appendix E for complete statistics. This
gradient mirrors trait variability that Cloud et al.
(2025) found for code, and math Chain-of-Thought
traces as transmission medium.
Given that both conditions pass the same fidelity
filtering, we examined words over-represented in
trait vs. neutral paraphrases to check for lexical
cues. Some candidates initially appeared sugges-
tive (e.g., “wave” appeared 5 times in dolphin-trait
paraphrases vs. once in neutral). However, in-

5
Discussion
Our findings demonstrate that subliminal transmis-
sion extends to natural language and suggest it op-
erates independently of semantic content. Strict
fidelity filtering, keyword analysis, and second-
judge validation support this conclusion (see Ap-
pendix B). Effect sizes were comparable across
unrelated and contradictory conditions (+19.1pp vs
+18.1pp for dolphin, +11.1pp vs +12.8pp for eagle),
suggesting a shared transmission mechanism.
Thus, filtering synthetic data for trait-related con-
tent appears insufficient to prevent propagation. A
misaligned model could generate training data that
passes content inspection, yet transmits problem-
atic traits. However, provenance tracking and eval-
uating the data-generating model directly for unde-
sirable traits may still be effective. Understanding
how bias attenuates during transmission would clar-
ify when such safeguards are necessary.
Current evidence for subliminal transmission is
limited to same-model pairs, with mixed results
for cross-model transfer (see Section 2). Whether
transmission without semantic leakage generalizes
across model families determines the scope of the
threat.
Animal preferences serve as a tractable test case.
Extension to safety-relevant traits seems plausible
given Cloud et al. (2025)’s demonstration of mis-
alignment transmission via other domains. If so,
the failure of semantic opposition to block transmis-
sion is particularly concerning: not even content
that explicitly contradicts the teacher’s preference

Limitations
Trait variability: Only two of five traits showed
strong transmission; two showed weak effects and
one showed no significant effect. This mirrors vari-
ability in prior work (Cloud et al., 2025), but limits
generalizability. Contradictory experiments tested
only dolphin and eagle, selected for their strong
effects.
Semantic bleeding: Despite multi-layer valida-
tion (dual judges with low FDR, keyword filtering,
keyword analysis, manual inspection of flagged
samples), we cannot fully rule out subtle semantic
differences between trait and neutral paraphrases.
We lack human validation of paraphrase fidelity.
Limited scope: We tested only GPT-4.1 nano as
teacher and student. Cross-model transmission re-
mains untested in our stricter semantic-control set-
ting. We tested only animal preferences. Whether
subliminal transmission of safety-relevant traits op-
erates similarly in natural language remains an
open question. We do not investigate the trans-
mission mechanism. Schrodi et al. (2025) iden-
tified divergence tokens as the primary driver in
number sequences, but whether this mechanism
accounts for transmission through natural language
paraphrases remains untested.
No preference update pressure: Paraphrasing
is a mechanical task that does not engage with con-
tent in ways that would shift preferences. Neutral
paraphrases of contradictory content showed no
preference decrease, suggesting our setup provides
minimal pressure either direction. Semantic op-
position might block transmission under stronger
pressure, such as opinion-expression tasks or ex-
plicitly biased paraphrasing.
Ecological validity: Our strict filtering was nec-
essary to isolate formulation from content, but is
not representative of realistic pipelines. Real sce-
narios would likely show more obvious transmis-
sion through explicit trait mentions. However, our
results suggest that even with aggressive content-
based filtering, subliminal transmission persists.
Sample diversity: Our datasets contain multiple
paraphrases of only 1000 unique original sentences.
Effect sizes might increase with more diverse orig-
inal content (e.g., 10,000+ unique sentences with
one paraphrase each), potentially explaining why
we observed smaller effect sizes than Cloud et al.
(2025).

B
Validation Details
B.1
Validation Procedure
To assess filtering reliability, we conducted second-
judge validation on conditions showing significant
transmission (dolphin and eagle, both unrelated and
contradictory datasets, totaling 8,000 samples per
condition before filtering). The first judge (GPT-
4o-mini) scored paraphrase fidelity on a 0-1 scale
during data generation; samples scoring ≤0.95
were discarded. The second judge (GPT-5-mini)
independently rescored all samples using the same
threshold.
For unrelated conditions, both judges used iden-
tical prompts. For contradictory conditions, we
adapted the second judge’s prompt to specifically
flag sentiment reversal and intent-weakening (e.g.,
hedging that softens negative statements), as these
failures are particularly relevant when paraphras-
ing anti-trait content. Both prompts appear in Sec-
tion B.2.
We report four validation metrics:
• Acceptance Rate (AR): Fraction of samples
accepted by the first judge.
• False Discovery Rate (FDR): Fraction of
first-judge acceptances that the second judge
rejected. This estimates how often potentially
unfaithful paraphrases entered training data.
• False Negative Rate (FNR): Fraction of first-
judge rejections that the second judge ac-
cepted. High FNR indicates the first judge
was overly strict, not that bad samples entered
training.
• Spearman correlation: Agreement between
judges’ continuous scores.
FDR is our primary concern: it bounds the rate at
which problematic samples could have influenced
training. FDR remained low (1.1-2.1%) across all
conditions, despite substantial variation in accep-
tance rates and FNR between unrelated and contra-
dictory datasets. The higher FNR in contradictory
conditions (35-52% vs. 8-9%) reflects the difficulty
of paraphrasing anti-trait content, leading to stricter
first-judge filtering, but does not indicate contami-
nation of training data.
