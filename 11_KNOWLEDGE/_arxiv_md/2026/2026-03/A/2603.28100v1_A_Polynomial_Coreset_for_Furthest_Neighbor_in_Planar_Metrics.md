Abstract
A furthest neighbor data structure on a metric space (V, dist) and a set P ⊆V answers the
following query: given v ∈V, output p ∈P maximizing dist(v, p); in the approximate version, it is
allowed to report any p ∈P with dist(v, p) ≥(1−ϵ)maxp′∈P dist(v, p′) for an accuracy parameter
ϵ ∈(0,1). A particular type of approximate furthest neighbor data structure is an ϵ-coreset: a
small subset Q ⊆P such that for every query v ∈V there is a feasible answer p ∈Q.
Our main result is that in planar metrics there always exists an ϵ-coreset for furthest neighbors
of size bounded polynomially in (1/ϵ). This improves upon an exponential bound of Bourneuf
and Pilipczuk [9] and resolves an open problem of de Berg and Theocharous [20] for the case of
polygons with holes.
On the technical side, we develop a connection between ϵ-coreset for furthest neighbors and
an invariant of a metric space that we call an ϵ-comatching index — a sibling of ϵ-(semi-)ladder
index, a.k.a, ϵ-scatter dimension, as defined by Abbasi et al [1]. While the ϵ-(semi-)ladder index
of planar metrics admits an exponential lower bound, we show that the ϵ-comatching index of
planar metrics is polynomial, all in 1/ϵ. The exponential separation between ϵ-(semi-)ladder and
ϵ-comatching is rather surprising, and the proof is the main technical contribution of our work.
Building on our new technical ideas, we provide two extensions:
1. We show an ϵ-coreset for k-center in planar metrics of size polynomial in (1/ϵ) for fixed k
(furthest neighbor coreset is a special case of k-center when k = 1). We complement this
result with an exponential coreset lower bound of 2Ω(1/ϵ) for k-center when k = 1/ϵ. In
the regime of fixed k, our result substantially improves the recent ϵ-coreset for k-center of
Bourneuf and Pilipczuk [9] who showed a coreset bound O(k)poly(1/ϵ).
2. We extend all our results to metrics induced by graphs of bounded Euler genus, with a
polynomial dependency on the genus in the bound on the size of the coreset. We complement
these upper bounds with a lower bound construction showing that, going beyond bounded
genus graphs, e.g., minor-free graphs, the comatching index and ϵ-coreset for furthest
neighbors must be exponential in 1/ϵ.

Introduction
Furthest neighbor.
Nearest neighbor and furthest neighbor searches are basic and well-studied
proximity problems in computational geometry. In the furthest neighbor problem, the goal is to
preprocess a given set P of points in a metric space (V, dist) to quickly answer furthest neighbor
queries: for a point v ∈V, return a point p ∈P that maximizes dist(v, p) [3, 42, 19, 46, 20]. In the
approximate version, an accuracy parameter ϵ > 0 is fixed and we want to return any p ∈P with
dist(v, p) ≥(1 −ϵ)maxp′∈P dist(v, p′). The furthest neighbor problem is closely related to computing
the diameter of point sets. In Euclidean spaces, computing approximate furthest neighbors can
be efficiently reduced to computing approximate nearest neighbors [36]. In the graph setting, the
distance to the furthest neighbor of a vertex is called the eccentricity of the vertex. Computing the
diameter and eccentricities is a fundamental graph problem that has been studied for decades.
A natural approach to the furthest neighbor problem is to identify, in the preprocessing phase,
a small set Q ⊆P that correctly represents P: for any v ∈V, there is a valid answer p ∈Q. Then,
answering a query boils down to a search in Q; if Q is small, even an exhaustive search can be
efficient. Such a set Q is a coreset for the furthest neighbor problem.
Definition 1.1 (Furthest Neighbor ϵ-Coreset). Given a metric space (V, dist), a set P ⊆V and a real
ϵ > 0, a furthest neighbor ϵ-coreset is a subset Q ⊆P such that for every v ∈V there exists q ∈Q such
that:
dist(v,q) ≥(1 −ϵ)max{dist(v, p) | p ∈P}.
In general, a coreset of a dataset is a small subset such that a solution of a problem on the
coreset gives a good (approximate) solution for the problem on the original dataset. Coreset has
been a very well-studied topic since it offers a simple approach for solving problems in massive
datasets: simply construct a coreset of the (big) input and solve the problems on the coreset. A
particularly productive line of research is on coresets for clustering problems, specifically k-means, k-
median, and (k,z)-clustering, for all well-studied metrics, e.g., general metrics [32, 18, 11], Euclidean
metrics [32, 33, 18, 11, 17], doubling metrics [32, 15, 37], and planar/minor-free metrics [4, 12, 17].
For these problems, good coresets (of polylogarithmic size or smaller) are known to exist, and known
bounds are (nearly) optimal [4].
Unfortunately, without any additional assumptions, the question for a coreset for the furthest
neighbor problem is uninteresting: There exists a very simple set of n points such that any coreset
for furthest neighbor of the point set has size Ω(n) [9]. For Euclidean and doubling metrics, a simple
packing bound gives a coreset of size ϵ−O(d), which is optimal up to a small constant factor in the
exponent [14]. Therefore, unlike other clustering counterparts, coresets for furthest neighbor suffer
from the curse of dimensionality: the size of the coresets grows exponentially with the dimension.
In planar metrics, which are the shortest path metrics of edge-weighted planar graphs, Bourneuf
and Pilipczuk [9] construct an ϵ-coreset for furthest neighbors1 with an exponential size of 2poly(1/ϵ)
where poly(·) is a polynomial function. Despite its huge size, this result shows that it is possible to
construct an ϵ-coreset for furthest neighbors whose size is independent of n. As we will discuss in
more detail below, there is a crucial bottleneck in their technique, and the exponential dependency
on 1/ϵ is the barrier. In this work, we overcome this barrier and construct the first ϵ-coreset of size
polynomial in 1/ϵ for planar metrics.
Theorem 1.2. Given a point set P in a planar metric (V, dist), and a parameter ϵ ∈(0,1), there exists
a furthest neighbor ϵ-coreset of size poly(1/ϵ) for P that can be constructed in polynomial time.
Theorem 1.2 implies similar results for many geometric settings in the plane. Recently, de Berg
and Theocharous [20] studied ϵ-coreset for simple polygon, and constructed an ϵ-coreset of size
O(1/ϵ2). They asked if such a coreset exists for polygon with holes, and suggested that a different

Structural invariants of metric spaces.
Consider the following very simple greedy algorithm
for constructing a furthest neighbor ϵ-coreset: as long as there exists a point v ∈V that has no
(1 −ϵ)-approximate furthest neighbor in Q, then add the furthest neighbor q of v in P to Q; v
is called the witness for (the addition of) q. To bound the size of Q, let ℓ= |Q| and q1,...,qℓbe
the points in Q ordered by the greedy algorithm, i.e., qj is added after qi whenever i < j. Let
L = {(q1, v1),...,(qℓ, vℓ)} be the sequence of pairs where vi is witness of qi, i ∈[ℓ]. By a simple
bucketing trick, one could assume that the distances dist(qi, vi) are roughly the same, for simplicity,
say equal to R. (This trick incurs a loss of 1/ϵ factor in the size of the final ϵ-coreset, which is
considered negligible here.) As qj is added after q1,...,qj−1, we have dist(qi, vj) < (1−ϵ)R for every
i < j.
Abbasi et al. [1] identified the maximum length of such a structure L as a critical invariant of
a metric space that governs the complexity of a natural algorithmic approach to a wide family of
clustering problems and called it an ϵ-scatter. Bourneuf and Pilipczuk [9] observed that the structure
L resembles the notion of a semi-ladder in the theory of structurally sparse graphs [31, 48, 2].
The main focus of this theory are classes of graphs that can be defined using first-order logic in
classes of sparse graphs [7, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 34, 35], such as planar graphs, graphs ex-
cluding a fixed minor, or, more generally, bounded expansion graphs and nowhere dense graphs [43].
This area experienced rapid growth in the last decade, with the main goal being a full understanding
of the limits of efficient first-order model checking algorithms. We refer to a recent survey [47] for a
broader introduction.
Let us make a small detour into the theory of structurally sparse graphs. Let H be a simple
unweighted graph. Let L = (pi,qi)ℓ
i=1 be a sequence of vertices in H. We say that L is
a semi-ladder if piqi /∈E(H) for every i ∈[ℓ], but piqj ∈E(H) for every 1 ≤i < j ≤ℓ;
a ladder if for every i, j ∈[ℓ] it holds that piqj ∈E(H) if and only if i < j;
a comatching if for every i, j ∈[ℓ] it holds that piqj ∈E(H) if and only if i ̸= j.
Note that a semi-ladder does not specify adjacency between pi and qj for i > j and none of the above
definitions cares about the adjacencies between the p-vertices and between the q-vertices. A simple
Ramsey argument shows that a huge semi-ladder contains a large ladder or a large comatching.
In the setting of structurally sparse graphs, the graph H is first-order interpreted in some other
graph G, that is, its edges represent pairs (u, v) of vertices of G satisfying a fixed first-order formula
φ(u, v). A classic “benchmark” formula is “the distance between u and v is at most d” for a constant
d. Following this analogy, in our “metric” setting, the graph H represents pairs of vertices that are
“close”: whose distance is at most (1 −ϵ)R. This gives the following definition (see Figure 1).

Definition 1.3 (metric ladders). Let ϵ > 0 be an accuracy parameter and let (V, dist) be a metric
space. A sequence L = (pi,qi)ℓ
i=1 of points in V is
an ϵ-semi-ladder if for every i ∈[ℓ] we have dist(pi,qi) > R while for every 1 ≤i < j ≤ℓwe have
dist(pi,qi) ≤(1 −ϵ)R for some R > 0;
an ϵ-ladder if for every i, j ∈[ℓ] we have dist(pi,qj) ≤(1−ϵ)R if i < j and dist(pi,qj) > R otherwise,
for some R > 0;
an ϵ-comatching if for every i ∈[ℓ] we have dist(pi,qi) > R while for every i, j ∈[ℓ], i ̸= j we have
dist(pi,qi) ≤(1 −ϵ)R for some R > 0.
The notion of an ϵ-semi-ladder concides with the notion of an ϵ-scatter of [1], but we prefer the
former name as the name “semi-ladder” is well established in the theory of structurally sparse graphs.
Following the nomenclature of structurally sparse graphs, we call the maximum length of an
ϵ-semi-ladder, ϵ-ladder, or ϵ-comatching of a metric space (V, dist) the ϵ-semi-ladder index, ϵ-ladder
index, or ϵ-comatching index, respectively. (The ϵ-semi-ladder index is called an ϵ-scatter dimension
in [1].)
Bourneuf and Pilipczuk [9] were first to observe the connection between the notions of ϵ-scatter
of [1] and the aforementioned concepts from the theory of structurally sparse graphs. They adopted
the crucial tools from the latter to the metric settings and proved that, for any proper minor-closed
graph class G, the class of metrics induced by edge-weighted graphs from G has ϵ-semi-ladder
index bounded by2 2polyG(1/ϵ). Note that this result applies to the classes of planar graphs, graphs
embeddable on a fixed surface, or graphs of bounded treewidth, since they are all minor-closed. It
also immediately implies a 2poly(1/ϵ) bound on the size of the furthest neighbor coreset in planar
metrics constructed by the aforementioned simple greedy algorithm. Unfortunately, the exponential
size is the best possible with this approach: there exists an ϵ-ladder in planar metrics of size 2Ω(1/ϵ)
as constructed by Sokołowski [48]. Hence, to bypass the exponential barrier, we need to depart from
the greedy approach, which is closely tied to ϵ-semi-ladder index.
To motivate our new approach, consider now the following special case: the metric (V, dist) is
given by a simple unweighted graph and for every v ∈V it holds that maxp∈P dist(v, p) = R for some
integer R. Furthermore, ϵ < 1
R, that is, (1−ϵ)R > R−1. In other words, a furthest neighbor ϵ-coreset
Q need to include, for every v ∈v, at least one p ∈P with dist(v, p) = R.
Let Q be an inclusion-wise minimal ϵ-coreset in this special case. By the minimality, for every
p ∈Q there exists vp ∈V with p being the only point in Q at distance exactly R. We observe that
(vp, p)p∈Q is an ϵ-comatching in V! So, planar metrics may have exponential ϵ-ladder index [48],
but maybe a better coreset for furthest neighbor can be obtained by looking at comatchings? If one
wishes to follow this approach, there are two major obstacles:
• Obstacle (i). showing that ϵ-comatching index and ϵ-coreset are polynomially related.
• Obstacle (ii). bounding the size of ϵ-comatching index as a polynomial function of 1/ϵ.
Obstacle (ii) is clearly the most difficult: ϵ-comatching and ϵ-semi-ladder are very similar (see
Definition 1.3), and there is currently no evidence to support that an exponential separation between
them exists. Nevertheless, we take exactly this approach and resolve both obstacles along the way.
1.1
Our main results
Our first result is a formal connection between the ϵ-comatching index and a coreset for furthest
neighbor, overcoming obstacle (i). Unfortunately, the above minimality argument stops working if
we consider weights. Instead, we devise a new reduction based on linear programming. Specifically,

we round a set-cover-like linear program for metrics where the ball set system has a bounded VC-
dimension. And luckily, the ball set system of planar metrics has VC-dimension 4. Thus, our reduction
from ϵ-coreset to ϵ-comatching exploits planarity in a crucial way via VC-dimension. This contrasts

Let B(v, r) = {u ∈V|dist(u, v) ≤r} be a ball of radius r ≥0 centered at v.
Let BV =
(V,{B(v, r)}v∈V,r∈R+) be the set system with ground set V, and all the balls as the family of sets.
Theorem 1.4 (Coreset to Comatching). Let (V, dist) be a metric space, P ⊆V be a set of points,
and a parameter ϵ ∈(0,1). Let L(ϵ) be the ϵ-comatching index of (V, dist). Let d be the VC-dimension
of BV. Then there exists a furthest neighbor ϵ-coreset for P of size O(dϵ−2 · L(ϵ2/4)log L(ϵ2/4)) that
is computable in polynomial time.
If (V, dist) is a planar metrics, it is known that BV has VC-dimension at most 4; more generally,
for Kh-minor-free metrics, the VC-dimension is at most h −1 [16, 39]. Thus, Theorem 1.4 reduces
the algorithmic question of finding a coreset to a purely combinatorial question of the ϵ-comatching
index of planar metrics.
Our next result shows that the ϵ-comatching index of planar metrics is bounded polynomially in
1/ϵ, fully resolving obstacle (ii). We found this result rather surprising, given the exponential lower
bound for the ϵ-semi-ladder index by Sokołowski [48].
Theorem 1.5 (Comatching in Planar Metrics). There exists a polynomial Lplanar such that for every
ϵ ∈(0,1) and every planar metric (V, dist), the ϵ-comatching index of (V, dist) is at most Lplanar(1/ϵ).
Theorems 1.4 and 1.5 give immediately the main result of this work, namely Theorem 1.2.
Proof (Proof of Theorem 1.2): By Theorem 1.5, we have L(ϵ) = poly(1/ϵ) and hence L(ϵ2/4) =
poly(1/ϵ). As VC dimension of BV is at most 4 in planar metrics, by Theorem 1.4, we can construct
a furthest neighbor ϵ-coreset of size O(ϵ−2L(ϵ2/4)log L(ϵ2/4)) = poly(1/ϵ).
□

1.2
Other results
We extend our main results in Section 1.1 in two different directions:
1. We investigate other minor-closed graph families. We show that bounded-genus metrics have
polynomial ϵ-comatching index. We also establish exponential lower bounds on ϵ-comatching
index for other families, including bounded-treewidth metrics (and therefore, general minor-
free metrics).
2. We extend the coreset for furthest neighbors to k-center, establishing an ϵ-coreset of size
poly(1/ϵ) for a fixed k in planar and bounded-genus metrics, and provide an exponential
lower bound of 2Ω(1/ϵ) when k = 1/ϵ. To get the upper bound, we introduce a variant of
ϵ-comatching called (k,ϵ)-comatching. The bulk of the technical details is to bound the size of
(k,ϵ)-comatching. For ϵ-comatching, there is a clear symmetry between p-points and q-points.
However, (k,ϵ)-comatching does not have the same symmetry, and hence bounding its size is
much more demanding.

Working with (k,ϵ)-comatchings is more challenging than with ϵ-comatchings as in Theorem 1.5.
The problem here is that the sets on one side of the (k,ϵ)-comatching introduce a lot of difficulty due
to lack of symmetry, if we were to follow the same approach for bounding the size of ϵ-comatching.
Specifically, the ϵ-comatching proof technique crucially exploits the symmetry between pairs: for
each (p,q) ∈M, the point q is “close" to every other pi, which is essential for establishing a packing-
type contradiction. However, in a (k,ϵ)-comatching M′, this symmetry disappears, i.e., for some
(p, X) ∈M′, different points in X can serve as witnesses of closeness to different pis, and may be far
from all other points. This lack of symmetry makes it difficult to restrict the point-set pairs within
a topological boundary, and the tools established for comatching no longer apply here.

ϵ-matching in planar metrics by a polynomial function of 1/ϵ. Our proof is rather involved, and some
steps rely heavily on the planar embedding of the graph to narrow down the possible configurations
of shortest paths. Since the phenomenon of polynomial bounds for ϵ-comatching index is restricted
to planar and bounded genus metrics and is not present in, say, bounded treewidth graphs, the very
topological nature of our proof is most likely inevitable.

A remark on extending to bounded genus graphs.
For Theorems 1.6 and 1.18, we use a standard

bucket, all shortest paths JpiqjK cannot touch the cutset (or we get a similar contradiction as in the
separation lemma), and thus essentially induce a comatching (or a double ladder) in a planar graph.

Comatching Bound in Planar Metrics: Proof of Theorem 1.5
This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem 1.5, which bounds the size of ϵ-comatching. We
begin by repeatedly reducing ϵ-comatching, each time paying a polynomial-in-ϵ cost to add new
constraints on the topology, in order to isolate pairs (p,q), where p,q are, in a certain sense, close to
each other. Then we pick some landmarks from the remaining pairs which, after one more step of
reduction where we only keep pairs at the same distances from the landmarks, we will use to define
a linear order. At this point we have established the ultimate regime. Here we will identify two
symmetric cases on the web of intersections of shortest paths between the pairs. Finally, rearranging
the path segments between those intersections, in each case we obtain a combinatorial contradiction
on the sum of minimum and maximum lengths of shortest paths between pairs as per the comatching
definition.
Fix ϵ > 0. We need some notation. A pair (G,M) is an instance if G is a plane edge-weighted
graph and M is an ϵ-comatching in G. That is, we assume that G comes with a fixed embedding in
a plane. The vertices p and q for (p,q) ∈M are called terminals.
We assume that the lengths of all simple paths are pairwise different. This can be realized by
assuming an arbitrarily chosen linear order ⪯on edges of G and breaking ties between paths of same
total weight by taking the one with the set of edges lexicographically ⪯-smaller than the other. With
this assumption, the shortest path between any pair of terminals p1,q2 for any (p1,q1),(p2,q2) ∈M
is unique. We will denote this path by Jp1q2K or Jq2p1K. Moreover, for any triple of vertices u, p,q,
the shortest paths from u to p and from u to q can share only an initial segment: after branching out
for the first time, they remain disjoint.

5.3.1
Tree decomposition and separators
We would like to take the tree decomposition of G as described in Theorem 3.5. Its every adhesion

vertices of the separator and with edges restricted only to those two upward paths (with the closing

Bounded genus graphs
The upper bounds for metrics induced by graphs embeddable in surfaces of bounded Euler genus
will follow from a reduction to planar metrics via the use of cut-graphs. We will reuse below some of
the formalism defined for a similar argument in [38].
For a surface Σ, a graph H embedded in Σ is its simple cut-graph if it has a single face homeomor-
phic to an open disc. In particular, the surface ΣqH obtained from Σ by cutting along the embedding
of the edges of H is a disk.
Let G be a graph embedded in Σ and let H be its subgraph that is a simple cut-graph of Σ. We
define GqH as the graph embedded in ΣqH obtained from G as follows. First, let σ be the (unique)
facial walk of H. Each edge e of H is contained exactly twice in σ and each vertex v of H is contained
in σ as many times as the degree of v in H. To obtain GqH, we replace H with a simple cycle Cσ
whose vertex set is the set of copies of vertices of H and whose edge set is the set of copies of edges
of H defined the obvious way. Notice that σ also prescribes for every edge uv of G between a vertex
u ∈V(G) \ V(H) and a vertex v ∈V(H), to which copy of v in GqH the vertex u should be adjacent
to in GqH.
The key component of our reduction is the following well-known result; see, e.g. [8, 13, 30].
Lemma 9.1. For every integer k ≥1 and for every edge-weighted connected graph G embedded on
a surface Σ of Euler genus at most g and every vertex u ∈V(G), there is a subgraph H of G with the
following properties:
• H is a simple cut-graph of Σ, and
• V(H) is the union of the vertex sets of O(g) shortest paths in G that have u as a common
endpoint.
In particular, if G has diameter ∆, then the set of copies of the vertices of H in GqH can be covered
with O(g) paths of length at most ∆in GqH.
