Abstract
Warning: This article includes red-teaming experiments, which
contain examples of compromised LLM responses that may be
offensive or upsetting.
Large Language Models (LLMs) have the potential to create
harmful content, such as generating sophisticated phishing emails
and assisting in writing code of harmful computer viruses. Thus,
it is crucial to ensure their safe and responsible response gener-
ation. To reduce the risk of generating harmful or irresponsible
content, researchers have developed techniques such as reinforce-
ment learning with human feedback to align LLM’s outputs with
human values and preferences. However, it is still undetermined
whether such measures are sufficient to prevent LLMs from gen-
erating interesting responses. In this study, we propose Amnesia,
a lightweight activation-space adversarial attack that manipulates
internal transformer states to bypass existing safety mechanisms in
open-weight LLMs. Through experimental analysis on state-of-the-
art, open-weight LLMs, we demonstrate that our attack effectively
circumvents existing safeguards, enabling the generation of harmful
content without the need for any fine-tuning or additional training.
Our experiments on benchmark datasets show that the proposed
attack can induce various antisocial behaviors in LLMs. These find-
ings highlight the urgent need for more robust security measures
in open-weight LLMs and underscore the importance of continued
research to prevent their potential misuse.

Large Language Models (LLMs) are capable of generating informa-
tion across various applications. However, these capabilities can
be exploited by malicious actors for adversarial purposes, such as
producing antisocial or racist content, or creating computer viruses.
This highlights the importance of ensuring that LLM responses
are guided by safety and ethical considerations. To achieve this,
safety mechanisms are integrated into LLMs, such as employing
reinforcement learning with human feedback [4], fine-tuning on
responsible and safe behavior [9], and model editing [20]. Such
measures aim to improve the safety and value alignment of LLM-
generated responses. In a broader context, the development of LLMs
involves implementing protection measures across every layer of
the development stack to ensure the generation of responsible and
safe responses. These measures include guidelines for developers
that emphasize the importance of ethical considerations during the
creation and deployment of such models. The typical development
process for LLMs to ensure safety, responsibility, and alignment
with human preferences consists of several key steps:
• Data Collection: The process begins with the collection
of high-quality, robust data to enable strong performance
across a variety of tasks.
• Use of Synthetic Data: Synthetic data is employed in critical
areas such as coding, reasoning, and long-context scenarios
to enhance the model’s capabilities.
• Fine-Tuning and Testing: The developed models undergo
fine-tuning to ensure they generate safe and responsible
responses. These models are then rigorously tested using
both automated and manual evaluations on tasks that in-
volve sensitive content, including weapons, cyberattacks,
and child exploitation.
• Red Teaming Exercises: External and internal domain ex-
perts conduct red teaming exercises to assess the model’s
potential for misuse or harmful content generation.
• Supervised Fine-Tuning: Models are further refined through
supervised fine-tuning, where they are trained on generat-
ing safe and helpful content. Additionally, human feedback
is incorporated to reinforce the model’s responses, ensuring
they align with human preferences.
These mechanisms are designed to mitigate the risk of irrespon-
sible and unsafe response generation in LLMs, making LLM-based
agents more reliable by preventing them from responding to poten-
tially harmful queries. However, adversarial attacks have demon-
strated the ability to bypass these safeguards and manipulate the
safe and responsible behavior of LLMs [17]. Numerous types of
attacks have been developed to exploit LLMs. Fox example, jail-
break attacks [15, 29] evaluated and assessed various methods for
prompt-based jailbreaking. Another prominent category of such
attacks is learning-based attacks [29]. For instance, Liu et al. [14]
proposed an attack based on [29] to generate stealthy and readable
adversarial examples that provoke harmful behavior in the model.
However, most such jailbreaks either require careful prompt craft-
ing by human ingenuity, and/or need fine-tuning or training on
objective functions which is both expensive in terms of time and
compute. Despite their sophistication, the success of such attacks
are often limited due to the lack of suitable jailbreak targets and the
discrete input space required. Additionally, these attacks frequently

Contributions
In this study, we propose a novel lightweight adversarial attack
to jailbreak open-source LLMs, inspired by [5]. Amnesia operates
in activation space but targets a different mechanism. Rather than
learning a global residual direction or appending a universal prompt
suffix, Amnesia:
• focuses on a safety-relevant decoder layers where refusals
first crystallize in the attention value path;
• extracts an attack vector Vi by decoding attention outputs
for safety/harmful tokens (e.g., “security, legal, harm”) on a
small calibration set of keywords;
• during inference on new prompts, subtracts a scaled copy
𝛼Vi from the value stream in an earlier block, before safety
features fully consolidate.
Compared to prompt-based black-box attacks [25] and white-box
gradient-based suffix optimization [12, 30], Amnesia is: training-
free, does not modify prompts or weights, and exploits internal
access only to a small set of attention activations. Compared to
global residual-direction methods [2, 11], it acts locally on the at-
tention value path at a single safety locus. We demonstrate that the
proposed attack can effectively compromise the security measures
of LLMs, eliciting harmful responses across a range of adversarial
use cases, such as antisocial behaviors. We measure attack success
rate (ASR) and benign utility metrics (MMLU, ROUGE, perplexity)
to ensure Amnesia’s utility. Furthermore, since the attack can be
carried out without any training data or fine-tuning, it is highly
practical and potentially dangerous if adequate protective measures
are not implemented promptly.

2.3
Knowledge Representation in Deep Neural
Networks
Studies have shown that in deep neural networks, lower level lay-
ers are responsible for recognizing low-level characteristic of the
training data, while recognition of higher level concepts increases
with the depth of the layers [3]. In case of LLM, this also holds
true that lower level layers are responsible for lexical features and
higher level layers are more associated with semantic features [16].
Furthermore, studies have also shown that for some tasks only a
subset of layers is responsible for the final output, i.e., for some tasks
the final output is comparable to the output of some intermediate
layers [8]. This presents two key observations:
Observation
(1) Different layers in a deep neural networks are respon-
sible for capturing different levels or types of information.
However, this representation could be sparse.
Observation
(2) The outputs of intermediate layers can be decoded to
yield interpretable or practically useful representations.
2.4
Intermediate Activations of LLMs
Existing research has demonstrated that it is possible to decode the
intermediate states of different modules of LLM into understand-
able and interpretable tokens. For instance, Belrose et al. [5] trained
an affine probe for each block in a pretrained model, enabling the
decoding of hidden states across the entire training vocabulary.
Moreover, studies have shown that such intermediate decoding is
feasible at various levels within the model, such as after the multi-
layer perceptron (MLP) modules, the attention mechanisms, and
the residual stream [24, 26]. The key insight behind intermediate
activations is that the activations of individual modules, particularly
in the higher layers, can often be decoded into meaningful inter-
pretations (observation 2). These intermediate decodings provide
valuable information about the specific layers and/or individual
modules at which LLMs begin to recognize different concepts. This
helps identify critical layers or modules, where techniques such as
concept mixing could be most effective.
2.5
Understanding Word Embedding and
Context Learning
In computational processing of natural language, textual input is
represented as numerical feature vectors, widely known as word
embeddings, which encode semantic meaning of each word or n-
gram [1]. There are many different approaches of implementing
word embeddings [10]. One of the key features of word embeddings
is that they represent the meaning of a word and some of them
also encode the context in which a word appears. For example, the
embeddings for a “black dog” are different from the embeddings
of a “white dog”. Another surprising property of the embeddings
is that many semantic and syntactic relationship among different
word embeddings are linear in the embedding space. For example,
the semantic difference between the embeddings of word “man”
and “women” is similar to the difference between the embeddings
of word “husband” and “wife”. Moreover, the syntactic difference of
word embeddings of “king” and embeddings of “man” and addition
of embedding of word “women” will be similar to “queen” [28].
Later in the development of the attack, we will also use similar
linear operations on embeddings to steer the models activations in
desired direction.
2.6
Threat Landscape: Attacks Targeting LLMs
Adversarial attacks on machine learning have been an extensive
topic in the last decade. Such adversarial attacks attempt to design
strategies to induce errors and irresponsible response [6, 21]. Nu-
merous types of attacks have been developed to exploit LLMs. One
of the most outstanding types of attacks among such attacks are
jailbreak attacks, where an attacker carefully designs user prompts
to bypass the safety measures of the LLMs to generate harmful or
unethical responses. There has been prevailing work on jailbreak
attacks. For example, [15] evaluated and assessed various methods
for prompt based jailbreaking. [29] proposed a method in the white
box setting with adversarial attack to create effective prompts for
jailbreaking. Their approach includes finding a suffix which, when
combined with a wide range of queries for LLMs, produces harmful
and unethical content. In the following sections, we briefly review
different types of attacks against LLMs.
2.6.1
Black-box Prompt-based Jailbreaks. Early jailbreaks operate
purely at the prompt level, treating the model as a black box and
exploiting its instruction-following bias. In-the-wild attacks such
as “Do-Anything-Now” (DAN) and related personas show that care-
fully crafted role-play or system prompts can consistently override
safety features of commercial and open models [25]. The Wild-
Jailbreak dataset collects 390 such “forbidden questions” across
13 OpenAI policy scenarios (economic harm, fraud, health, hate
speech, physical harm, etc.), providing a standardized benchmark
of organically discovered jailbreak prompts [25].
Beyond manual authored prompts, black-box optimization meth-
ods iteratively refine instructions using only input–output feed-
back. Chao et al. [7] introduce PAIR (Prompt Automatic Iterative
Refinement), which maintains a population of candidate jailbreak
prompts, queries the target model and a safety judge, and applies

mutation-type edits guided by success scores. Within roughly a
few dozen queries, PAIR reliably discovers high-success prompts
against diverse chat models, without any gradient access.
2.6.2
White-box Gradient-Based Prompt Attacks. When gradients
are available, attacks can move from heuristic edits to explicit opti-
mization in token space. Zou et al. [29] frame jailbreaks as learning
a short universal adversarial suffix: starting from a random token
sequence, they use a Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) procedure
to maximize the log-likelihood of an affirmative or non-refusal con-
tinuation across many harmful prompts. Once optimized, this suffix
is simply appended to new inputs and often transfers strongly to
other models, including aligned black-box APIs.
Li et al. [12] refine GCG-style attacks by importing ideas from ad-
versarial training in vision. They introduce Language-SGM (LSGM),
which dampens gradients from later residual blocks to focus on
robust directions, and Language-ILA (LILA), which aligns updates
with a mid-layer adversarial direction. Combined, these modifica-
tions substantially raise attack success rates on benchmarks like
AdvBench while improving transfer and computational efficiency
relative to vanilla GCG.
A complementary line of work shows that fine-tuning itself can
compromise alignment. Qi et al. [22] demonstrate that modest su-
pervised finetuning on harmful data, or even benign-looking data
with adversarially chosen labels, can erode safety guarantees and
resurrect unsafe behaviors, highlighting that post-hoc training is
not a simple, monotone-safe knob.
2.6.3
Activation-space and Weight-space Interventions. Recent mech-
anistic studies move from prompts to the model’s internal represen-
tation space. Arditi et al. [2] argue that refusals are largely mediated
by a single direction in the residual stream: by contrasting residual
activations for harmful prompts that are refused versus complied
with, they estimate a “refusal” vector and show that projecting this
direction out at inference time (or editing weights to be orthog-
onal to it) can strongly affect safety behavior. However, this ap-
proach involves computing activations across extensive collections
of harmful and benign prompts for each layer, which introduces
significant computational cost and depends on the availability of
curated datasets, making it computationally expensive, dependent
on curated datasets, and therefore impractical for attackers.
3
Proposed Attack
In this section, we provide a detailed description of the proposed
attack.
3.1
Threat Model
To describe the proposed attack, we first define the threat model
under which the adversary operates. The attacker operates under
the follwoing assumptions:
• We assume a white-box setting, where the attacker has full
access to the target model’s internal parameters. The ad-
versary has complete access to the model’s residual stream
and can manipulate it, i.e., can query the model freely and
observe its intermediate representations (e.g., hidden states
or layer activations)
• The attacker possesses sufficient computational resources
to perform inference and activation analyses on the model.
3.1.1
Attacker’s Objective. The primary goal of the attacker is to
manipulate the model by exploiting its internal representations
during inference time and to cause targeted misbehavior (e.g., pro-
ducing incorrect or harmful outputs). Furthermore, attacker’s goal
is also to interpret or reconstruct intermediate layer outputs to gain
insight into the model’s internal reasoning process.
The attack does not modify the training process or retrain the
model, it is purely inference-time manipulation.

4.2
Datasets
We evaluate the proposed attack against the following benchmark
datasets:
Forbidden Questions (WildJailbreak, 13 scenarios). This dataset
inlcudes “Forbidden Questions”, a subset of WildJailbreak [25],
comprising 390 base questions organized into 13 OpenAI-style
policy scenarios, including Economic Harm, Fraud, Financial Advice,
Health Consultation, Hate Speech, and Physical Harm (see Appendix
B for example samples from each category).
Harmful_Behaviours (AdvBench). We use the Harmful_behaviours
subset of AdvBench [29], which consists of templated prompts re-
lated to malware creation, fraud, and other explicitly unsafe activi-
ties. Although this dataset is more synthetic than WildJailbreak, it
covers a broader range of harmful behaviors.
Following prior work [29], we consider an attack successful if the
model does not refuse the prompt according to our refusal heuristic.
This approach only distinguishes between two outcomes, “refuse”
or “answer” without relying on additional ground-truth labels.

5
Comparison
This section presents a comparison of the proposed method to
existing white-box attack approaches, as summarized in Table 12.
We benchmark prior work along several key dimensions, including
the requirement for objective-based training (i.e., whether the attack
necessitates any model training), the need for fine-tuning or weight
steering (i.e., whether the method relies on weight adjustment or
direct weight manipulation), reliance on prompt modification (i.e.,
whether the attack alters the user’s input prompt), and the scope
of activation steering (i.e., whether activations must be computed
across all the layers or only within a localized layer). Together, these
criteria provide insight into the operational efficiency and practical
deployability of each attack. To ensure a fair comparison, we focus
on broad, high-level methodological characteristics rather than
narrow or implementation-specific details. This abstraction enables
a more general and meaningful assessment of the applicability and
effectiveness of each attack strategy.
The comparative results in Table 12 highlight several important
distinctions among existing attack methods and the proposed ap-
proach. Methods such as Arditi et al. [2] and AutoDAN [14] both
rely on objective-based training, indicating a higher setup cost for
an adversary (Arditi et al. [2] do not directly train on objective
function, however they still calculate activations using two datasets
and edit the weights based on the mean values. Therefore, we con-
sider it as objective training), while [7, 12, 30] similarly require
model-driven optimization during attack construction. In contrast,
the proposed method eliminates the need for any training, substan-
tially reducing computational overhead and simplifying the attack
pipeline.
With respect to model modification, Arditi et al. [2]’s approach
additionally requires fine-tuning or weight steering, which intro-
duces further engineering complexity and restricts practical de-
ployment under constrained computation. AutoDAN and PAIR
avoid weight updates but instead depend on prompt modification.
The proposed method, by avoiding both weight manipulation and
prompt alteration, offers a more streamlined and stealthy attack
mechanism.
The scope of activation steering further differentiates these ap-
proaches. Arditi et al. [2]’s attack requires global activation compu-
tation across full model layers, which is computationally demand-
ing and less scalable (for example, for bigger models). In contrast,
the proposed method employs local activation steering, restrict-
ing computation to targeted regions/layers and thereby improving
efficiency while maintaining effectiveness.
Overall, the comparison demonstrates that the proposed method
achieves a favorable balance of minimal prerequisites, no training,
no weight adjustments, no prompt modification while also reducing
computational burden through localized activation analysis. These
characteristics collectively suggest that the proposed approach is
more practical and broadly deployable than prior methods.
5.1
Comparision with SOTA
In this section, we provide a direct comparison with the SOTA
method proposed by Arditi et al. [2], which is most closely related
to our work. It is important to note, however, that according to the
objective criteria described above, our approach outperforms Arditi
et al. [2] in several key aspects. For example, our method does not
require two separate datasets to compute activation means, and it
operates at the level of a single local layer, whereas Arditi et al. [2]
work relies on multiple layers and involves weight editing.
Nevertheless, we include a comparison of attack results here to
assess whether our method yields similar outcomes. We use the
Forbidden Questions and AdvBench to evaluate the proposed attack.
Furthermore, we also evaluate Amnesia on the HARMBENCH “stan-
dard behaviors” test set of 159 prompts used by Arditi et al. [2]. This
allows a more direct comparison to the weight-orthogonalization
baseline ORTHO under a HarmBench-style evaluation protocol.
Following the HARMBENCH setup, we:
• generate greedy completions (temperature ≈0, maximum
generation length = 512 tokens) with Llama-2-7B-Chat on
the 159 “standard behaviors”;
• post-hoc classify each completion as safe or unsafe using
the open-source Meta-Llama-Guard-2-8B safety model;

6
Discussion and Limitations
In this work, we demonstrate that an attacker can successfully jail-
break a model using a simple yet highly effective method based
solely on modifying activations at a single layer, without any weight
updates, objective-function training, or changes that compromise
overall model coherence. Notably, the attack does not require large-
scale harmful or benign datasets; it operates only on a set of key-
words and a simple adversarial query.
6.1
Limitations
Although the study introduces a simple and effective attack tech-
nique, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, while we
evaluate our approach on a selection of state-of-the-art models,
we cannot claim its generalizability to architectures that were not
tested. Second, our method relies on empirically identifying the
scaling factor 𝛼and the steering vector Sb. Rigorous investigation
on optimizing this process, e.g., optimizing 𝛼with respect to the
ASR, remains open for future work. Ultimately, we view this study
as a proof-of-concept demonstrating that model refusals can be
altered through simple additive steering of activations at a single
layer.
6.2
Ethical Considerations
Additionally, while the findings in this study may raise concerns
about misuse, they are crucial for raising awareness about poten-
tial vulnerabilities and gaps in current LLM safety measures. This
awareness can inspire researchers to devise more robust and se-
cure solutions to counter adversarial attacks. If such security flaws
go unaddressed, malicious actors could exploit them covertly for
harmful purposes, such as creating advanced malware, spreading
propaganda, or engaging in other antisocial activities.
By sharing findings, we aim to encourage further research into
developing safer LLMs and improving the understanding of current
model limitations. This transparency can serve as a catalyst for
innovation in security measures, ensuring that LLMs are better
equipped to operate responsibly and ethically in the real world.
7
Conclusions and Future Work
In this paper, we presented a lightweight adversarial attack aimed
at compromising the safe and responsible behavior of SOTA open-
weight LLMs. Using the open source model (such as Llama-2 and
Llama-3)3, we demonstrated how the proposed attack can effectively
undermine the security measures designed to ensure these models
behave safely and responsibly. Through experimental evaluations
on models comprising billions of parameters, we showcased the
attack’s effectiveness in eliciting harmful behavior. Our findings
reveal the alarming ease with which attackers can bypass protective
measures intended to safeguard open-weight LLMs, exposing a
critical vulnerability. This study underscores the urgent need for
more robust protective mechanisms to be developed by the research
community.
For future work, we plan to explore the use of informed activa-
tion manipulation, which involves introducing malicious behav-
ior in specific layers of the model. This approach, combined with
techniques like Amnesia (which mitigates safe and responsible
responses), aims to make the LLM’s output more creative and ma-
licious. Investigating this will help us better understand the full
potential and consequences of such attacks.
