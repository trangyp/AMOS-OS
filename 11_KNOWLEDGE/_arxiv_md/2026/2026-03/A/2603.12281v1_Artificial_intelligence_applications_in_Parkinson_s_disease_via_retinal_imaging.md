Abstract 
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is projected to increase substantially due to population aging, making early 
diagnosis increasingly important, as timely detection may delay progression and reduce long-term 
complications. Retinal microvasculature has emerged as a promising anatomical biomarker of 
neurodegeneration, and when combined with artificial intelligence AI, retinal imaging may provide an 
advanced, noninvasive, and cost-effective screening strategy for PD. This study evaluated the evidence 
from the past 35 years regarding the capability of AI to detect early PD-related changes in retinal vascular 
structure. Five electronic databases including PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and 
ProQuest were systematically searched from January 1990 to January 2025. In addition, Annals of 
Neurology and Frontiers in Neuroscience were hand-searched, and the reference lists of included studies 
were screened for additional eligible publications. Nineteen studies met the inclusion criteria. Three 
principal diagnostic AI tasks were identified, including disease classification, retinal vessel segmentation, 
and PD risk stratification. The best-performing models were ShAMBi-LSTM on the Drishti dataset with 
97.2 percent accuracy, 99.5 percent precision, 96.9 percent sensitivity, and an F1 score of 0.981 for 
classification, nnU-Net with 99.7 percent accuracy, 98.7 percent precision, 98.9 percent sensitivity, 99.8 
percent specificity, and a Dice score of 98.9 percent for segmentation, and AlexNet for risk prediction with 
area under the curve values of 0.77, 0.68, and 0.73 across datasets. Overall, application of AI algorithms to 
retinal vasculature for detecting early signs of PD and predicting disease severity suggests that integration 
of AI with retinal biomarkers holds substantial potential for earlier and more accurate detection compared 
with traditional clinical evaluation alone. 
Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Parkinson`s disease, Retinal imaging, Microvasculature, Deep learning.

1. Introduction 
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disease affecting nearly 12 million people 
worldwide (Luo et al., 2025). PD incidence increases with age; only 4% of cases are less than 50 years old 
(Van Den Eeden et al., 2003). With global population aging, the prevalence of PD is estimated to increase 
by 76% from 2021 to 2050 (Su et al., 2025). Early diagnosis is crucial, as timely detection and management 
can delay progression and reduce long-term complications. Clinical diagnosis of PD is based on detailed 
history and neurological examination. It is important to exclude neurological conditions that mimic PD 
(Kobylecki, 2020) using neuroimaging, e.g., magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Heim et al., 2017; 
Pyatigorskaya et al., 2014). Accurate diagnosis enables appropriate management, but the task is onerous 
and requires expertise. There is an unmet need for accessible screening tools for population-level detection 
of PD.  
Retinal imaging has been proposed as a cost-effective solution for PD screening. The retina 
undergoes distinct structural and functional changes in PD. PD severity and duration are negatively 
associated with retinal ganglion cell inner plexiform (GCL-IPL) and ganglion cell complex (GCC) 
thicknesses (Bayhan et al., 2014; Sari et al., 2015). The close relationship between retinal and central 
nervous system (CNS) pathologies is implied by several studies. Simultaneously, in PD, there are numerous 
degenerative, dysfunctional, and structural retinal changes. PD duration is negatively correlated with retinal 
nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness, and the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) is 
negatively correlated with light-adapted electroretinography findings (Elanwar et al., 2023). Notably, retinal 
imaging data are amenable to artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled analysis. Both machine learning (ML) and 
deep learning (DL) are increasingly being applied in medical imaging to perform various tasks, such as 
image segmentation, artifact detection, quality improvement, and image classification (Thrall et al., 2018). 
This work reviews published research on AI applications for various aspects of PD detection using retinal 
imaging (Figure 1).

2. Method 
We performed a literature search for articles published between January 1990 to January 2025 in 
PubMed, Web of Science (WOS), Scopus, ScienceDirect, and ProQuest. We included original research that 
employed ML or DL for the prediction and diagnosis of PD in patients, including image processing (e.g., 
image segmentation) of retinal images obtained by different methods, such as fundoscopy and optical 
coherence tomography (OCT). We excluded review studies, book chapters, editorials, letters, studies that 
did not use ML or DL, studies that did not include patients with PD, studies that did not use retinal images,

4.1 Classification 
21 
A support vector machine (SVM) is a ML algorithm used to identify hyperplanes and categorize 
22 
data into distinct groups (Cosma et al., 2017). SVM is mainly classified as a supervised learning model and 
23 
can perform linear and non-linear classifications (An et al., 2021; Mack, 2014; Winters-Hilt & Merat, 2007). 
24 
Nunes et al. (2019) (Nunes et al., 2019) used multiple non-linear SVM models to differentiate between 
25 
healthy controls (HC), AD, and PD. They included OCT images from a local dataset comprising 75 
26 
participants. The gray-level co-occurrence matrix was used for the feature extractor. In the next stage, each 
27 
SVM model was used as a binary classification between two of the three possible groups. Each model also 
28 
assessed each of the five retinal layers individually to make the diagnosis. They claim that excluding the 
29 
RNFL layer improved classification performance, and it was excluded. The best reported accuracy was 
30 
achieved with a five-fold cross-validation training model, ranging from 75.3% to 87.7% (median = 80.8%). 
31 
They also report that 94.4% (median; range: 88.9-96.6%; k=10) of diagnoses were correct when both eyes 
32 
of a participant received the same classification. In all scenarios, the model achieved the highest specificity 
33


## Page 21


21 
 
for PD and the highest sensitivity for HC, compared with AD. This finding may be due to the larger sample 
34 
sizes used for PD and HC compared with AD. 
35 
Temporal RNFL layer thickness correlates with functional PD scoring (Chang et al., 2022; Shafiee 
36 
et al., 2019). The correlation between retinal thickness and PD severity may be due to dopamine's role in 
37 
maintaining retinal thickness (Ahn et al., 2018; Svetel et al., 2025). Ahn et al. (2023) (Ahn et al., 2023) 
38 
developed five CNN models to differentiate PD from patients with atypical motor abnormalities. ResNet-
39 
18 achieved the best results with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.76 and an accuracy of 76.75%. Then, 
40 
they used the ResNet-18 model as a basis to predict H-Y scale and UPDRS-III (PD severity assessment) 
41 
scores from color fundus photography (CFP) and demographic data, including age, sex, hypertension status, 
42 
and diabetes status. For the H-Y stage (3-class), the best model (Model 4) achieved a sensitivity of 83.23%, 
43 
a specificity of 66.81%, an AUROC of 0.77, and an accuracy of 73.38% on internal data. For UPDRS-III 
44 
(3-class), Model 4 reached a sensitivity of 82.61% (81.38–83.83), a specificity of 65.75%, an AUROC of 
45

46 
70.73%, specificity 66.66%, AUROC 0.67, and accuracy 70.45%.

Álvarez-Rodríguez (2024) (Álvarez-Rodríguez et al., 2024) evaluated screening performance 
48 
across single-view (optic disc or macula) and multi-view (fusion of both) configurations, using F1-score as 
49 
the primary metric due to class imbalance for classification of PD from other neurological diseases. The 
50 
optic-disc single-view yields the weakest results, with PD F1 of approximately 0.50–0.6. Macular 2D 
51 
representations improved slightly. The strongest single-view performance is achieved by macular 3D 
52 
volume features, with PD F1 typically 0.70–0.80. Multi-view 2D (macula 2D + disc) sits around 0.60–0.70, 
53 
showing a gain over either 2D view alone but still below macula-3D. Multi-view 3D and 2D+3D fusion 
54 
generally matches macula-3D at ~0.70–0.80 with slightly lower variance rather than a consistent jump. In 
55 
short, the ranking is: macula-3D ≳ multi-view (3D or 2D+3D) > multi-view 2D > macula-2D >> optic disc 
56 
only. An important innovation of this study is the combination of nnU-Net for segmentation, the DieT 
57 
feature extractor, and a histogram-based gradient-boosting classification tree for classification. Although 
58

Lee et al. (2023) (Lee et al., 2023) utilized the AlexNet model to classify high- and low-quality 
61

62

63

64

65 
AI can detect and learn specific patterns in data and use them for classification. For example, PD 
66 
and primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) both result in retinal thinning (UlAin et al., 2025). Chrysou et 
67

68

69

70

71 
plexiform layer (IPL) (34.2 µm vs. 36.1 µm) and the outer plexiform layer (OPL) (22.3 µm vs. 23.1 µm). 
72 
Subsequently, they trained a random forest (RF) model to distinguish between PD and POAG and between 
73 
PD and HC. RF randomly selects a feature from the data and then grows decision trees. The classification 
74 
is done based on the results of all grown decision trees (Salman et al., 2024). Chrysou et al.'s RF model 
75 
successfully identified the former layers (GCL, RPE, IPL, and OPL) as the best features in differentiating 
76

77 
in differentiating PD from HC (0.92 vs. 0.71), which may be due to a greater thickness difference between 
78 
PD and POAG.

4.2. Segmentation 
145 
Context encoder network (CE-Net) is a DL algorithm proposed by Gu et al. (2019) (Gu et al., 2019) 
146 
for medical image segmentations. CE-Net segments 2D images using three main components: a feature 
147 
encoder, a content extractor, and a feature decoder. The encoder used in this model is a pre-trained ResNet-
148 
34 architecture trained on the ImageNet dataset. The content extractor uses a dense atrous convolutional 
149 
block and a residual multi-kernel pooling block to minimize spatial data loss. Zhao et al. (2021) (Zhao et 
150 
al., 2021) used the CE-Net model for OCT image segmentation and determined the retinal layer thickness 
151 
changes in PD. They report that the interdigitation zone and retinal pigment epithelium-Bruch membrane 
152 
complex were thicker in PD compared to healthy controls. However, their study included only six 
153 
participants with PD and utilized a cross-sectional design. 
154 
The multi-scale GCN-assisted U-shape network (MGU-Net) was first developed by Li et al. (2021) 
155 
(Li et al., 2021). It aimed to segment retinal layers from OCT images in different patients using a U-Net-
156


## Page 26


26 
 
based approach. This method was superior to similar algorithms, but it required that the input images be 
157 
standardized in a specific manner. Gende et al. (2023) (Gende et al., 2023) applied the MGU-Net to retinal 
158 
layer segmentation of different neurological diseases, including PD. The segmentation task is performed in 
159 
two stages: first, the retina is separated from background noise, and then the layers are identified. The best 
160 
PD performance was achieved by multi-disease training: leave-one-disease-out (train on other NDDs, test 
161 
on PD) yielded RNFL precision of 0.938 and GCL–BM precision of 0.984; pooling all diseases produced 
162 
essentially the same results. Training on a single disease and testing on PD yielded weaker results for RNFL 
163 
(precision of 0.930–0.946 with lower Dice scores), while GCL–BM remained near-ceiling (~0.983–0.989 
164 
Dice). The main limitation of this study is treating GCL-BM as a single complex, as in the study by Álvarez-
165 
Rodríguez (Álvarez-Rodríguez et al., 2024). 
166 
They reported uniformly strong layer masks across views: on macula OCT, the RNFL dice score 
167

168

169

170

172

4.3. PD Risk Prediction 
198 
The retinal age gap, defined as the difference between deep learning predicted retinal age from 
199 
fundus photographs and chronological age, has emerged as a systemic risk signal; for example, Zhu et al. 
200 
(2022) (Zhu et al., 2023) showed that a positive retinal age gap is associated with higher all-cause mortality. 
201 
Building on this concept, Hu et al. (2022) (Hu et al., 2022) tested whether the retinal age gap predicts 
202 
incident Parkinson’s disease. The underlying rationale is that retinal tissue undergoes neurodegenerative 
203 
and vascular changes that parallel those in the brain; therefore, accelerated retinal ageing may signal an 
204 
elevated risk of PD. In the UK Biobank cohort of 35,834 PD-free participants (median follow-up 5.83 years; 
205 
63 incident PD), each 1-year increase in retinal age gap was associated with a 10% higher risk of developing 
206

234 
To reduce the data required to train AI models, Varghese et al. (2023) [24] used few-shot learning 
235 
methods. Multiple few-shot approaches, such as TL, have been proposed; however, the technique used by 
236 
Verghese et al. (2023) (Varghese et al., 2023) is called inductive logic programming (ILP). ILP logically 
237

learns facts from background knowledge and from positive and negative examples (Kononenko & Kukar, 
238 
2007). The ILP uses these inputs to infer and generate a point, and then makes decisions based on that point. 
239 
Verghese et al. (2023) (Varghese et al., 2023) extracted histograms from patients' CFPs and used binning 
240 
methods to enable the ILP to learn them. The ILP models were then tested for the diagnosis of multiple

5. Limitations and Future Direction 
246 
Study design: Some studies have attempted to use longitudinal data to predict future risk of PD 
247 
development [52]; however, most studies employ a cross-sectional design, focusing on retinal segmentation 
248 
and PD/HC classification. Levodopa treatment affects the RNFL thickness in PD (Sen et al., 2014). RNFL 
249 
thickness can predict the future cognitive decline of PD (Zhang et al., 2021). These findings suggest that 
250 
retinal imaging may serve as a biomarker of disease progression and treatment response, and that 
251 
longitudinal studies can evaluate AI's performance in these tasks. 
252 
Sample size and Datasets: Only a few studies included more than 100 patients with PD (Ahn et 
253 
al., 2023; Chrysou et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2023; Varghese et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023). It is noteworthy 
254 
that the study by Hu et al. (Hu et al., 2022) used a large dataset, but, given its aim, no patients with PD were 
255 
included at baseline. Only a small number of studies have used large databases such as the UK Biobank 
256 
(Hu et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2022; Tran et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2023) or iMIND (Lee et al., 2023). Other 
257 
databases used in the studies were MEH-MIDAS (Zhou et al., 2023), MEH-AlzEye (Zhou et al., 2023), 
258 
Heidelberg-SPECTRALIS (Álvarez-Rodríguez et al., 2024), and OCD-PD (Chen et al., 2024). Small 
259 
sample sizes in studies can significantly reduce model performance and increase model uncertainty (Riley 
260 
et al., 2025). The optimal sample size for each study should be calculated based on its specific setting. The 
261 
type of outcome, the number of predictor variables, the prevalence of the outcome in target individuals, the 
262


## Page 32


32 
 
desired margin of error, the desired shrinkage factor, and the signal-to-noise ratio (Riley et al., 2020). 
263 
PMASAMPSIZA and PMVALSAMPSIZE are Stata models proposed for calculating the minimum required 
264 
sample sizes for training and validation of models (Ensor, 2018, 2023; Riley et al., 2025). Using multiple 
265 
databases can increase sample size, but this should be done carefully to reduce bias during data integration 
266 
(Putrama & Martinek, 2024). 
267 
Generative models: Studies can also employ generative methods to artificially increase sample 
268 
size. These models can detect features and generate similar data that is indistinguishable from the original 
269 
ones (Reddy, 2024). The data can then be used to train an actual model. This way, studies can increase their 
270 
sample sizes with high-quality images while keeping costs to a minimum. Generative adversarial networks 
271 
(GANs) are among the most widely used generative AI models for data synthesis (Suthar et al., 2022). 
272 
Using these methodologies is reported to increase the accuracy compared to conventional models (Gulakala 
273 
et al., 2022). GANs are less frequently used in ophthalmology-related image generation than in other 
274 
medical fields. However, it has been demonstrated that GANs can generate high-quality OCT images useful 
275 
for educational purposes (Peng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2021).  
276 
GANs are also used to create future CFPs for age-related macular degeneration from baseline 
277 
fundus images (Pham et al., 2022). In a study by Coronado et al. (Coronado et al., 2023)GAN recreated 
278 
OCTA images from CFP. This approach can effectively reduce the cost while maintaining high-quality data. 
279 
These results demonstrate that GANs can be effectively applied to retinal imaging research, including OCT, 
280 
OCTA, and CFP images. Although the use of generative models can improve performance, some reports 
281 
indicate that they have led to a decline (Riley et al., 2025). Thus, the authors need to apply them carefully.  
282 
Transfer learning: We also encourage testing the proposed models with other external data 
283 
sources. AI models tend to perform worse when presented with data that differs from the data on which 
284 
they were fine-tuned. Most studies used ImageNet as the source domain and medical images as the target 
285 
domain. ImageNet is an extensive database of mostly non-medical images (Deng et al., 2009). The 
286 
similarity between the source and target domains in TL-based training approaches can enhance AI 
287


## Page 33


33 
 
efficiency (Peng et al., 2022) and improve performance. Using PD-based databases as the source domain 
288 
and fine-tuning the models on local PD data may yield more accurate models.  
289 
Explainable AI: Using explainable AI (XAI) models can help identify the features the AI model 
290 
uses for its tasks. Only a few studies have used this method (Hu et al., 2025; Tran et al., 2024). However, 
291 
the study by Hu et al. (Hu et al., 2025) applied XAI to patients with PD. Using XAI models can enable 
292 
human operators to override AI results (Ali et al., 2023). Large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT, 
293 
can also help summarize and organize the findings of other AI models and prepare an interpretable report 
294 
(Parillo et al., 2024). In specific situations, LLMs have also demonstrated potential to generate plausible 
295 
answers when provided with sufficient clinical data (Gilson et al., 2023). Using XAI and LLMs can help 
296 
clinicians better understand the AI`s reasoning and make models more user-friendly. However, these models 
297 
should be used with extreme caution, as there are reports of misdiagnoses, incorrect reports, and errors (de 
298 
Bruijn et al., 2022; Parillo et al., 2024). Future studies can integrate XAI and LLMs into the current 
299 
proposed methodologies for retinal imaging in PD and investigate their performance. Figure 6 summarizes 
300 
the proposed future directions.

6. Conclusion 
304 
In this review, we have summarized proposed AI models for detecting PD, segmenting the retina 
305 
in PD, enhancing image quality, predicting PD severity, and forecasting PD progression. Retinal thickness, 
306 
particularly the RNFL and GCL, is reduced in patients with PD. The former changes are observed early in 
307 
PD and can serve as valuable biomarkers for diagnosis. AI can segment retinal layers from OCT images or 
308 
use texture analysis of OCTA and CFP to predict PD from HC and other neurodegenerative diseases, such 
309 
as AD, WMH, and multiple sclerosis, as well as other diseases with Parkinsonian features. The performance 
310 
of AI models depends on many factors, such as sample size, database balance, use of TL, AI architecture, 
311 
imaging technology, image quality, task complexity, and the number of classification classes. AI shows 
312 
promising results in PD-related functions. However, the use of TL, XAI, and generative models can increase 
313 
the performance. Future research can elucidate the role of retinal imaging in PD progression and  
314 
prognostication using AI models trained on larger longitudinal datasets.
