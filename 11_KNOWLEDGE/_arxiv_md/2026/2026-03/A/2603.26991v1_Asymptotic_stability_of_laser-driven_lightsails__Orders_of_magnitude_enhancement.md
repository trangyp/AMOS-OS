Lightsails are promising spacecraft that can traverse interstellar distances within decades via
radiation-pressure propulsion from high-power lasers. The envisioned missions crucially rely on the
sail being confined within the propelling laser beam, requiring restoring and damping mechanisms
for both translational and rotational degrees of freedom. Here, we use a two-dimensional rigid model
to show that full asymptotic stability of planar nanophotonic sails can be achieved through purely
optical, relativistic forces and torques, which damp all unstable degrees of freedom. By judiciously
optimizing the angular and frequency dispersion of diffraction gratings, we find that damping can
be enhanced by orders of magnitude compared to plane-mirror sails. Therefore, relativistic effects
can, in principle, provide comprehensive and realistic control over lightsail motion.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Detailed imaging of neighboring exoplanets such as
Proxima B at a distance of 4.2 light years is near-
impossible using Earth-based instruments, making long-
distance probes an enticing method for determining exo-
planet habitability.
To approach nearby stars within
a human lifetime, probes traveling at near-relativistic
speeds (e.g. 0.2c) are necessary. Lightsails are a con-
tender for such missions, with realistic technological
progress expected over the next few decades. The cur-
rent vision considers a payload attached to a ∼10 m2 sail
membrane with a small total mass (< 10 g), accelerated
by a kilometer-scale, 50 GW laser over several minutes
(or over a distance of 0.1 AU) [1–3]. With a kilometer-
scale laser-array aperture, the laser would be capable of
focusing on the comparatively narrow meter-scale sail
throughout the acceleration phase and thus impart maxi-
mum momentum. Critically, the sail must remain within
the laser focus. Any perturbations on the sail’s motion
(e.g. due to laser-beam distortion, imperfect tracking or
noise) can misalign the sail relative to the beam center,
leading to unbalanced forces and torques that generate

transverse displacements (relative to the beam propaga-
tion direction) and rotations. Due to the large distances
involved, feedback to the laser source is too slow, and
with no mass budget for active corrective thrusters on
the sail, the best option is to use radiation pressure itself
to provide stabilization of the sail within the beam. Ra-
diative restoring forces and torques have been proposed,
with nanophotonic structures [4–8] emerging as preferred
candidates over mirror-based, shaped structures [9–11] in
the literature [12].
However, these restoring mechanisms only provide
marginal stability, meaning restoring forces and torques
lead to sail oscillations that do not decay and can in fact
grow under stochastic excitation. Marginally stable de-
signs can therefore be perturbed off-course or leave the
beam altogether [Fig. 1].
Thus, in addition to restoring mechanisms, damping
mechanisms are necessary. In the right conditions, damp-
ing can lead to transverse displacements, rotations and
velocities that all decay in time, in which case the sail is
said to be asymptotically stable [13] [Fig. 1]. Damping is
challenging to implement in the vacuum of space. One
proposed damping method is feedback-based parametric
stabilization, where the laser is periodically modulated to
create time-varying force coefficients [14, 15], reducing
the amplitude of the oscillations. Such active-feedback
solutions need to be synchronized with the phase of the
sail’s oscillations, which becomes difficult once the light

II.
LIGHTSAIL GEOMETRY
A.
Overview
The full description of the geometry and relativistic ef-
fects requires multiple reference frames, coordinate sys-
tems and a formalism which can rapidly obfuscate the
essence of the problem. We thus begin with a simplified
overview, entrusting greater detail to subsequent subsec-
tions.
We consider a rigid flat sail in two dimensions, with
rest length L′ and mass m. Our aim is to calculate the dy-
namics for a “bigrating”, where the left and right sections
are single diffraction gratings (depicted in Fig. 2(a)), but
much of our formalism is more generally applicable to
planar sails. The left and right sections must be mirror-
symmetric relative to each other at the center-of-mass
(CoM) O′, where the sections are attached. Each sec-
tion reflects light non-specularly, for example in different
grating orders, which leads to a net change in momentum
components both perpendicular and parallel to the sail.
This leads to acceleration (in the direction of the laser)
and a transverse force (orthogonal to the laser).
When the sail is centered on the beam and its plane
is aligned orthogonal to the beam axis, the mirror sym-
metry ensures that the two sections experience identical
longitudinal forces and that the transverse forces have
opposite directions (and equal magnitude). Thus, there
are no net transverse forces and no torques, which de-
fines a dynamic equilibrium position in which the sail
is accelerated purely in the direction of the laser, as de-
sired. The two sections are designed so that the net trans-
verse forces and torques are restoring towards equilibrium
should the sail be perturbed away from equilibrium (at
least for small movements) [22].
In the instantaneously comoving (inertial) frame M
attached to the CoM, perturbations may rotate the sail.
Rotation speeds are non-relativistic so that rotational
motion can be calculated using non-relativistic rigid body
mechanics in that frame. All other kinematics and dy-
namics need a full relativistic treatment.
The sail is illuminated by a laser beam modeled as a
Gaussian. The laser beam originates from earth at fixed
wavelength λ0, with a wavevector pointing towards the
sail’s destination. The rapid movement of the sail CoM
leads to a longitudinal Doppler shift that can be sub-
stantial (up to 22% for 0.2c).
As the sail accelerates,
the laser wavelength measured in frame M is continually
redshifting, depicted by the redshift of the green laser
between Figs. 2(bi) and 2(bii). Once perturbed, the sail
oscillates at relatively low (i.e. non-relativistic) veloci-
ties in the directions transverse to the beam axis. This
slightly modifies the Doppler coefficients compared to a
pure longitudinal motion. More significantly, the trans-
verse velocities cause relativistic aberration, a change of
the angle of incidence of the light in the sail’s reference
frame [Fig. 2(b)]. If the sail rotates, each point along
the sail has a different velocity relative to the laser. This
velocity results in the Doppler shift and relativistic aber-
ration acquiring dependence upon the position along the
sail.
The relativistic aberration and Doppler shift’s depen-
dence on position along the sail provide the damping
mechanisms [18]:
a.
Damping force
Consider a sail with a non-zero
velocity transverse to the laser axis. Due to relativistic
aberration, the laser light is incident at an angle θ′ ̸= 0
that is proportional to the transverse velocity [Fig. 2(b)].
By properly designing the angle dependence of scattered
orders, aberration can result in a transverse force propor-
tional to and opposite the transverse velocity [20], i.e. a
damping force.
b.
Damping torque
A sail rotating clockwise around
its CoM as in Fig. 2(c) has a higher velocity away from
the laser on its left tip than on the right tip. Light on
the left tip is redshifted relative to the light on the right
tip, carrying less momentum. There is thus a force im-
balance leading to a torque countering the rotation, i.e. a
damping torque. The argument is presented for a simple
mirror, but we will show in Sec. V that this effect can
be enhanced dramatically when reflection coefficients de-
pend on wavelength, as small differences in wavelength
can become large differences in forces between the left
and right sides.
The scattering properties affect restoring and damping
forces and torques, but also lead to cross-coupling, i.e.
torques due to translation and forces due to rotation.
The system is thus rich in dynamics and requires careful
optimization to achieve asymptotic stability.
While the relevant effects are perhaps conceptually
straightforward, their quantitative analysis requires care-
ful consideration of the dynamics and momentum four-
vectors of the light in different reference frames, and ap-
propriate parameterization which we detail below.

B.
Integration
A significant caveat with the equations of motion
(Eqs. (5)) is that they are only valid in frame M. Rota-
tions are nonrelativistic in frame M, meaning the rota-
tions can be treated in this frame using Newtonian me-
chanics. However, quantities such as the sail’s position
or velocity must be known and updated in frame L. In
L, the rotating sail cannot be considered rigid because
length contractions are different along g′
2. That is, no
single angle can describe the rotational motion. To re-
solve this issue, we develop a “comoving integrator” for
relativistic-lightsail dynamics, which applies rigid-body
dynamics in frame M while tracking the sail’s position
and velocity in frame L.
In broad terms, the comoving integrator works as fol-
lows. We apply forces and torques in frame M at time
t′, updating the sail’s position, orientation and velocity
for time t′+dt′. The updated position and velocity infor-
mation is transferred to frame L via an inverse Lorentz
transformation. Then, the sail’s new rest frame at t′+dt′
is defined by its new velocity in L, and the process re-
peats. Angle and rotation information, which are only
meaningful in M due to the sail’s non-rigidity in L, are
not considered in L. Therefore, rotational dynamics is
only treated in the comoving frame. The method is il-
lustrated in Fig. 3, which we subsequently describe in

IV.
LINEAR STABILITY ANALYSIS
The full nonlinear equations of motion derived in Ap-
pendix B are difficult to interpret. Furthermore, using
them to assess sail stability would require a full set of dy-
namics simulations with different initial conditions. In-
stead, we perform a linear stability analysis on the sail
in a Gaussian beam, keeping the relativistic corrections.
We extract coefficients representing the restoring mech-
anisms, relativistic damping mechanisms and coupling
between rotational and translational degrees of freedom.
These enable predictions of asymptotic stability from the
eigenvalues of a Jacobian matrix, which we subsequently
use to optimize bigrating sails.
Frame M is inappropriate for stability analysis be-
cause, by definition, the lightsail’s CoM is at rest in this
frame. Therefore, we define a new frame U that is cen-
tered on the laser-beam axis and has the same longitu-
dinal velocity (vx) as the sail according to L. Quantities
measured in frame U are marked with two primes. By
performing a Lorentz boost with velocity vx = (vx, 0)
from L to U, the velocity of the sail in U is (0, γ(vx)vy),
where vy is the lateral velocity of the sail in L. Hence,
frame U and M coincide when vy = 0. Since vy ≪c,
we approximate that the forces derived in M are valid
in U, which ceases to be an approximation during linear
stability analysis because vy = 0 at dynamic equilibrium.
We limit the stability analysis to the transverse sub-
space, i.e. without the longitudinal coordinate or veloc-
ity. This is permissible because, to lowest order, near the
dynamic equilibrium the longitudinal acceleration decou-

VI.
DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS
In this paper, relativistic damping was shown to be ef-
fective at providing asymptotic stability for laser-driven
lightsails when harnessing grating resonances. However,
the degree of damping enhancement with nanophotonic
structures can, in principle, be increased further.
Im-
provements may come from alternative optimizations
(e.g.
over a larger parameter space with a neural-
network-enhanced search [32]) or a multiple-grating de-
sign [4, 8].
The latter idea is a natural extension be-
cause it is highly likely that multiple photonic sections
are needed to simultaneously satisfy all lightsail require-
ments such as propulsion, thermal management and sta-
bility [12]. For stability in particular, multiple gratings
can divide the wide Doppler band by having each grating
operate at a preset portion of the full wavelength range.
With narrow-band resonances, the dispersion enhance-
ment within the operating band of the individual grat-
ings can be extreme, as highlighted in Sec. V B, where the
eigenvalues were enhanced by a factor 1000 over narrow
bands compared to the full Doppler spectrum. By com-
bining multiple gratings that contain such enhancement,
we expect broadband damping that is orders of magni-
tude larger than those obtained with the single grating.
The main issue with the multiple-grating approach is the
possibility of the out-of-band, “inactive” gratings gener-
ating instability.
Alternatively, strategies to decrease the operating
bandwidth can be employed, activating the resonant ef-
fects for the entire acceleration phase. Conceptually, the
simplest method is to tune the laser to higher frequency
during acceleration. This requires > 200 nm bandwidth,
which would be extremely difficult given the laser-array
scale required for the mission. Another approach is to
uniformly stretch the sail, thereby increasing the grating
pitch in step with the increasing wavelength.
Stretch-
ing can be performed using centrifugal forces (e.g. sail
spinning) on a sail with small Young’s modulus or by ap-
propriating the sail heating to promote thermal expan-
sion. However, stretching likely cannot accommodate the
full 22% wavelength shift as the amount of elongation is
limited by the tensile strength of materials.

and the Appendices can readily be applied to other
sail geometries and extended to three dimensions. For

adequate for gauging the dynamical behavior of light-
sails close to equilibrium (i.e.
with displacement, an-

zero). However, this analysis cannot determine the sail
dynamics sufficiently far away from equilibrium. That
is, there is a region of state space around equilibrium
within which the sail is stable, but sufficient perturba-
tions out of this stable subspace would usher the nonlin-
ear regime. Moreover, linear stability analysis does not
predict the volume of the stable subspace for a particu-
lar laser-grating configuration. The required phase-space
volume of the asymptotic-stability region for the mis-
sion depends on the expected perturbation magnitudes
and the properties of the sail. For example, our grating
optimization relied on dispersion enhancement near the
first-order diffraction cutoff in wavelength. As the wave-
length approaches cutoff, the range of sail angles where
non-specular grating orders exist tends to zero (as per the
grating equation).
Therefore, small angular perturba-
tions can render the non-specular orders evanescent, de-
stroying asymptotic stability. Future optimizations must
account for a sufficiently large region around equilibrium
for which the sail is asymptotically stable.
In advanced lightsail designs, enhanced dynamic sta-
bility and propulsion must be obtained simultane-
ously [12], e.g. through multi-objective nanophotonic op-
timization [16]. While we have chosen to concentrate on
transmissive bigratings that can, in principle, combine
restoring forces and torques, our final design is stable
with an anti-restoring force because it instead balances
rotation-translation cross coupling.
Given that purely
reflective gratings with only first-order diffraction can-
not simultaneously experience restoring forces and restor-
ing torques, asymptotic stability requires engineering the
coupling terms to generate negative Jacobian eigenvalues.
Alternatively, metasurfaces or combinations of gratings
are capable of bypassing the anti-restoring behavior of
purely reflective gratings, allowing propulsion and stabil-
ity to be optimized in unison.
One significant assumption applied here and in most
of the literature on lightsails [12] is that the sail is a rigid
body. This assumption is clearly unrealistic given that
the sail is tens of nanometers thin, the laser beam is im-
parting nonuniform stresses and that substantial lateral
forces are being generated by restoring/damping mech-
anisms or sail spinning [34].
In the few investigations
of flexible lightsail structures [34, 35], flexible modes are
flagged as an extra source of beam-riding instability. We
are optimistic that such instabilities may also be partly
countered with the relativistic, optical damping proposed
in this manuscript. Regardless, a complete investigation
of flexible-shape beam-riding in the relativistic regime is
needed to determine the viability of various stabilizing
schemes [12].
Thus, with judicious nanophotonic design, laser-driven
sails can experience the full suite of restoring and damp-
ing mechanisms in the relativistic regime.
The effects
are completely integrated into the membrane design at
the fabrication stage and are engaged without external
input. The design has no active elements, no added in-
ternal degrees of freedom and requires no external feed-
back, simplifying the lightsail design criteria and hence
elevating the viability of lightsail missions.

Appendix E: Numerical optimization
Maxwell’s equations for the diffraction grating were
solved using the rigorous coupled-wave analysis method
in the TORCWA Python package [42], with PyTorch [43]
used to perform gradient calculations. We took the inci-
dent plane wave to have TE polarization and λ0 = 1 µm.
When optimizing lightsails aiming for vf = 0.2c, the av-
erage over wavelength in Eq. (14) is performed over the
wavelength range [λ0, 1.22λ0] corresponding to the to-
tal Doppler shift, which amounts to just over 200 nm of
bandwidth. We assumed that the Gaussian-beam spa-
tial intensity variation is much larger than the grating
period, which is reasonable given that the beam width is
of similar size to the macroscopic sail diameter. Hence,
the diffraction efficiencies accurately represent the pro-
portion of scattered power on each cluster of unit cells.

Sec. V, however, our code allows its optimization [36].
In contrast to our previous approach [20], the grating
pitch is optimized instead of wavelength. This choice en-
ables efficient gradient calculation for the figure of merit
(Eq. (14)), which contains an average over wavelength.
Across all parameters, the minimum bound is offset from

were passed to the local optimizer. However, the global
optimization routine does not respect the local optimiza-
tion constraints and sometimes initializes points in the
redundant parameter spaces. The Gaussian-beam width
