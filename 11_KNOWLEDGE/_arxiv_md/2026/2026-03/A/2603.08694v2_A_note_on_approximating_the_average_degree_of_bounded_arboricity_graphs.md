Abstract
Estimating the average degree of graph is a classic problem in sublinear graph algorithm. Eden, Ron,
and Seshadhri (ICALP 2017, SIDMA 2019) gave a simple algorithm for this problem whose running
time depended on the graph arboricity, but the underlying simplicity and associated analysis were buried
inside the main result. Moreover, the description there loses logarithmic factors because of parameter
search. The aim of this note is to give a full presentation of this algorithm, without these losses.
Consider standard access (vertex samples, degree queries, and neighbor queries) to a graph G = (V, E)
of arboricity at most α. Let d denote the average degree of G. We describe an algorithm that gives a
(1 + ε)-approximation to d degree using O(ε−2α/d) queries. For completeness, we modify the algorithm
to get a O(ε−2p
n/d) query algorithm for general graphs.

Introduction
Given a simple graph G = (V, E) with n vertices and m edges, the average degree is denoted d := 2m/n.
The problem of estimating d in sublinear time goes back to the early days of sublinear graph algorithms.
Feige initiated study on this problem [Fei06], and Goldreich-Ron [GR08] (henceforth GR) obtained the first
sublinear (1 + ε)-approximation algorithm for the average degree. The query complexity (and running time)
were poly(ε−1 log n)
p
n/d, and GR gave a nearly matching Ω(
p
n/d) lower bound. The GR algorithm is
relatively complex, and loses various logarithm and ε factors due to bucketing techniques.
Eden-Ron-Seshadhri [ERS19] (henceforth ERS) gave a significantly simpler algorithm that removes the
bucketing. This algorithm has an easier analysis, and additionally, introduced a connection to the graph
arboricity. ERS proved that the sample complexity of their algorithm is eO(ε−2α/d), where α is a bound
on the graph arboricity.
It is known that α ≤
√
nd, so that yields the eO(ε−2p
n/d) query complexity
bound for general graphs. Unfortunately, the simple algorithm and analysis are buried deep inside the paper
(Section 5.1 of [ERS19] has the details). Chapter 10.3.2.2 of Goldreich’s book [Gol17], Section 3 of Ron’s
survey [Ron19], and Chakrabarty’s lecture notes [Cha21] give nice expositions of the ERS algorithm, albeit
without the arboricity connection and the additional overhead of parameter search.
The aim of this note to give a full presentation of the ERS algorithm with the arboricity dependence, and
explain all the technicalities of the local search. We do not incur any logarithmic factors and get a simple,
streamlined algorithm.
We do not give a comprehensive description of previous work, but it is worth mentioning a number
of results on edge counting and arboricity. Eden-Rosenbaum [ER18] study the problem of sampling uni-
form random edges, and Eden-Ron-Rosenbaum [ERR19] prove the arboricity captures the complexity of
this problems. Tˇetek-Thorup [TT22], Beretta-Tˇetek [BT24], Beretta-Chakrabarty-Seshadhri [BCS26], and
Chanda [Cha26] all study the problem of average degree/edge estimation in variants of the standard adja-
cency list model. We note that Chanda’s algorithms also get the α/d query complexity, albeit with extra
poly(ε−1 log n) factors.

Setup
We consider the adjacency list query model (refer to Chap. 10 of [Gol17]). The graph G is accessed via the
following queries. (1) Vertex query: get a uar v ∈V , (2) Degree query: for any vertex v ∈V , returns v’s
degree dv; and (3) Neighbor query: for any vertex v ∈V , return a uar neighbor of v. The standard model
often includes pair queries, but we do not need this query in our algorithms.
A subtlety pointed out by Beretta-Tˇetek [BT24] and subsequently expanded upon by Beretta-Chakrabarty-
Seshadhri [BCS26] is that the knowledge of n can make a difference. In our setting, we will assume that n
is not known.
We introduce the concept of graph arboricity (Pg. 52 of [Die10]).
Definition 1.1 (Arboricity). The arboricity of a graph G, denote α(G), is the minimum number of forests
required to cover the edge set of G.
The arboricity has played an important role in subgraph counting algorithms, since the seminal work
of Chiba-Nishizeki [CN85]. A closely related quantity called the graph degeneracy is a 2-approximation to
the graph arboricity. The class of bounded degeneracy graphs is rich and contains all minor-closed families.
Refer to the short survey of Seshadhri [Ses23] for more details.

The general case, for unknown arboricity
When the arboricity is unknown (or the case for general graphs), ERS as defined will not work. For this case,
we need to assume that n is known. Observe that the input α is only used to set the threshold τ; naively,
one could initialize τ to n. A direct application of Theorem 1.5 would lead to a O(ε−2n/d) complexity. On
the other hand, the “correct” bound according to Lemma 1.4 would be O(ε−2√m/d) = O(ε−2p
n/d).
