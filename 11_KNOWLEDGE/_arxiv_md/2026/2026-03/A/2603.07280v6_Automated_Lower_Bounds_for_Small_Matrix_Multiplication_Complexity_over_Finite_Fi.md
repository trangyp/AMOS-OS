Automated Lower Bounds for Small Matrix Multiplication
Complexity over Finite Fields
Chengu Wang
wangchengu@gmail.com
Abstract
We develop an automated framework for proving lower bounds on the bilinear complexity
of matrix multiplication over finite fields. Our approach systematically combines orbit classifi-
cation of the restricted first matrix and dynamic programming over these orbits with recursive
substitution strategies, culminating in efficiently verifiable proof certificates.
Using this framework, we obtain several new lower bounds for various small matrix formats.
Most notably, we prove that the bilinear complexity of multiplying two 3 × 3 matrices over F2
is at least 20, improving upon the longstanding lower bound of 19 (Bl¨aser 2003). Our search
program finds the proof in under an hour on a laptop, and the resulting certificate verifies in
seconds.

Introduction
Matrix multiplication is a central problem in algebraic complexity theory. Strassen [15] showed
that two 2 × 2 matrices can be multiplied using seven scalar multiplications instead of the obvious
eight, and the matrix multiplication complexity has been studied intensively ever since.
In the bilinear and non-commutative setting, one asks for the minimum number of scalar products
needed to express the entries of the product of two matrices as bilinear forms in the input entries.
Equivalently, one may ask for the tensor rank of the corresponding matrix multiplication tensor. In
a ground field K, the matrix multiplication tensor ⟨l, m, n⟩can be viewed naturally as an element
of the tensor product space Kl×m ⊗Km×n ⊗Kn×l (or its duals). These viewpoints coincide, and
we denote the tensor rank of this common quantity by R(⟨l, m, n⟩).
Small matrix formats play a particularly important role. On the algorithmic side, efficient bilinear
algorithms for small formats can be composed recursively to obtain algorithms for larger matrices.
On the lower-bound side, small formats are among the few settings where one can still hope to
obtain exact ranks or nearly sharp bounds, making them a natural laboratory for understanding
the strengths and limitations of current methods. Among these, the tensor ⟨3, 3, 3⟩is especially
prominent: it is the smallest square format for which the best-known upper and lower bounds have
remained separated.

Our Lower Bounds
We prove that the tensor rank of the matrix multiplication tensor for two 3 × 3 matrices is at
least 20 over F2; equivalently, any bilinear algorithm for multiplying two 3 × 3 matrices over F2
must use at least 20 scalar multiplications. This improves upon the longstanding lower bound of
19 established by Bl¨aser in 2003 [3].

Theorem 1. R(⟨3, 3, 3⟩) ≥20 over F2.
To achieve this, we develop an automated framework designed to prove lower bounds for small ma-
trix multiplication tensors over finite fields. Our framework computes lower bounds by performing
dynamic programming on restricted first matrices with four lower-bound techniques—including the
substitution method via backtracking search—and produces proof certificates that can be indepen-

Some sets of restrictions are equivalent because they either define the same linear subspace or
are equivalent under the symmetries defined above (sandwich, cyclic, transpose). For example, the
restriction sets {a0,0 = 0, a0,1 = 0} and {a0,0 = 0, a0,0+a0,1 = 0} are equivalent because they define
the exact same subspace. The restrictions {a0,j = 0 : 0 ≤j < m} and {a1,j = 0 : 0 ≤j < m} are
equivalent under the sandwich symmetry, as we can simply permute the rows of A by a non-singular
matrix P.
Our goal is to classify the orbits of these subspaces in Fl×m
2
under the (extended) bimodule action.
When dealing with square formats (l = m = n), the corresponding action group is G = (GL(l, F2)×
GL(m, F2)) ⋊C2. For L ∈GL(l, F2) and R ∈GL(m, F2), the action on a subspace S ⊆Fl×m
2
is
defined as (L, R, 0) · S = {LMR | M ∈S}, and when transposed, (L, R, 1)· S = {LMT R | M ∈S}.
If the matrices are not all square, the transpose operation is invalid, simplifying the action group
to G = GL(l, F2) × GL(m, F2).
Without the transpose action, equivalence of two d-dimensional subspaces is exactly the tensor
isomorphism (TI) problem on l × m × d tensors: L acts on the first dimension, R on the second,
and linear combinations of the restrictions on the third. TI sits above Graph Isomorphism and Code
Equivalence in the complexity hierarchy (GI ≤P CodeEq ≤P TI), lies in NP ∩coAM, and is the
defining problem of the complexity class TI introduced by Grochow and Qiao [7]. No polynomial-
time algorithm is known, and the best general upper bound for TI over Fq is q e
O(n3/2) [8].
Any subspace can be represented by a set of restrictions in the reduced row echelon form (RREF)
via Gauss-Jordan elimination. The RREF naturally serves as the lexicographically minimal form
for a subspace (depending on the chosen lexicographical ordering of the variables, possibly after
reversing row or column orders).

a0,0 + a1,0, a0,1 + a1,1, and a0,0 + a0,1 + a1,0 + a1,1 by the sandwich symmetry.
If a0,1 + a1,0 appears as uλ at least once, setting a0,1 + a1,0 = 0 reduces A to Orbit 8. This yields
R(T9) ≥R(T8) + 1 ≥6 + 1 = 7. The same argument applies to a0,0 + a1,1, a0,0 + a0,1 + a1,0,
a0,0 + a0,1 + a1,1, a0,0 + a1,0 + a1,1, a0,1 + a1,0 + a1,1 by the sandwich symmetry.
These exhaust all the nonzero cases for uλ. Therefore, R(⟨2, 2, 2⟩) = R(T9) ≥7.
5
Rank Lower Bound Techniques
In this section, we formally describe the four lower bound techniques used in the dynamic program-
ming process.
5.1
Flattening
The tensor rank of a order-3 tensor is trivially bounded below by its matrix rank under any
flattening. We flatten the tensor along its three bipartitions (grouping dimensions A and B, B and
C, and C and A) and compute the respective matrix ranks. We then take the maximum of these
three ranks as our flattening lower bound.
5.2
Forced Product
This technique systematically applies Hopcroft and Kerr’s method (Lemma 2) to strip rank-1 terms
from the decomposition. We illustrate this general algorithm using Orbit 4 as an example.
First, we group the tensor along the C factor into t terms. For T4, this yields four terms (t = 4):
(a0,1 ⊗b0,0 + a1,1 ⊗b1,0) ⊗c0,1, (a0,1 ⊗b0,1 + a1,1 ⊗b1,1) ⊗c1,1, a0,1 ⊗b1,0 ⊗c0,0, and a0,1 ⊗b1,1 ⊗c1,0.
Next, we identify the terms whose A ⊗B components have a matrix rank of exactly 1, meaning
they can be expressed as a single product. In our example, the first two terms have rank 2, while
the third (a0,1 ⊗b1,0) and fourth (a0,1 ⊗b1,1) have rank 1.
We greedily choose a maximal linearly independent subset of these rank-1 terms, denoting the
size of this subset by s. In T4, the two rank-1 terms are independent, so we select both (s = 2).
By Lemma 2, any minimal bilinear algorithm computing this tensor can be assumed to explicitly
compute these s single products.
We project these s terms out of the tensor. Since the exact C-components of these terms in the
11


## Page 12


decomposition are unknown, we must introduce arbitrary coefficients for the remaining t −s basis
elements of C. Over F2, this yields s(t −s) unknown binary coefficients. We enumerate all 2s(t−s)
possible coefficient assignments, compute the flattening rank lower bound for each resulting residual
tensor, and take the minimum across all cases. Because this branching step is computationally
expensive, we skip this Forced Product technique entirely if s(t −s) is too large. Finally, we repeat
this entire process for all three cyclic permutations of the tensor and take the maximum of the
resulting ranks.
In practice, we skip this technique if s(t −s) ≥32. To accelerate the process, the 2s(t−s) cases can
be enumerated in parallel, even on the GPU.
5.3
Degenerate Reduction
If the restriction subspace Y is contained within the subspace X, then R(TX) ≥R(TY ). Thus,
any lower bound established for the heavily restricted subspace Y provides a lower bound for the
looser subspace X.
5.4
Substitution with Backtracking
Substitution is a powerful inductive technique, used extensively by Pan, Hopcroft, Kerr, and Bl¨aser.
The core idea is to assume the existence of a decomposition of length r, T = Pr
i=1 ui ⊗vi ⊗wi,
and apply a carefully chosen linear functional to one factor space (e.g., setting a specific linear
combination of A-variables to zero) so that one or more rank-1 terms vanish. By analyzing the
rank of the residual tensor, we can conclude an inequality of the form R(T) ≥R(T ′)+(rank drop).
While the substitution for Orbit 6 was constructed manually in Section 4, we present an automated
backtracking algorithm to systematically discover these substitution sequences.
First, we establish a baseline lower bound, denoted known lower bound, using the previous three
techniques. We enumerate all possible valid evaluations (non-zero linear forms) for the A-components
uλ in the decomposition. By setting a subset of these A-components to zero, we algebraically reduce
the tensor to another, more restricted orbit.
To minimize redundant branching, we only consider canonical A-components. For example, under
the restriction set {a0,1 + a1,0 = 0, a0,0 + a0,1 + a1,1 = 0} in Orbit 6, many linear forms evaluate to
the same value, like a0,1 and a1,0. We select the lexicographically minimal representative from each
equivalence class. Specifically, a canonical A-component must not contain the variable ai,j if (i, j)
corresponds to the leading pivot (most significant bit) of any restriction in the RREF restriction
set. In Orbit 6, (1, 0) is the leading pivot of the restriction a0,1 + a1,0 = 0, and (1, 1) is the leading
pivot of the restriction a0,0+a0,1+a1,1 = 0. Thus, canonical A-components must be formed strictly
from the remaining free variables {a0,0, a0,1}. The valid non-zero canonical A-components are a0,0,
a0,1, and a0,0 + a0,1.

5.4.1
Optimizations
Algorithm 2 is sufficient for small formats like ⟨2, 2, 2⟩, but requires significant optimization to scale
to larger matrices.
Multi-threading:
Because the backtracking for an orbit depends strictly on the lower bounds of
orbits with more restrictions, we process the orbits in order from most restricted to unconstrained.
For each number of restrictions, we process the orbits with that number of restrictions in parallel.
Also, the a-component enumeration loop at the first depth of the backtracking tree is parallelized.
Symmetry Orbit Hash Map:
In Algorithm 2, identifying the reduced orbit (Line 6) requires
enumerating all full-rank matrices P, Q−1, and transpositions, which is a major computational
bottleneck. We optimize this by precomputing a hash map for each orbit that stores its right-
multiplied and transposed configurations, just like the idea used in Algorithm 1.
During the
search, we only need to enumerate the left-multiplication matrices P, yielding a roughly square-

## Page 15


root speedup in lookup time. To avoid lock contention during parallel inserts, the hash map is
sharded (e.g., into 997 shards) using fine-grained mutex locks.
Thread-Local Cache:
Even with the optimized map, enumerating P remains expensive. We
introduce a thread-local cache for each orbit that maps sub a components directly to their previously
computed lower bounds. To limit the memory usage, when a local cache exceeds a threshold (e.g.
107 elements), we randomly evict half of its entries.
Incremental Target Strategy:
Rather than immediately searching for the ultimate target
bound (e.g., 4 to 6 in the example), we iteratively set the target lower bound to the best-known
lower bound plus one (e.g., 4 to 5, then 5 to 6). This incremental approach makes each step easier
and increases the chance of pruning.
Step Limits:
To prevent very slow search on difficult branches, we enforce a strict step limit. If
the backtracking algorithm exceeds this limit, the entire backtracking search is aborted.
6
Proof Certificates and Verification
To ensure our computational results are quickly verifiable, our framework generates a formal proof
certificate for every established lower bound. For each restriction orbit, the certificate records the
specific technique successfully used to establish its lower bound.
Depending on the technique, we record the following minimal information required for a verifier
program to reconstruct the proof quickly:
• Flattening: Nothing is recorded. It runs fast.
• Forced Product: We record the cyclic permutation index (in {0, 1, 2}) under which the
rank-1 terms were identified and projected out.
• Degenerate Reduction: We record the additional restrictions (the set difference Y \ X)
that project the current orbit X into the strictly smaller subspace Y .
• Substitution with Backtracking: Because full backtracking trees are prohibitively large,
we optimize the trade-off between certificate size and verification time. Specifically, at the
point a target bound is successfully reached (Line 9 in Algorithm 2), we record:
1. the size of the sequence a components;
2. the specific index set of the sub a components within a components;
3. the exact symmetry transformation (the base change matrices P, Q−1, and the transpose
Boolean flag) mapping the substituted residual tensor to a known canonical orbit.

Since the lower bound proof for an orbit only depends on orbits with more restrictions, we verify
the certificates from the most restricted orbits to the general one. We accelerate this procedure by
parallelizing the verification across all orbits with the same number of restrictions.

Applications
Using the automated framework, we established several tensor rank lower bounds over F2.
7.1
R(⟨3, 3, 3⟩) ≥20 over F2
Using a backtracking step limit of 107, our search algorithm found the proof in 40 minutes on an
Apple MacBook Air M4 with 8 cores and 16 GB of memory. The generated proof certificate is
approximately 32 MiB in size and can be verified on the same hardware in about 3 seconds.
7.2
R(⟨2, 3, 4⟩) ≥19 over F2
By symmetry, R(⟨2, 3, 4⟩) = R(⟨3, 2, 4⟩). Our program runs on the ⟨3, 2, 4⟩format. With a back-
tracking step limit of 107, the search phase took 110 minutes on the MacBook Air.
The resulting proof certificate is under 100 KiB. Because the proof uses the Forced Product tech-
nique at large scale, verification takes roughly 110 minutes on the same machine using only the
CPU. Although verification takes as long as the search, it remains valuable: the verifier is much
shorter and simpler than the search code, and so is much easier to audit. The verification can be
accelerated by an NVIDIA L20 GPU to approximately 1 minute.
7.3
R(⟨3, 3, 4⟩) ≥25 over F2
For this larger format, we increased the backtracking step limit to 108. The search was run on a
cloud server equipped with 64 CPU cores and 128 GB of memory, taking 4.6 days to complete (at a
compute cost of roughly 52 USD∗). With restriction subspaces of dimension greater than or equal
to 4, we set the backtracking thread-local cache threshold to 107. With subspaces of dimension less
than 4, we set the threshold to 3 × 106 to avoid OOM.
The proof certificate is approximately 600 MiB. Verification takes roughly 70 minutes on 192 CPUs
(1 USD†), or just 32 minutes using an NVIDIA L20 GPU.

Conclusion and Open Problems
In this paper, we presented an automated framework for establishing lower bounds on the bilinear
complexity of small matrix multiplication over F2. By systematically combining restriction-subspace
orbit classification and dynamic programming on the orbits with four lower-bound techniques, we
obtained several new bounds. Most notably, our computer search program successfully proved that
R(⟨3, 3, 3⟩) ≥20 over F2, improving upon a longstanding lower bound. This demonstrates that
computational methods can effectively navigate the combinatorial explosion inherent in tensor rank
decompositions when the search space is rigorously pruned using algebraic symmetries and canonical
forms. Furthermore, this framework also applies to the multiplication of structured matrices, such
as triangular, symmetric, and skew-symmetric matrices.
Several natural directions for future research remain open. First, while the results presented here
focus on F2, the underlying methodology can be adapted for other finite fields. We reproduce some
lower bounds over F3 without improving them. Discovering new lower bounds over other finite
fields is a problem we leave for future work.
Second, although we successfully evaluated formats up to ⟨3, 4, 4⟩, scaling the search to ⟨4, 4, 4⟩
remains out of reach due to the huge number (more than 1011) of orbits. Scaling these methods
further will likely require stronger theoretical constraints or more aggressive symmetry-breaking to
compress the search space.
Finally, the exact tensor rank of ⟨3, 3, 3⟩over F2 is still an open question, currently constrained
between our new lower bound of 20 and the known upper bound of 23. Closing this gap completely
remains a compelling challenge for future computational algebraic complexity research.
