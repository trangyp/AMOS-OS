## Page 2


High entropy alloys (HEAs) are multi-principal-element alloys designed for tailorable mechanical performance and have been
attracting significant engineering interest, yet their fundamental behaviour under extreme dynamic conditions, such as shock
loading, remains unexplored. Here, we report laser-shock experiments on two different types of ∼1-µm-thick HEA microfilms,
CuPdAgPtAu and CrFeCoNiCuMo, on ∼25-µm-thick black-Kapton ablator driven by a high intensity laser pulse (532 nm, 5 ns,
16 J, ∼0.5-mm diameter focal spot) and probed by an X-ray free electron laser (XFEL) pulse (12 keV, 7 fs). Time-resolved
X-ray diffraction (XRD) shows the formation of a transient phase with a lattice compression up to ∼5.1% of the CuPdAgPtAu
HEA along the (111) plane; this transient compressed phase existed for ∼0.3 ns. The impedance matching Hugoniot analysis
estimated a shock pressure of 55±6 GPa in the HEA film, while Au- and Fe-based equations of state (EoS) modelling predict
80 GPa (0.8 MBar) at the free HEA surface. The free HEA surface reached maximum velocities of ≈5 km/s as recorded from
in situ monitoring with the velocity interferometry system for any reflector (VISAR) imaging. These initial HEA results show the
suitability of HEA sample preparation and XFEL-based XRD characterisation under extreme shock loading, and are promising
for experimental determination of the EoS of this emerging class of materials (beamtime proposal No.: 2024A8503 for a 6-hour
preliminary experiment).

2 Experimental: materials, methods, conditions
2.1 High Entropy Alloy (HEA)
High entropy alloys (HEAs) are new alloy materials that were theorized23 then realized24 around the early 2000s. Unlike
traditional alloys, HEAs are composed of five or more primary elements, each with atomic concentrations ranging from 5%
to 35%24,25. The elements used are usually 3d transition metals similar atomic size that leads to the tendency to form a
solid solution when the configuration entropy is met.
HEAs are versatile due to their potential combinations. This opens
the door for a wide range of applications such as hydrogen storage26,27, biomaterials28, functional coatings29–31, nuclear
applications32,33, and more. With the potential of applying HEAs under harsh environments, it is important to understand the
metamorphism of such materials under extreme conditions. Two different HEAs are selected for this experiment, CuPdAgPtAu
(Au-HEA) and CrFeCoNiCuMo (Fe-HEA), due to their unit cell structures, range of lattice parameter differences between
the constituent elements, and atomic size differences between the constituent elements. The measured lattice parameters
of Au-HEA’s constituent metals, determined with Cu-Kα 8.04 keV/1.54056Å line34, are 4.08Å (Au, fcc), 4.09Å (Ag, fcc),
3.62Å (Cu, fcc), 3.89Å (Pd, fcc), and 3.90Å (Pt, fcc); and 2.51Å (Co, hexagonal structure(hex)), 2.88Å (Cr, body-centre
cubic(bcc)), 2.87Å (Fe, bcc), 3.52Å (Ni, fcc), and 3.15Å (Mo, bcc) for Fe-HEA35. Both alloys have face-centered cubic (FCC)
structure, with a theoretically predicted lattice parameter of a = 3.92Å36 for Au-HEA and a = 3.56Å36 for Fe-HEA (3.59Å for

diameter dx = 27.9 ± 1.9 µm (±σ standard deviation) at FWHM and vertical dy = 18.9 ± 1.2 µm without considering the

3.1.1 XRD of AgAuCuPdPt under high pressure
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are known for their exceptional structural stability under applied stress, owing to sluggish atomic
diffusion and nanoscale lattice distortions. These properties result in high strength and ductility, making HEAs well-suited for
demanding applications23. Despite this stability, HEAs may still undergo various nanostructural changes under uniaxial pressure,
including phase transitions, lattice parameter shifts, amorphization, and dislocation generation. The structural evolution of the
AgAuCuPdPt HEA under shock compression is presented in Fig. 3(b). Prior to compression, the CuPdAgPtAu (Au-HEA)
exhibits a face-centered cubic (fcc) crystal structure with a lattice parameter of a = 3.94Å, as confirmed by peak fitting using
the Pawley method. The nature of shock-induced structural evolution depends on the magnitude of the applied stress. If the
material is compressed within the elastic regime, the changes are expected to be reversible. However, stresses exceeding the
elastic limit can lead to irreversible deformation and permanent micro-structural modifications.
The high-pressure compressed AgAuCuPdPt HEA exhibits two trends: compression of initial phase and formation of a
transient phase. During the initial phase of compression, up to 4.0 ns, the lattice parameter remains unchanged, and the XRD
pattern shows a single set of Bragg peaks corresponding to the original cubic fcc structure with a lattice parameter of a = 3.94Å.
At 4.0 ns, a second set of Bragg peaks emerges in the XRD data, indicating the formation of a new cubic fcc phase with a
smaller lattice parameter of a = 3.69Å. From this point onward, two distinct cubic fcc phases coexist: the original phase with

4.2 Transient phase of HEA
We propose that the observed and unexpected peak splitting (Fig. 3) indicates that, under a high pressurecompression, the initial
Au-HEA phase temporarily evolves into two distinct HEA phases that coexist for a brief period of approximately 0.6 ns. These
include the original phase with a larger lattice parameter and a newly formed intermediate phase, which appears to be highly
metastable and disappears shortly after the pressure is removed. The formation of this metastable phase can be rationalized in
terms of thermodynamic phase stability under non-equilibrium conditions.
The single-phase AgAuCuPdPt high-entropy alloy solid solution forms as a result of a negative Gibbs free energy of mixing
∆Gsol, which is enabled by the high configurational entropy ∆Ssol associated with its multi-component nature. This relationship
is governed by the thermodynamic equation:
∆Gsol = ∆Hsol −T∆Ssol,
(1)
where ∆Gsol is the Gibbs free energy change of the solution (in this case, the AgAuCuPdPt HEA), ∆Hsol is the enthalpy of
mixing, T is the absolute temperature, and ∆Ssol is the configurational entropy. The increase in entropy, ∆Ssol, helps suppress
the formation of competing intermetallic compounds (e.g., A+B, B+C, A+C, etc.) and instead promotes the stabilization of a
single-phase random solid solution, ABCDE-solution62,63.
The tendency for solid solution formation in multi-component alloys can be quantified using the parameter Ω, defined as:
Ω= (T∆Ssol)
|∆Hsol| .
(2)
When Ω> 1, the formation of a random solid solution is thermodynamically favored over the formation of intermetallic phases.
The stability of a solid solution, such as the AgAuCuPdPt, can be disrupted by changes in the enthalpy of mixing, ∆Hsol. The
general expression for the change in enthalpy is:
∆Hsol = ∆Usol +∆(pV),
(3)
where ∆Usol is the change in internal energy, p is the pressure, V is the volume, and ∆(pV) represents the pressure-volume
work. Although heat input, ∆Q, can also affect the system’s energy, in shock compression scenarios, the dominant contributions
typically arise from changes in internal energy and volume due to the sudden application of the high pressure.
Under ambient conditions, the AgAuCuPdPt satisfies the criterion Ω> 1, indicating that the entropy term T∆Ssol dominates
over |∆Hsol|, thus favoring a stable single-phase solid solution. However, under dynamic conditions such as high pressure

compression, this balance may shift. The rapid increase in pressure and resulting compression of the lattice can significantly
raise ∆Hsol, potentially lowering Ωbelow 1. As a result, the thermodynamic driving force for single-phase stability may be lost,
and the material can become metastable or unstable, allowing for the nucleation of new intermediate phases.
X-ray diffraction analysis reveals that the lattice parameter of the AgAuCuPdPt decreases under compression, indicating
a rapid structural response to the applied stress. At 4.0 ns after the onset of the high pressure, the diffraction pattern shows
the emergence of a second set of peaks, signifying the coexistence of two distinct phases. This observation suggests that the
pressure-induced compression not only reduces the unit cell volume of the original phase but also drives the formation of a new,
transient or metastable phase. The resulting peak splitting provides direct evidence of this structural transformation, likely
triggered by the destabilization of the single-phase solid solution as the enthalpy of mixing ∆Hsol increases under extreme
loading conditions.
The time evolution of this transient phase requires experimental observation by XRD over a longer time span as well as
theoretical predictions of composition to be experimentally verified in future experiments.
4.3 HEA-ablation pressure, velocity at the critical surface of Kapton
Let us estimate laser ablation conditions for the pulse energy Ep = 16 J, focal spot size area Sfoc = 1.735×10−3 cm2 for the laser
wavelength of λ = 532 nm at the average intensity I = 1.84×1012 W/cm2; the cyclic frequency ω = 3.54×1015 s−1 defines
the critical density nc = 3.94×1021 cm−3. Kapton (containing atoms C, O, N, H) has an average ion mass, Mav ≈2×10−23 g
(the mass number A = 12, taken arbitrarily ). The critical mass density (assuming single ionisation per atom) then is
ρc = Mav ×nc = 8×10−2 g/cm3.
Absorption in ns regime occurs in the vicinity of the critical density. Assuming high absorption, fast energy equilibration of
electrons and ions and quasi-stationarity the laser energy flow (intensity) transforms into energy flow of heated ions: I ≈ρcv3
i ,
here vi is the velocity of ions. Taking the above numbers, one gets vi = 6×106 cm/s. The pressure at the critical surface driving
heat wave gradually transformed into the shock wave then estimates as P ≈ρcv2
i = 3×1012 erg/cm3 ≡300 GPa ≡3 Mbar.
The pressure at the shock wave front propagating through the Kapton gradually decreases due to the dissipation, energy
losses on ablation and on expansion. These losses are easily taken even from 1D hydrodynamic code. Hence pressure at
the shock wave front entering HEA is much smaller. Ways to improve the estimates: account for absorption coefficient < 1,
ionisation rate (might be more than one), average ion’s mass. The 16 J per pulse is a huge energy, which is a few times larger
than necessary for ablation of the whole Kapton in the focal volume.
The pressure at the shock wave front propagating through the Kapton gradually decreases due to the heat conduction
perpendicular to the laser beam direction (small), ionisation, bond breaking, energy losses on expansion. The volume of Kapton
Sfoc ×25 µm contains Nkap = 4.9×10−6 cm3 ×1023 ∼4.9×1017 atoms. Sum of losses per atom is εloss = εcoh +εion +εexp,
here the cohesion energy εcoh ≈4 eV/atom, ionisation εion ≈12 eV/atom, expansion εexp ≈10 eV/atom, i.e., εloss = 26 eV/atom.
Energy spent for the ablation of Kapton volume affected by laser equals to ∼2 J. Therefore, at the used energy of 16 J, the
Kapton should be burned through.

5 Conclusions and Outlook
Formation of a transient phase of FCC lattice in Au-HEA was detected at ∼55 GPa within short 4-4.2 ns time window
after laser-induced shock in black-Kapton ablator of 25 µm thickness using XFEL SACLA BL3 beamline. The meta-stable
(transient) “X”-phase is hypothesised based on an increase of ∆Hsol (due to pressure; Eqn. 3), resulting in ∆Gsol to increase as
well. It is probable that for some composition “X” the condition ∆Gsol < ∆GX is no longer satisfied for HEAABCDE at high
pressures. After the pressure is removed, the condition ∆Gsol < ∆GX is restored. The intermediate phase is stable as long as
pressure is applied.
The shock-compression conditions were analysed using Hugonoit impedance matching, numerical ab initio modelling with
EoS of related materials, and analytical ablation pressure estimates for the theoretically achievable highest pressures.
This hypothesis of transient “X” phase will be further explored with a higher temporal resolution and over longer time
span. In the current introductory (6 hours) beamline experiment, longer delay times were not tested and are planned for the
full proposal. Further improvements of thin-film HEA samples via surface polishing of Kapton and/or in situ imidisation of
mirror-flat kapton precursor resist is planned to achieve a few-nm min-max roughness. This is expected to develop methodology
when the impedance matching Hugonoit analysis will become quantitative even for few-micrometer thick HEA films. Such
technique would become highly valuable for characterisation of fast expanding family of HEAs subjected to high pressures.

discussed in a similar experiment for gold64.
6.1 Geometry and choice of ablator
Selection of ablator was based on time-space constraints shown schematically in Fig A3. A black Kapton ablator of 25 µm thickness allowed
to apply compression for long enough time, which was additionally limited by a relatively small 1 µm thickness of HEA films. In thin films,
a back reflected shock from HEA-air interface has π phase reversal and reduces initial compression wave. We chose the largest diameter laser
beam 470 µm in order to simplify overlap the volume of shocked film and X-ray which was ∼30 µm in diameter on front surface of HEA.
Another constraint on the ablator thickness is due to finite rise time of laser pulse to the intensity maximum (plateau) tup. This is
important in ablators, e.g., Si, where elastic shock wave has high velocity in a low-pressure region uup (during tup). The shock wave launched
at the intensity maximum (after rise time) with velocity utop defines the minimum thickness of ablator: tmin =
uuptup
utop−uup ×utop. For Si ablator,
tmin ≈20 µm at 110 GPa. Due to a high longitudinal sound wave velocity cL (proportional to the elastic wave) in Si along ⟨100⟩, ⟨110⟩,
⟨111⟩(slow-to-fast), a single shock wave emerges into Si only above 100 GPa at speeds > 9.5 km/s66. The multi-wave structure precludes an
ideal single shock compression in samples when Si is employed as an ablator below ∼100 GPa67.
