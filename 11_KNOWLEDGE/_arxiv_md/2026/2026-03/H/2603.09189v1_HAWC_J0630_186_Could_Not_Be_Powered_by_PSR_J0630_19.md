ABSTRACT
3HWC J0630+186 is one of the very-high-energy gamma-ray sources listed in the third High-Altitude
Water Cherenkov (HAWC) catalog; however, its origin remains unidentified. The only plausible coun-
terpart is PSR J0630+19, which is offset from the center of 3HWC J0630+186. Several TeV halos
around pulsars are currently considered the most dominant TeV-PeV gamma-ray sources. PSR
J0630+19 was first discovered in an Arecibo survey as a normal-period pulsar, but its age and
spin-down luminosity are not available, making it difficult to assess whether it is associated with 3HWC
J0630+186. Using awarded observing time on the Five-hundred-meter Aperture Spherical radio Tele-
scope (FAST), we conducted follow-up timing observations of PSR J0630+19 over more than one year.
Through pulsar timing analysis, we obtained a more precise position and derived key parameters.
These parameters indicate that PSR J0630+19 is an old pulsar with a spin-down luminosity too low
to power the very-high-energy emission from 3HWC J0630+186.

1. INTRODUCTION
Discovering the origin of cosmic rays and elucidating their underlying acceleration mechanisms require precise, multi-
wavelength observational data and analysis. The physical nature of numerous unidentified galactic sources detected in
the very-high-energy (≥100GeV) gamma-ray (γ-ray) emissions band remains unclear. As one of the brightest gamma-
ray sources, the Crab Nebula is powered by the ultra-relativistic electron-positron wind emanating from its central
pulsar. It has been extensively observed from GeV to TeV energies (F. Aharonian et al. 2004; S. Funk 2015; J. Aleksi´c
et al. 2015; A. U. Abeysekara et al. 2019; M. Amenomori et al. 2019; H. E. S. S. Collaboration 2020; M. Arakawa et al.
2020; MAGIC Collaboration et al. 2020). The discovery of gamma-ray emissions with energies above PeV ( Lhaaso
Collaboration et al. 2021; Z. Cao et al. 2021) from this source implies that it is a PeV particle accelerator.
The existence of TeV halos around pulsars is now firmly established, defining a new class of gamma-ray sources with
properties distinct from those of supernova remnants and pulsar wind nebulae (PWNe). Their emission is interpreted
as originating from electrons and positrons freely diffusing in the surrounding interstellar medium (e.g. G. Giacinti
et al. (2020); K. Fang (2022); R. Y. Liu et al. (2024)). Extended TeV gamma-ray emission around the nearby Geminga
pulsar was first revealed by the Milagro experiment (A. A. Abdo et al. 2009) and later confirmed by the High Altitude
Water Cherenkov (HAWC) observatory (A. U. Abeysekara et al. 2017a,b). Furthermore, T. Linden et al. (2017) found
a large fraction of 2HWC catalog sources coincident with pulsars whose radio beams are not oriented towards
Earth.
3HWC J0630+186 is one of the new TeV sources reported in the 3HWC catalog (A. Albert et al. 2020), which
notes that a significant fraction of its sources are candidate counterparts to pulsars listed in the ATNF catalog5 (R. N.

To investigate the origin of the very-high-energy γ-ray emissions from 3HWC J0630+186, we used the Five-hundred-
meter Aperture Spherical radio Telescope (FAST), which offers unprecedented sensitivity (R. Nan 2006; P. Jiang
et al. 2019), to conduct follow-up pulsar observations of the nearby source PSR J0630+19. Owing to its unparalleled
sensitivity as the world’s largest single-dish radio telescope, FAST has profoundly advanced pulsar discovery and
timing. This is demonstrated by its key survey projects, including the Commensal Radio Astronomy FAST Survey
(CRAFTS; D. Li et al. (2018)), the Galactic Plane Pulsar Snapshot Survey (GPPS; J. L. Han et al. (2021)), and
the FAST Globular Cluster Pulsar Survey (Z. Pan et al. 2021). Precise follow-up timing observations of pulsars
(e.g., Q.-C. Liu et al. (2024); K. Yang et al. (2025)) enable the measurements of their period derivatives.
These
measurements are crucial as they yield key physical parameters, including the characteristic age and spin-down energy
loss rate of pulsars.
In this paper, we describe the FAST observations and data reduction for PSR J0630+19 in Section 2. Section 3
presents the results of the timing analysis and the derived pulsar parameters. We provide a comprehensive discussion
of these results in Section 4, followed by a concluding summary in Section 5.

2. OBSERVATION AND DATA ANALYSIS
To confirm the pulsar signal reported by J. S. Deneva et al. (2016), we observed PSR J0630+19 with FAST on
September 4 and December 14, 2024, with integration times of 1.0 and 2.0 hours, respectively. We employed the 19-
beam L-band receiver in tracking mode, covering a frequency range of 1.0–1.5 GHz with 4096 channels (P. Jiang et al.
2019). Data from all 19 beams were recorded with a temporal resolution of 49.152 µs and in full Stokes polarization.
We conducted blind periodicity searches using the PulsaR Exploration and Search TOolkit (PRESTO) software package
(S. M. Ransom 2001). Radio frequency interference (RFI) mitigation was performed using routine rfifind provided in
PRESTO. The dispersion measure (DM) search range was 0–500 pc cm−3, where the upper limit significantly exceeds
the DM value reported in J. S. Deneva et al. (2016). Data were de-dispersed using steps determined by DDplan
routine in PRESTO: 0.2 pc cm−3 below 376 pc cm−3 and 0.3 pc cm−3 above. Additionally, Fourier-domain acceleration
search was performed with the parameter zmax = 200.
As a point source, the position and its uncertainties of PSR J0630+19 were given in J. S. Deneva et al. (2016). The
positional uncertainties were reported as 7′.5 in both Right Ascension (R.A.) and Declination (Dec.), larger than
the angular resolution of FAST at 1.4 GHz (∼2′.9). After the radio pulses from J0630+19 were re-discovered and
confirmed, we used the same receiver of FAST in SnapShotDec mode to obtain a more accurate position for PSR

In order to obtain high-precision timing solution for PSR J0630+19, we conducted multiple follow-up observations

20-MHz bandwidths were removed (1000–1020 MHz and 1480–1500 MHz) due to sensitivity loss and phase instability.
Second, the remaining spectral channels were cleaned using a median-filter algorithm. As a result, approximately 10%
to 30% of the frequency channels were zapped for different epoch. Then we use routine pac from PSRCHIVE (A. W.
Hotan et al. 2004) software package to do the polarization calibration. We calculated the pulse time of arrivals (ToAs)
by cross-matching each profile with a noise-free template profile obtained by fitting a set of Gaussian functions to
the integrated profile formed from summing several of the highest-SNR observations. We used the Fourier domain
Markov chain Monte Carlo ToA estimator to calculate ToAs, implemented in routine pat provide by PSRCHIVE
software package. In order to measure the DM in timing procedure, the data of each observation were
divided into 4 frequency channels. We then measured the ToAs in each channel. We use the TEMPO2 (G. B.
Hobbs et al. 2006) software package to get the final timing solution and timing residuals.

3. RESULTS
3.1. Confirmation and Localization of PSR J0630+19
During the two observation epochs on September 4 and December 14, 2024, we successfully detected radio pulses in
beam M01 (as shown in Figure 1), with SNR of ∼16.2σ and ∼39.1σ, respectively. The periods of the two signals
were both ∼1248.54 ms, and the DM values were ∼51.7 pc cm−3, which are consistent with the values reported in
J. S. Deneva et al. (2016).
In our SnapShotDec Mode observation on February 6, 2025, the pulse signals were detected both in the first pointing
of M01, the forth pointing of M01 and the third pointing of M02. We folded the raw data from these three beams
using the same integration time to generate pulse profiles. Assuming that the intensity of pulse profiles remained
constant over a half-hour and based on the coordinates of three beam pointing that detected the pulse signals and the
SNR of the detected pulses (see in Table 2), we calculated a new position for PSR J0630+19, as R.A.=06:30:05.33,
Dec.=+19:35:03.8 (as shown in Fig. 2). The pointing accuracy of the feed receivers is 16′′ (P. Jiang et al. 2019).
3.2. Timing and Properties of PSR J0630+19
We present the timing solution for PSR J0630+19 in Table 3, along with the post-fit timing residuals using four
frequency sub-bands in the right panel of Fig. 3. The measured spin period is P ≈1.25 s, with first period derivative
of ˙P ≈1.23 × 10−17 s s−1. These parameters yield a characteristic age of approximately 1.6 × 109 yr, indicating that
this is an old pulsar. The fitted DM, ∼47.87 pc cm−3, is slightly smaller than the value obtained from our searching
analysis. And the coordinates derived from the timing solution, R.A. = 06:30:05.4770, Dec. = +19:34:32.99, show an
offset of ∼0.51′ from the position we calculated in Section 2.
Fig. 3 shows the 1.6-hour integrated pulse profile of PSR J0630+19, which exhibits a very small duty cycle. Since
the pulse profiles cannot be easily described by Gaussian-like curves, we therefore use the intensity weighted width
(IWW) to define the pulse width, i.e. we treat the pulse profile as the temporal intensity distribution function, and
calculate the standard deviation of time. A correction factor of (8 ln 2)1/2 is applied to the standard deviation when
reporting the final pulse width. This factor ensures that the resulting IWW corresponds to the full width at half
maximum (FWHM) in the case of a Gaussian profile. Using this method, the IWW of the integrated pulse profile is
determined to be 31.41 ms in units of time, with an uncertainty of σIWW = 0.03 ms.

4. DISCUSSION
The “death line” delineates the theoretical boundary beyond which a pulsar’s magnetosphere can no longer sustain
pair-production cascades. It is defined by the condition that the electrostatic potential drop along a magnetic field
line traversing the polar gap is sufficient to generate enough electron-positron pairs to screen the accelerating electric
field (M. A. Ruderman & P. G. Sutherland 1975; K. Chen & M. Ruderman 1993; B. Zhang et al. 2000). Two primary
inner gap models account for the binding of ions at the neutron star surface. The traditional vacuum gap model
(M. A. Ruderman & P. G. Sutherland 1975; D. Bhattacharya et al. 1992), which forms the basis for the classic death
line, assumes that ions are strongly bound to the surface. In contrast, the space-charge-limited flow (SCLF) model (J.
Arons & E. T. Scharlemann 1979; B. Zhang et al. 2000) assumes the free emission of particles from it. Both models
were originally proposed under the postulate that the primary mechanism for generating the high-energy photon seeds,
which ignite the pair-production cascades, is either curvature radiation (CR) or inverse Compton scattering (ICS, B.
Zhang & G. J. Qiao (1996); B. Zhang et al. (1997)). Please note that CR cannot contribute to the primary
photons responsible for the TeV emission. The “death valley” (K. Chen & M. Ruderman 1993) is bounded by
the theoretical death lines of two pulsar models: one with a pure dipole field and the other with an extreme case of a
twisted surface magnetic field, both based on the vacuum gap model.
Fig. 4 presents the P −˙P diagram for the pulsars, with PSR J0630+19 marked by a red star. And several theoretically
predicted death lines are also plotted in the figure. The traditional death line of radio pulsars is equivalent to an spin-
down luminosity ˙E ≈1030 erg s−1 (Q.-D. Wu et al. 2020), and low ˙E cases are usually used to test the pulsar radio
emission models. For example, PSR J2144-3933 is a low ˙E pulsar with period of 8.5 s and beyond the traditional
death line and death valley (M. D. Young et al. 1999). But the ICS in the SCLF model, i.e., ICS-SCLF model
proposed by B. Zhang et al. (2000) can sustain strong pair production for this long-period pulsar. In this work, PSR
J0630+19 is at lower-right of the P −˙P diagram in Fig. 4 with a long period about 1.2 s. The death line of traditional
one (red solid line) defined by M. A. Ruderman & P. G. Sutherland (1975) and those investigated by B. Zhang et al.
(2000) including CR in the vacuum gap model (blue dash-dotted line), CR in the SCLF model (orange
dash-dotted line) are all above the location of PSR J0630+19. But the pulsar lies on the ICS-SCLF
model (green dash-dotted line) derived by B. Zhang et al. (2000).

An “observational limit-line” was proposed by Q.-D. Wu et al. (2020) based on the lowest flux levels detectable
by the FAST telescope, corresponding to a spin-down luminosity of about ˙E ≈1028 erg s−1. This can explain the
radio emissions from low- ˙E pulsars, such as PSR J2144–3933 (M. D. Young et al. 1999; Q.-D. Wu et al. 2020)
and PSR J0211+4235 (Q. D. Wu et al. 2023). An upper limit in the efficiency (ξmax = 0.01) of radio emission was
proposed to correspond to the death line of low- ˙E pulsars (A. Szary et al. 2014). The radio emission efficiency
is defined as the fraction of the rate of loss of rotational energy transformed into radio emission (ξ ≡L
˙E , where L is
the radio luminosity from the integrated radio energy flux). The spin-down power ˙E is derived from pulsar timing
measurements. The radio luminosity L is derived from measuring the radio emission flux and the pulsar distance.
The radio efficiency is different from X-ray (e.g. A. Szary (2013)) or γ-ray (e.g. A. A. Abdo et al. (2013)) ones. A
near-linear inverse correlation between ξ and the spin-down power, ξ ∝˙E−0.9 for normal pulsars was reported by A.
Szary et al. (2014). In this work, the radio luminosity of PSR J0630+19 is L ≈6.5 × 1026 erg s−1 based on the Eq
(2) in the work of A. Szary et al. (2014) with FAST measured mean flux density at 1.25 GHz and the pulsar distance
(J. M. Yao et al. 2017) to the Earth. Combining with the ˙E given in Table 3, we can derive the radio efficiency for
this pulsar as ξ ≈2.6 × 10−3, much lower than the upper limit of ξ for low- ˙E pulsars.

an underestimated distance d and/or an erroneous assumed TeV efficiency. Future distance measurement and pulsar
searching around 3HWC J0630+186 may help to understand the origin of its very high energy emissions.
5. SUMMARY
PSR J0630+19 had been considered the only plausible counterpart to the TeV source 3HWC J0630+186.
We
observed this pulsar utilized FAST from September 4, 2024 to October 4, 2025, to find out if both
the TeV source and the pulsar are related or not.
We detected the pulse period of the pulsar as the
same as that in previous observations reported by Arecibo telescope. Using the FAST observations conducted in the
SnapShotDec Mode, we derived a new, more precise localization for PSR J0630+19. Timing solution obtained by
FAST tracking Mode for PSR J0630+19 showed that it is an old pulsar with spin-down energy luminosity (2.50 ±
0.16) × 1029 erg s−1. The spin-down power and radio luminosity of the pulsar yield a radio efficiency that places it
within the region predicted for the lowest flux levels detectable by the FAST telescope. Adopting the distance to PSR
J0630+19 derived from its DM and the HAWC spectral parameters, we calculated the TeV γ-ray luminosity of 3HWC
J0630+186. This γ-ray luminosity significantly exceeds the spin-down power of PSR J0630+19, consequently ruling
it out as the power source for 3HWC J0630+186. Therefore, the very-high-energy emission most likely originates
from another, yet-unidentified source or physical process. Future multi-wavelength observations targeting the region
of 3HWC J0630+186 will help to understand its true origin.
