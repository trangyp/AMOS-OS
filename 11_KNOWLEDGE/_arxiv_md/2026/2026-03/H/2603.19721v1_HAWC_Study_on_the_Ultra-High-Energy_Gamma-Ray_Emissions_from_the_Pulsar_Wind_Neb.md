ABSTRACT
Multi-TeV gamma-ray emission around eHWC J1850+001 (a source from the first HAWC catalog of
gamma-ray sources emitting above 56 TeV) is spatially coincident with the pulsar wind nebula (PWN)
G32.64+0.53, powered by PSR J1849-0001. The absence of counterparts in radio, optical, and GeV
energy ranges, contrasted with clear detections in X-rays and very-high-energy (VHE) gamma-rays,
is indicative of a non-thermal leptonic origin for the nebula. We apply a systematic analysis pipeline,
including a sophisticated model for the Galactic diffuse emission, to 2860 days of data from the HAWC
Observatory. Our detailed analysis confirms that the ultra-high-energy (UHE) emission originates from
G32.64+0.53, and we measure its spectrum up to 270 TeV with significant emission well beyond 100
TeV. We fit the multi-wavelength observations with a time-dependent leptonic model powered by the
pulsar’s rotational energy, and the results establish the nebula as a leptonic PeV accelerator, capable
of accelerating electrons to a maximum energy of Ecut = 1.5+1.7
−0.6 PeV. The model also constrains the
true age of the system to 26.8 kyr and the nebular magnetic field to a low value of 2.5 µG, supporting
a leptonic PWN origin for the observed UHE emission.

1. INTRODUCTION
Ultra-high-energy (UHE) gamma-ray astronomy is
providing an unprecedented view into the most power-
ful particle accelerators within our Galaxy. The recent
discovery of numerous Galactic sources emitting pho-
tons with energies beyond hundreds of tera-electronvolt
(TeV) has confirmed that these objects can accelerate
particles to peta-electronvolt (PeV) energies, ushering
in the era of UHE astronomy (A. U. Abeysekara et al.
2020; Z. Cao et al. 2021; Z. Cao et al. 2023; A. Albert
et al. 2024).
Pulsar wind nebulae (PWNe) are nebulae inflated by
magnetized, relativistic electron winds, powered by the
immense rotational energy of rapidly spinning neutron
stars. These relativistic electrons generate synchrotron
radiation in the nebula’s magnetic field, seen from radio
to X-rays. These electrons also upscatter ambient pho-
tons (e.g., cosmic microwave background (CMB) pho-
tons) to the very-high-energy (VHE) regime via inverse
Compton scattering (ICS). According to the first cata-
log of the Large High Altitude Air Shower Observatory
(LHAASO), among the 43 UHE sources discovered by
LHAASO (4σ detection at E > 100 TeV), 22 sources are
found to be pulsar-associated, including PWNe and pul-
sar halos (Z. Cao et al. 2023). This suggests that PWNe
are a major class of Galactic accelerators emitting UHE
gamma rays through ICS.
A prime example of such an extreme accelerator is the
PWN powered by PSR J1849-0001, G32.64+0.53, which
might accelerate particles to the PeV regime. The pulsar
itself is highly energetic, with a spin-down luminosity of
˙E = 9.8 × 1036 erg s−1 (G. Hobbs et al. 2004). The
pulsar has a characteristic age of τc = 43 kyr, placing
it in the class of middle-aged pulsars (G. Hobbs et al.
2004; C. Kim et al. 2024). Based on the large hydrogen
column density derived from X-ray observations, E. V.
Gotthelf et al. (2011) obtained a distance of 7 kpc to the
pulsar. This distance is also adopted for the modeling
in this work.
Previous studies using the High Energy Stereoscopic
System (H.E.S.S.) have firmly identified the associated
nebula, HESS J1849-000, as a PWN (E. V. Gotthelf

2. HAWC OBSERVATIONS AND DATA ANALYSIS
2.1. The HAWC Observatory
The HAWC observatory is a wide field-of-view de-
tector sensitive to gamma rays from approximately
300 GeV to hundreds of TeV. It is a particle sampling ar-
ray that detects secondary particles from extensive air
showers (EAS) located at an altitude of 4,100 m near
Pico de Orizaba, Mexico. The main array consists of
300 water Cherenkov detectors (WCDs) designed to de-
tect extensive air showers produced by incident gamma
rays.
Each WCD is equipped with four photomulti-
plier tubes (PMTs) at the bottom, which collect the
Cherenkov light produced as shower particles traverse
the purified water. The timing and charge information
from the PMTs is used to reconstruct the core location,
incoming direction, and energy of the primary gamma
ray. Detailed descriptions of the detector can be found
in A. U. Abeysekara et al. (2023).

2.3. Galactic Diffuse Emission Modeling
The Galactic plane is a bright, complex source of dif-
fuse gamma rays originating from the interaction of cos-
mic rays with interstellar gas and radiation fields. To
properly model the contribution of individual sources
in the ROI, this GDE must be accurately accounted
for. We have adopted a sophisticated, energy-dependent
GDE template generated with the HERMES software
(A. Dundovic et al. 2021).
This GDE template incorporates emission from ICS,
bremsstrahlung, and neutral pion (π0) decay from
cosmic-ray interactions with atomic (HI) and molecu-
lar (H2) hydrogen. The HI and H2 gas maps used as
spatial templates are from N. Ben Bekhti et al. (2016)
and T. M. Dame et al. (2001), respectively. For the π0
decay channel, which dominates in the HAWC energy
range, we have adopted the cross-section calculations
from S. R. Kelner et al. (2006), as they are valid for
proton energies well into the PeV scale. A free scaling
parameter was applied to the GDE template within the
ROI, to allow its overall contribution to be adjusted in
the likelihood fit, accounting for potential local varia-
tions in the cosmic-ray sea density.
2.4. Source Finding Algorithm
With the GDE template established as a baseline, we
have applied an iterative pipeline to search for and char-
acterize individual gamma-ray sources. This process is
adapted from the procedure used for the Boomerang
region analysis by the HAWC collaboration (R. Alfaro
et al. 2024). There is an additional step to test the el-
liptical morphology of the found extended sources com-
pared to R. Alfaro et al. (2024). The pipeline systemat-
ically executes the following steps:
1. Point Source Search —The pipeline begins by identi-
fying the location of the most significant hotspot in the
current residual map. A new test point source is placed
at this location and modeled with a PL spectrum (Equa-
tion 2), where the positional parameters RA, Dec, the
flux normalization (Φ0) and the spectral index (α) are
treated as free parameters. The spectral parameters of
all sources in the current model are also free param-
eters.
If the inclusion of this source results in a TS
value greater than 25, it is added to the source model.
This iterative process of identifying hotspots and adding
sources is repeated until no new location in the ROI
meets the detection threshold.
2. Extension Search —After the point source search is
complete, each source in the model is tested for spatial
extension, proceeding iteratively from the brightest to
the faintest. For each source, its point-like morphology
is temporarily replaced with a spatially extended tem-
plate, typically a radially symmetric Gaussian distribu-
tion, with its spectrum, position, and extension size as
free parameters. We remark that when a morpholog-
ical model is fitted, the point spread function is con-
volved with the morphological model. If the extended
model is favored over the point-source model with a TS
value greater than 16, the source’s morphology is up-
dated to the extended model. Modeling a bright source
as extended can significantly alter the flux distribution
in the region. Therefore, after a source’s morphology is
updated, all other sources in the model are re-evaluated.
Any source whose significance drops below the detection
threshold (TS < 25) is removed from the list. After this
pruning, all parameters of the remaining sources in the
model are refit.
3.
Elliptical Morphology Search —For sources that are
found to be extended in the previous step, we perform
an additional test for elliptical morphology. This step
was not included in the original pipeline presented in
R. Alfaro et al. (2024). The radially symmetric Gaus-
sian morphology is replaced with an elliptical 2D Gaus-
sian model, which includes two additional free parame-
ters: the eccentricity e and the position angle θ (see Ap-
pendix A). The elliptical model is adopted if it is statis-
tically preferred over the symmetric one with a TS > 16.
Detailed descriptions of the spectral and morpholog-
ical models used in this analysis are presented in Ap-
pendix A. The results of this source-finding procedure,
including the composition of the final best-fit model for
the ROI, are presented in Section 3.

3.2. Systematic Uncertainties
In addition to the statistical uncertainties, we have es-
timated the systematic uncertainties for our final model
parameters. These uncertainties account for potential
discrepancies between simulation and data, as well as
known detector biases. The main sources of systemat-
ics considered include the late-light effect, the charge
uncertainty, the detection threshold of PMT, and vari-

deviation observed for each parameter across these sys-
tematic sources.

combined in quadrature to yield the total systematic
uncertainty. The resulting values represent a conserva-
tive uncertainty and are applied symmetrically to the
best-fit parameters, as the second uncertainty term for
each parameter in Table 2, 5.

4. DISCUSSION
In this section, we interpret the new HAWC mea-
surements within the framework of a multi-wavelength,
time-dependent, leptonic model for the pulsar wind neb-
ula powered by the rotation of PSR J1849-0001. This
model assumes a single population of electrons is con-
tinuously injected from the pulsar and loses energy
via synchrotron radiation and ICS (B. M. Gaensler &
P. O. Slane 2006).

PWNe, such as HAWC J2019+368 and HAWC J1809-
1919 (A. Albert et al. 2021, 2024b).

The broadband spectral energy distribution, from X-

leptonic model. The best-fit parameters for this model,
with 68% credible intervals, are summarized in Table 6,
and the resulting fit is shown in Figure 5.
A key result from the model is an electron spectrum
cutoff energy of Ecut = 1.5+1.7
−0.6 PeV, which establishes
HAWC J1849-0000 as a PeV electron accelerator. In the
Klein-Nishina regime, these PeV electrons can produce
gamma rays up to approximately 0.6 PeV via ICS on
CMB photons, by the relation Ee ≃2.15 (Eγ/1 PeV)0.77
(Z. Cao et al. 2021). The model estimates the true age of
the pulsar to be τ ≈26.8 kyr. This value is lower than its
characteristic age of τc = 43 kyr (C. Kim et al. 2024), as
expected when the initial-to-current-period ratio (P0/P)
is non-negligible.
The model also constrains the average nebular mag-
netic field to B(τ) ≈2.5 µG. This low magnetic field
allows ICS to be the dominant cooling mechanism for
the observed UHE gamma-rays (E. V. Gotthelf et al.
2011). Furthermore, the efficiency of converting the pul-
sar’s spin-down power into the relativistic electron pop-
ulation is determined to be η ≈1.2%.
A two-zone emission model, which includes an addi-
tional free parameter for the age fraction of young elec-
trons, was also tested (A. Albert et al. 2024b).
This
model did not provide a statistically significant improve-
ment to the fit. The BIC for the best-fit two-zone model
is larger by ∆BIC = 3.5, indicating a preference for the
simpler one-zone model. Therefore, the current multi-
wavelength data are consistent with emission from a sin-
gle population of electrons. However, future radio ob-
servations to fit the synchrotron emission may reveal
that a two-zone emission model is plausible, a scenario
successfully applied to other sources (A. Albert et al.
2024b).
5. CONCLUSIONS
In this analysis of the pulsar wind nebula HAWC
J1849-0000, which utilized 2860 days of HAWC Observa-
tory data and other multi-wavelength data, the source
is confirmed as a bright and slightly extended source.
Its spectrum, reaching beyond 100 TeV, marks it as one
of the most powerful UHE gamma-ray sources in the
galaxy.
By performing time-dependent leptonic modeling of
the broadband spectral energy distribution, we have suc-
cessfully constrained the key physical parameters of the
system. The PWN is a PeV electron accelerator can-
didate, capable of accelerating leptons to a maximum
energy of Ecut = 1.5+1.7
−0.6 PeV. The model yields a true
age for the system of τ ≈26.8 kyr, a magnetic field of
B(τ) ≈2.5 µG. An efficiency of η ≈1.2% for converting
the pulsar’s spin-down power into relativistic electrons
indicates that the majority of the pulsar’s rotational en-
ergy is not channeled into the electron population re-
sponsible for the observed gamma-ray emission.
Future observations with next-generation instruments
will be essential to further resolve the nebula’s structure
and probe the detailed physics of the extreme accelera-
tion.
