Abstract
This study investigates the interconnectivity of firms and Environmental Justice Orga-
nizations (EJOs) involved in socio-environmental conflicts worldwide, using data from the
Environmental Justice Atlas (EJAtlas). By constructing a multilayer network that links firms,
conflicts, and EJOs, the research applies social network analysis to evaluate the simultaneous
involvement of these actors across multiple disputes. Both projected networks of firms and
EJOs have been analysed by aggregating nodes by categories and countries to reveal structural
differences between global networks of actors. Findings reveal a stark contrast between the
interconnectedness of firms and EJOs. Multinational corporations form a cohesive global net-
work, enabling them to coordinate strategies and exert influence across regions. Conversely,
EJOs are fragmented, often operating in isolated clusters with limited interconnection but
forming a robust, decentralized and self-organized global network. Firms network present a
strong dependence on pertaining conflict category while EJOs network does not depend on
conflict category. This structural difference suggests a risk of systemic and structural coor-
dination for firms towards exploitative expansion while EJOs dynamics seems to be led by a
white blood cells defense-like mechanism. While fragmentation may represents a critical chal-
lenge for social movements, decentralization and self-organization show a more diffuse global
networks supported by a limited number of central hub able to build stronger global alliances
to effectively counter the power dynamics of transnational corporations. By providing robust
evidence of these asymmetrical networks, this research contributes to discuss how structural
differences in global coordination for companies and EJOs directly derives as an emergent
properties depending on the purpose of the network itself, sectorial expansion for firms while
ecosystem preservation for EJOs.

Introduction
Socio-environmental conflicts have become a defining feature of contemporary political ecology and
sustainability transitions, emerging where economic development and resource extraction collide
with the rights and ecological integrity of local communities (Martinez-Alier et al., 2016; Menton
et al., 2020). These conflicts often arise along global commodity chains, where the expansion of ex-
tractive industries (Atapattu, 2018), recently accelerated by the energy transition and the demand
for critical raw materials (IEA, 2021; Dowling and Otero, 2025), or industrial agriculture (Busscher
et al., 2020) requires large-scale infrastructure mega-projects (Stammler and Ivanova, 2016; Orihuela
and Serrano, 2025) and generates uneven distributions of environmental costs and benefits between
territories and populations (Temper et al., 2018; Hickel et al., 2022; Llavero-Pasquina, 2025). Such
disputes are widely conceptualized as ecological distribution conflicts, referring to struggles over
access to natural resources (Jerez et al., 2021) or exposure to environmental harms (Temper et al.,
2018; Martinez-Alier et al., 2016). Over the past decade, the Environmental Justice Atlas (EJAtlas)
has become one of the most comprehensive global databases documenting these conflicts and the
actors involved in them (Martinez-alier, 2015; Temper et al., 2018; Menton et al., 2020). Developed
through a collaborative effort between researchers, activists, and environmental justice organiza-
tions, EJAtlas collects structured information on thousands of environmental conflicts worldwide,
including data on affected communities, corporations, governments, and civil society organizations
involved in each case1. By systematically mapping conflicts across sectors such as mining, fos-
sil fuels, waste management, infrastructure, and industrial agriculture, the database provides an
unprecedented empirical basis for studying the global political ecology of environmental conflicts
(Martinez-Alier et al., 2016; Temper et al., 2018; Martinez-Alier, 2021; Cantoni et al., 2025a).
A central dimension, yet not fully investigated, of these conflicts concerns the role of involved
actors: corporate actors, particularly multinational corporations operating in extractive and infras-
tructure sectors, on one side, and Environmental Justice Organizations (EJOs) reacting to environ-
mental degradation, resource extraction, land dispossession, or violations of community rights and
generating opposition, on the other side. At the same time, individuals who defend environmental
rights and community territories often face severe risks, including intimidation, criminalization, and
violence, illustrating the asymmetry of power between corporate actors and grassroots defenders
(Morreale and Giuliani, 2023) highlighting how environmental conflicts are not merely technical
disputes over resource management but are deeply embedded within broader structures (Aydin
et al., 2017) of global political power and economic governance (Vitali and Battiston, 2014; Carroll
and Carson, 2003). Recent research has therefore begun to analyze environmental conflicts not
only as isolated events but as manifestations of systemic dynamics within global economic and
governance systems (Llavero-Pasquina, 2025; Cantoni et al., 2025b). In this perspective, socio-
environmental conflicts can be understood as nodes within a broader network of relationships (Ay-
din et al., 2017) linking corporations, governments, investors, and civil society organizations across
multiple territories and sectors, i.e. a complex system (Ladyman et al., 2013), a multilayer net-
work (De Domenico, 2022; Aleta et al., 2025), where systemic effects and structural properties may
emerge (De Domenico et al., 2019). These relational structures may reflect the underlying pat-
terns of power concentration (Llavero-Pasquina et al., 2025), that enable certain actors to appear
repeatedly in environmental conflicts across different regions and time periods, systemic corruption
(Luna-Pla and Nicol´as-Carlock, 2020), or unequal exchanges among countries in the global North
and South (Llavero-Pasquina, 2025) .
Despite the growing body of EJAtlas-based research, relatively few studies have examined the

relational architecture of actors involved in environmental conflicts using network approaches (Aydin

et al., 2025), rather than on the structural relationships between the actors participating in them.
However, environmental conflicts are inherently relational phenomena in which multiple actors
interact, cooperate, and oppose each other across different contexts and scales (Martinez-alier,
2015). Therefore, however the EJAtlas has been widely studied there is still a lack of studies on
the network structure and the interconnectivity of firms and EJOs at global level and across all
environmental sectors.
In this context, social network analysis (SNA) provides a powerful methodological framework for
investigating the relational structure of complex socio-ecological systems (Barab´asi, 2011). Network
science conceptualizes social systems as sets of actors connected through relationships, allowing re-
searchers to analyze structural properties such as connectivity, centrality, clustering, and modularity
(Newman, 2006; Blondel et al., 2008; Castellano et al., 2009; Fortunato, 2010). These approaches
have been widely applied to study complex systems ranging from biological networks to economic
systems and social movements, revealing how systemic behaviors often emerge from the structure
of interactions between actors rather than from individual actions alone (De Domenico et al., 2019;
Helbing, 2013).
Starting form the EJAtlas grandfather question ”is there a global EJ movement?” (Martinez-
Alier et al., 2016), in this study we aim to answer: (A) What are the main properties of the global
network both for EJOs and firms? and (B) What are the main structural differences between the
those networks at global level and across sectors?
To analyse the global network of firms and EJOs involved in socio-environmental conflicts, in
this study, we analysed the projected networks - i.e, the projection starting from the bi-partite net-
works, conflicts-company and conflicts-EJOs - of company and EJOs. Through the use of common
centrality degrees Castellano et al. (2009) we detected structural differences between the company
and EJOs global networks. The projected networks have been obtained starting from the hypothesis
of co-engagement of actors in socio-environmental conflicts documented in the EJAtlas. Specifically,
we draw an edge between two actors whenever those two actors co-occurred in the same conflicts.
Findings reveal how the two networks are structurally different. The company network is more
densely connected with many companies with a high degree, i.e., a high number of conflicts where
they are involved and consequently a high number of co-occurrence with other companies, while
the EJOs network is composed by EJOs engaged in fewer number of conflicts with some evident
central hubs represented by a limited number of transnational EJOs. Despite this difference, the
largest connected component (LCC) in the EJOs network connect more than the 80% of the whole
network, while in the corporations network the giant is composed by only the 60% of all the nodes,
revealing how a environmental justice global movement exists although with structural differences
with respect to the corporations network.
The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In section 2 we present the adopted methodology,
discussing both the EJAtlas database and its structural characteristics, the two types of projection,
i.e., via conflict and via actors, and their implication as well as the centrality degree and clustering
algorithm adopted to analyzed the network.
In section 3 we discuss in details the descriptive
statistics and main differences between the two networks, while in section 4 we present a critical
discussion of results, implications and main limitations of the underlying hypothesis.

Methodology
In this study, we apply a network perspective to examine the co-engagement of actors in socio-
environmental conflicts (Aydin et al., 2017) documented in the EJAtlas (Martinez-alier, 2015).
Specifically, we focus on two key categories of actors: companies and environmental justice orga-
nizations. Companies frequently appear as project developers, investors, or operators in environ-
mentally contested activities, particularly in sectors such as mining, fossil fuels, and large-scale
infrastructure. Environmental justice organizations, by contrast, represent networks of civil society
actors—including NGOs, grassroots organizations, and transnational advocacy groups—that mobi-
lize to defend environmental rights, local livelihoods, and ecological integrity (Martinez-Alier, 2021;
Martinez-alier, 2015; Temper et al., 2018; Martinez-Alier et al., 2016). By constructing and analyz-
ing projected networks of corporation–corporation and EJO–EJO co-participation in environmental
conflicts, this paper investigates the structural properties of these two actor groups and compares
their patterns of connectivity, clustering, and community formation. Through this analysis, we seek
to understand whether corporate actors and environmental justice organizations exhibit different
forms of network organization within global environmental conflicts.

The EJAtlas database
The EJAtlas, one of the most comprehensive global databases documenting socio-environmental
conflicts, has been developed through a collaborative process between researchers, activists, and
environmental justice organizations, and it systematically collects, via a crowdsourcing process,
structured information on environmental conflicts worldwide including data on conflict character-
istics, sectors, geographical location, and the actors involved (Martinez-alier, 2015; Martinez-Alier
et al., 2016; Temper et al., 2018; Menton et al., 2020; Martinez-Alier, 2021; Cantoni et al., 2025a).

conservation, reflecting the diversity of ecological distribution conflicts globally Martinez-Alier et al.
(2016); Temper et al. (2018); Menton et al. (2020). The numbers shown here represent the full
working dataset, i.e., the dataset where each conflict has at least one company and one EJO.

4
Discussion
4.1
Is there a global environmental justice movement?
The question of whether a global environmental justice movement exists has been central to the
EJAtlas literature from its own conception (Martinez-Alier et al., 2016). Our findings provide a
nuanced answer to support the existence of a global robust, resilient and emergent EJ movement.
Moreover, while a global EJ movement does emerge structurally, it exhibits a fundamentally different
network organization compared to corporate actors.
From a network perspective, the EJOs network is characterized by a high level of fragmentation
combined with the presence of a large connected component encompassing more than 80% of all the
EJOs. This result suggests the coexistence of two seemingly contradictory dynamics: on the one
hand, EJOs are predominantly embedded in localized clusters, reflecting the place-based nature
of environmental conflicts; on the other hand, a limited number of highly central organizations
act as bridges connecting otherwise disconnected regions.
These actors, often transnational or
international EJOs (see Fig.8), could play a crucial role in sustaining the global coherence of the
movement by facilitating information flows and coordination across geographically distant struggles.
A stress and robustness test should be done in future studies to verify the effective role of these
actors in connecting different disconnected components and clusters of EJOs (Bellingeri et al., 2020).

This structure is consistent with the conceptualization that local struggles are globally inter-
connected through shared narratives, and common struggles in an emergent way (Lindell, 2009;
Vanhaute, 2014). In this sense, the global EJ movement does not resemble neither a coordinated
structure nor a highly clustered core group as, instead, it emerges in the company network (see
Fig.5 and 6), but rather it presents an emergent, decentralized system shaped by relational dynam-
ics across conflicts.
In contrast, the corporations network displays a more cohesive and densely interconnected struc-
ture, with multiple actors exhibiting high degree and forming tightly clustered communities (see
Fig. 5). This reflects the systemic nature of global production networks and transnational corpo-
rate structures (Vitali and Battiston, 2014; Carroll and Carson, 2003), where firms operate across
multiple regions and sectors, often participating in several conflicts simultaneously. Such structural
cohesion suggests a higher capacity for coordination, and strategic alignment among corporate ac-
tors with a strong risk to reproduce worst and illicit practices across different regions and countries.
Further investigation and analysis, both in depth through case studies (Mliless and Larouz, 2022),
sectorial analysis (Avila, 2018) or via structured risk and corruption indicators (Nicol´as-Carlock
and Luna-Pla, 2021b,a; Luna-Pla and Nicol´as-Carlock, 2020; Christiansen, 2025; Williams, 2024)
are needed.
The comparison between the two networks highlights a clear asymmetry of structural power.
While corporations form a relatively integrated and highly clustered global system (see Fig. 5)
according to the belonging sector (see Fig. 6), EJOs’ clusters remain more independent by the
belonging sector. This asymmetry may reflect broader dynamics of ecological distribution con-
flicts (Martinez-Alier, 2021) and unequal exchange (Hickel et al., 2022), where economic power is
concentrated in transnational corporate networks, whereas resistance is often rooted in localized,
and place-based struggles that can be derived and reacted from the widely known NIMBY (Not In
My Backyard) phenomenon or from more structural social phenomena as described by Sebastien
et al. (2019) as enlightening process, i.e., a context-specific dynamics depending on different types
of capital, namely social, knowledge, patrimonial and political. In this sense, it is crucial in future
to conduct case by case analyses to identify the emergence of shared values and common cultures
across the EJ global movements, i.e., a social movement scene, a common identity or a common set
of belief, values and norms (Creasap, 2012).
At the same time, the presence of a large connected component in the EJO network provides
empirical support to the hypothesis of a global EJ movement. However, this movement should
be understood as an emergent property of a complex adaptive system (Ladyman et al., 2013;
De Domenico et al., 2019), rather than as a formally organized global actor. Its structure, indeed, is
shaped by co-engagement in conflicts, and shared environmental injustice episodes. These structural
differences could be direct consequences of a deep difference in nature between corporations’ and
EJOs’ inner and intrinsic purpose. The former acts based on their exploitative and sectorial nature,
while the latter reacts based on shared values and common believes (so, not on a specific sector).
Finally, the country- and region-level aggregations further reinforce this interpretation. While
corporate networks reproduce patterns of ecologically unequal exchange (Hickel et al., 2022) and
global North–South dependencies (Llavero-Pasquina, 2025; Cantoni et al., 2025b), EJOs tend to
remain more territorially embedded, with limited transnational connectivity. In this sense, the role
of international EJOs becomes therefore critical in enabling the emergence of a global movement.
An emergent and distributed global movement.
While international EJOs play a key role
in connecting geographically distant conflicts, our findings suggest that they are not the sole drivers
of global connectivity within the EJ network. Rather, the global environmental justice movement
18


## Page 19


emerges as a distributed and self-organizing system. Local EJOs, even when primarily engaged in
place-based struggles, contribute to the global structure through shared participation in conflicts,
and the diffusion of knowledge, and narratives across cases. This results in a form of emergent
globality, where the overall coherence of the network does not depend exclusively on centralized
or transnational actors, but arises from the accumulation of local interactions and relational ties.
This consideration is supported by the high inter sectorial edge frequency sign of the emergent
property in the EJO–EJO network to connect different sectors. In addition, the EJO–EJO network
shows a more robust and resilient LCC and structure even within the same cluster (see Fig. 7).
Such a configuration is consistent with theories of complex adaptive systems, where global patterns
emerge from decentralized interactions without requiring hierarchical coordination (Ladyman et al.,
2013; De Domenico et al., 2019). In this sense, international EJOs act as important accelerators
or amplifiers of connectivity, but the underlying structure of the movement remains fundamentally
distributed, and rooted in local struggles.
Divergent actor logics and structural organization.
The structural differences observed be-
tween corporate actors and EJOs can be further interpreted in light of their fundamentally different
organizational logics and objectives. Corporations are primarily oriented toward the development
and expansion of infrastructure and production systems within specific sectors, such as mining, en-
ergy, or transport. This sectoral specialization is reflected in the strong clustering patterns observed
in Fig. 7, where companies tend to co-occur within similar categories of conflicts, indicating coordi-
nated or parallel involvement in projects characterized by comparable technological and economic
logics. Such a structure suggests a tendency toward increasing global integration, where corpo-
rate actors expand their activities across regions while maintaining sectoral coherence, reinforcing
patterns of coordination and accumulation at global scale.
In contrast, EJOs operate according to a fundamentally different rationale. Their primary ob-
jective is not expansion, but the protection of specific territories, and communities affected by
environmental harm. As a result, their emergence is often reactive and context-dependent, trig-
gered by localized instances of environmental injustice. This gives rise to a network structure that
resembles a distributed response system, where EJOs appear and mobilize in specific locations with-
out central coordination. In this sense, EJOs can be metaphorically understood as white blood cells
of socio-ecological systems, acting as decentralized guardians that respond to threats as they arise.
This analogy highlights both the strengths and limitations of the movement: while it enables rapid
and context-sensitive responses, it also contributes to fragmentation and limits the capacity for
coordinated action at global scale. The coexistence of these two logics—expansive and coordinated
for corporations, reactive and distributed for EJOs—ultimately shapes the asymmetric structure of
the global environmental conflict network.
4.2
Policy and Managerial Implications
The structural differences identified between corporate and EJO networks have important implica-
tions both for policy-making and corporate risk management as well as for environmental justice
movement coordination. Therefore, this study may support ethical practices not only for policy
and decision-makers but also for corporations who want to act ethically and avoid to be involved
in scandal and conflicts.
Systemic corruption.
First and foremost, beyond the discussed structural asymmetries, ob-
served patterns point toward the existence of systemic forms of corruption embedded within global
19


## Page 20


networks of environmental conflicts. Rather than being reducible to isolated illegal practices, cor-
ruption can be understood as an emergent property of interconnected systems of actors, and in-
stitutions (Luna-Pla and Nicol´as-Carlock, 2020; Helbing, 2013). In this perspective, corruption
manifests through the repeated co-occurrence of specific corporate actors across multiple contested
projects, often in contexts characterized by weak regulatory frameworks (Gonz´alez, 2022; Brinks
et al., 2019), and consequently limited accountability and transparency (Leonard, 2024). As sug-
gested by recent works applying network analysis to environmental conflicts, these patterns may
signal the presence of structural risk configurations, where the concentration of influences and the
recurrence of actors increase the likelihood of opaque decision-making processes and regulatory
capture (Carrasco, 2025), further supported by deregulations, from one side, or neo-extractivism,
from the other, (Acosta, 2013; Warnecke-Berger et al., 2023; Balc´azar and Argento, 2026), where
transnational corporations act with the consensus of, or even in collaboration and the collusion
with local government and institutions, reducing the local benefit to a mere royalties debate instead
of a systemic injustice practice of exploitation (United Nations Human Rights Council, 2022). In
line with Luna-Pla and Nicol´as-Carlock (2020), systemic corruption should therefore be interpreted
not as an anomaly but as a networked risk, emerging from the interaction between institutional
fragilities and the opportunistic strategic positioning of actors within the network of global capital
flows16 (Coppola et al., 2021). This reinforces the need to move from case-based assessments (Mli-
less and Larouz, 2022) toward systemic indicators (Nicol´as-Carlock and Luna-Pla, 2021b) capable
of identifying patterns of recurrent involvement and potential governance failures.
Towards complex reputational risk indicators for corporations.
Similarly, the high level
of interconnectivity observed in the corporate network suggests that socio-environmental conflicts
should not be treated as isolated events, but rather as part of a broader systemic risk landscape.
When corporations co-occur across multiple conflicts, they become embedded in a network of repu-
tational (Christopher and Gaudenzi, 2009), and regulatory risks (Wang et al., 2023) that may prop-
agate across projects and regions. This is consistent with recent literature on globally networked
risks (Helbing, 2013), where scandals in one part of the system can cascade through interconnected
actors. From this perspective, network-based indicators derived from EJAtlas data could support
the development of early-warning systems for socio-environmental conflicts. For instance, centrality
measures may serve as proxies for exposure to systemic conflict risk, identifying corporations that
are repeatedly involved in contested projects across different contexts. Similarly, country-level and
region-level aggregations can reveal structural dependencies and hotspots of conflict concentration,
supporting more informed territorial planning and governance.
Reinforcing information flow in the EJ global movement.
On the other side, the frag-
mented structure of the EJO network suggests that strengthening transnational coordination among
EJOs could significantly enhance their capacity to counterbalance corporate power. Although evi-
dences support the idea of a robust and resilient global EJ movement, policies and funding mech-
anisms aimed at supporting knowledge exchange, or transnational network formation among EJOs
may contribute to reducing fragmentation and improving collective action at global scale by sup-
porting knowledge and best-practice transfer. This aligns with calls for fostering global solidarity
networks within environmental justice movements (Temper et al., 2018; Menton et al., 2020).

project-level risk evaluations may underestimate the broader network effects associated with con-
flict involvement. Moreover, incorporating network-based metrics into Environmental, Social, and
Governance (ESG) frameworks (Patel and Gloor, 2024) could improve the identification of sys-
temic exposure to environmental justice conflicts and support more responsible decision-making
processes. Similarly, as the demand for critical raw materials and infrastructure projects increases
in the context of the energy transition (IEA, 2021; Dowling and Otero, 2025), the risk of generating
new socio-environmental conflicts is likely to intensify. A network perspective can help anticipate
these dynamics by identifying patterns of conflict co-occurrence and transnational interdependen-
cies, thereby supporting more equitable and conflict-sensitive development pathways. In this sense,
the integration of network science and political ecology, similarly to the convergence of network
science and economic sociology (Granovetter et al., 2000; Barbera et al., 2008) and network sci-
ence stakeholder theory (Rowley, 1997; Cottafava and Corazza, 2021; Corazza et al., 2024), offers
a promising avenue for advancing both analytical and policy-oriented approaches to environmental
justice, moving from isolated case studies (Corazza et al., 2023; Mliless and Larouz, 2022) towards
a systemic understanding of global socio-environmental conflicts.
4.3
Limitations and Future Research
This study presents several limitations that open avenues for future research. While the EJAtlas
provides a uniquely rich empirical basis for analyzing ecological distribution conflicts (Martinez-
Alier, 2021), and unequal exchange at the global scale (Llavero-Pasquina, 2025), it also presents
important constraints that must be acknowledged.
First, the database relies on a crowdmapping approach, which may introduce geographical
and informational biases, with stronger representation in regions where research networks and
activist documentation are more developed, particularly in Latin America, Europe, and North
America (Martinez-Alier, 2021). Second, the presence of actors within the same conflict reflects co-
involvement in a contested socio-environmental process rather than formal, or legal relationships,
limiting the possibility to infer a real coordination, or power dynamics (Aydin et al., 2017). As a
consequence, the network structures analyzed in this study capture patterns of co-occurrence rather
than explicit relational ties. Third, the EJAtlas encompasses a wide spectrum of conflicts—from
cases involving violence and repression to legal disputes and public mobilizations—making it diffi-
cult to assume homogeneity in the nature or intensity of interactions across cases (Martinez-Alier
et al., 2016; Temper et al., 2018). Finally, the aggregation and classification procedures adopted in
this study (e.g., country attribution, or network projections) inevitably simplify the heterogeneity
of actors (e.g. no difference in size has been study) and their involvement in conflict (e.g. legal
dispute or active social movements) and may hide or underrepresent real dynamics.
Concluding, future studies should deeper investigate underlying hypothesis and other available
information in EJAtlas such as actor size, intensity of conflicts or other aspects. Specifically, the
structural differences identified between corporations and EJOs—summarized in Table 1—highlight
divergent organizational logics that are only partially captured by this study and the current network
representation. Therefore, we call for cautious interpretation of the results.

interactions across different types of actors (e.g., small company or large corporations, civil society

Conclusion
This study aimed to explore the global structure of environmental justice conflicts by adopting
a network perspective on the EJAtlas database, with the objective of identifying patterns of co-
occurrence and structural organization among actors to compare and identify similarities and struc-
tural differences between the company and the environmental justice organization network.
Methodologically, we combined a bipartite network approach with successive projections (co-
occurrence in conflicts) and aggregation procedures (belonging to same category or country), allow-
ing for a multi-level quantitative analysis of the global networks. The results reveal highly skewed
and unequal network structures with structural differences between the company and the envi-
ronmental justice organization network. Specifically, the company network reveal high densely and
sectorial clusters of companies with many shared conflicts and high degree, suggesting for a systemic

Moreover, the EJOs network suggest a more globalized and connected network with more than 80%
of total EJOs belonging to the largest connected components (against the 59% of the companies).
Therefore, to answer to the EJAtlas’s father question: is there a global EJ movement? According
to findings from this study, yes there exist a global self-organized, distributed global movement.
Concluding, these quantitative findings are further complemented by qualitative insights, which
highlight the coexistence of two fundamentally different logics: a coordinated, expansion-driven
corporate network operating at a global scale, and a decentralized, place-based, and reactive network
of EJOs emerging locally in response to environmental conflicts.
