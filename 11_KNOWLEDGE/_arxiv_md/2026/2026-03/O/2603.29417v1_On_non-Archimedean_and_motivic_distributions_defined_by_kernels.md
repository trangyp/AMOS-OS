Abstract. —
As in real microlocal analysis, we prove a Schwartz kernel theorem
for p-adic distributions. We extend this result for motivic distributions using Cluckers–
Loeser’s motivic integration. In both settings, we give also a relation between the
wave front sets of the distribution and its kernel.

The theory of real distributions has been introduced by Sobolev [18] and
Schwartz [16], it generalizes the notion of functions and it creates an adapted setting
to find weak solutions to partial differential equations.
In this context, to each
distribution on a product space, we can associate a kernel. That object was first
introduced by Schwartz [17] and the Schwartz kernel theorem can be found for instance
in Hörmander’s book [11] (see Theorem 0.1).
A kernel is an object which generalizes integral operators, i.e. operators T on some
space of the form
Tf(y) =
Z
f(x)K(x, y) dx
for some function K (for instance, the Fourier transform is an integral operator
with K(x, y) = e−i⟨x,y⟩). In particular, kernels generalize differential operators (see [3,
§0.4]) and pseudo-differential operators (see [3, §I.3]). This generalization can be done
using the theory of distributions. As an application in the theory of linear partial
differential equations, kernels can be used to compute inverses of differential operators
(see [19, §52] or [12, Theorem 13.2.1 & Remark p. 185]).

Let us recall some notations. Let m and n be two positive integers. We consider
the m-dimensional euclidean space Rm. Let X be an open set of Rm and Y be an
open set of Rn. We denote by D(X) the set of test functions on X — functions of
class C∞with compact support on X — and D′(X) the set of distributions on X
— continuous linear forms on D(X). For tests functions ϕ on X and ψ on Y , we denote
by ϕ ⊗ψ the test function on X × Y defined by the relation
ϕ ⊗ψ(x, y) = ϕ(x)ψ(y),
∀(x, y) ∈X × Y.
We can now state the Schwartz kernel theorem.
Theorem 0.1 (Schwartz kernel theorem, Hörmander [11, Theorem 5.2.1])
For each distribution u on the product X × Y , there exists a unique continuous
linear map K: D(Y ) −→D′(X) such that
⟨u, ϕ ⊗ψ⟩= ⟨Kψ, ϕ⟩,
∀ϕ ∈D(X), ∀ψ ∈D(Y ).
(1)
Conversely, for each continuous linear map K: D(Y ) −→D′(X), there exists a unique
distribution u on the product X × Y such that equality (1) holds.
Such a map K is called the kernel of the distribution u. For a distribution u
on X ⊂Rm, we denote by WF(u) ⊂X × (Rm \ {0}) its wave front set [11, §8.1]. We
have the following relation between wave front sets.
Theorem 0.2 (Hörmander [11, Theorem 8.2.12]). — Let u be a distribution on X × Y
and K be its kernel. Let ψ be a function of D(Y ). Then
WF(Kψ) ⊂{(x, ξ) ∈X × (Rm \ {0}) | ∃y ∈supp ψ, ((x, y), (ξ, 0)) ∈WF(u)}.
In the p-adic setting, we also have a notion of distribution. Since the p-adic numbers
field Qp is totally disconnected, a distribution is simply a linear form over the space of
Schwartz–Bruhat functions (see [14, §7.1]). In this context, a notion of kernel can be
formulated and, as in the real case, kernels generalize pseudo-differential operators
(see [21]). A p-adic Schwartz kernel theorem exists as it is presented in the books [2,
§4.6] and [20, §VI.7], but proofs are incomplete in both cases. In §1, we propose to
complete these proofs (see Theorem 1.9). Moreover, as Heifetz does [10, Theorem 2.11]
without proof, we give an analogue to Theorem 0.2 (see Theorem 1.18).
In §2, we consider the case of the non-Archimedean field k((t)) for a characteristic
zero field k.
In that setting, we need to use motivic integration introduced by
Kontsevich, developed successively by Denef–Loeser [9], Cluckers–Loeser [8, 7] and
Hrushovski–Kazhdan [13]. We will use Cluckers–Loeser’s motivic integration. In this
context, Raibaut [15] has developed motivic distributions. They satisfy some analogous
properties to those verified by p-adic distributions. We prove a motivic Schwartz
kernel theorem (see Theorem 2.17) and a result of the motivic wave front set on the
kernel (see Theorem 2.26), analogous to Theorem 1.18.

Remark 2.21. — Let us discuss about conditions (K1)–(K4). The first condition
ensures integrability in order to consider the functions FK,φ,α.
Conditions (K2)
and (K3) are pretty natural: we want that the distribution satisfies the pullback
condition (D1) and the pushforward condition (D2).
Thanks to the proof of Theorem 2.20, condition (K3) is satisfied for any definable
morphism g of DefP if and only if it is satisfied for any identity morphism g = idW .
Indeed, the direct sense is clear. Reciprocally, if a kernel K satisfies the condition (K3)
for definable morphisms of the type g = idW , then by Theorem 2.20, it defines a
distribution u and, by Theorem 2.17, its kernel, namely the kernel K by uniqueness,
satisfies the condition (K3) for general definable morphisms g.
Condition (K4) is implied by condition (K3) for Schwartz–Bruhat functions of the
form φ = φ1 ⊗φ2. Nevertheless, it does not seems to be true for general Schwartz–
Bruhat functions. It would be the case if one could compare the morphisms α and β,
but it is not the case. Indeed, a definable morphism is a family of functions indexed
by the field of Fieldk and, for instance, it could be(5) that there exists two points w
and w′ of W such that
α(w) < β(w)
and
α(w′) > β(w′).
This hypothesis (K4) allows to define the distribution u with formula (34). Moreover,
it provides flexibility in the choice of morphisms α+(−). For instance, if we work with
a sum φ = φ1 + · · · + φℓof some Schwartz–Bruhat functions φ1, . . . , φℓ, we want
to take the morphism α+(φ) as the morphism max(α+(φ1), . . . , α+(φℓ)) and not the
