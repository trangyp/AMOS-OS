Abstract—Room Impulse Response (RIR) estimation is a
fundamental problem in spatial audio processing and speech
enhancement. In this paper, we build upon our previously
introduced diffusion-based inpainting framework for RIR in-
terpolation and demonstrate its applicability to enhancing the
performance of practical multi-microphone array processing
tasks. Furthermore, we validate the robustness of this method
in interpolating real-world RIRs.
Index Terms—Diffusion models, RIR interpolation, microphone
array processing, Virtual Microphone (VM)
I. INTRODUCTION
Room Impulse Responses (RIRs) play a fundamental role
in acoustic signal processing [1]. They characterize sound
propagation in enclosed environments and underpin key tasks
such as source localization, source separation, dereverberation,
and speech enhancement. However, acquiring dense and ac-
curate RIR measurements in real rooms is time-consuming
and resource-intensive. As an alternative, simulated RIRs [2],
[3] are often used, yet they typically fail to capture the full
complexity of real acoustic environments.
To address these limitations, numerous methods have
been proposed to reconstruct or interpolate missing RIRs
from sparse measurements. Existing approaches range from
geometry-based modeling techniques [4]–[7] to data-driven,
learning-based methods [8], [9], all aiming to enhance spatial
resolution without requiring exhaustive measurement cam-
paigns.
In our previous work [10], we proposed DiffusionRIR, a
diffusion-based generative framework that treats the collection
of RIRs as a grayscale image and formulates the reconstruction
problem as an inpainting task. DiffusionRIR demonstrated
strong performance on simulated datasets, outperforming stan-
dard interpolation baselines. However, the evaluation in [10]
was limited to synthetic data, leaving open the question of
how well the model generalizes to real, measured RIRs.
Beyond RIR reconstruction itself, the quality and density
of spatial acoustic information have a direct impact on down-
stream array signal processing applications. Beamforming
performance, including that of the Minimum Variance Distor-
tionless Response (MVDR) beamformer, depends directly on
the number and spatial distribution of available microphones.
To improve performance in scenarios with limited sensing, the
Virtual Microphone (VM) framework [11]–[13] synthetically
increases the number of channels—often using geometric
assumptions and interpolation in the short-time Fourier trans-
form (STFT) domain—thereby enabling more effective spatial
filtering with a small number of physical microphones.
When microphone signals are available, MVDR beamform-
ing can be implemented using either a direction-of-arrival
(DOA)-based or an Relative Transfer Function (RTF)-based
steering vector. Previous studies [14], [15] have shown that
RTF-based beamformers are effective for interference sup-
pression; however, they inherently preserve the reverberant
component captured at the reference microphone, thereby lim-
iting dereverberation performance. In contrast, beamforming
based on the full Acoustic Transfer Functionss (ATFs) enables
not only spatial filtering but also suppression of reverberation
effects, since the complete acoustic transfer function is incor-
porated into the steering vector. While estimating the DOAs
or RTFs constitutes a non-blind problem, estimation of the full
ATFs from measured microphone signals is inherently blind
and significantly more challenging. Furthermore, acquiring
dense and accurate ATFs (or equivalently, RIRs) measure-
ments, required for high-resolution beampattern synthesis, is
difficult in practical acoustic environments. This challenge
motivates the reconstruction of missing RIRs from a limited
set of measurements.
In this work, we assume that microphone signals are
available at all array positions, while RIR measurements are
available only at a subset of microphones. DiffusionRIR is
used to reconstruct the missing RIRs, which are then employed
for beamforming. We assess the usefulness of DiffusionRIR
for beamforming performance and evaluate its robustness
using real-world acoustic measurements, thereby extending
prior validation beyond synthetic data.
II. PROBLEM FORMULATION
The objective of this work is to exploit reconstructed RIRs
for spatial speech enhancement and separation. We build upon
our previously proposed framework [10], which addressed the
task of estimating missing RIRs in microphone arrays.
Consider an array of N microphones, of which only M
RIRs are measured. The remaining L = N −M RIRs are
unmeasured. Let H ∈RK×N denote the complete RIR matrix,

enhancing beamforming performance.
III. RIR INTERPOLATION USING INPAINTING
In this section, we briefly summarize the DiffusionRIR
framework introduced in our previous work [10], which serves
as the basis for the interpolation method used throughout
this study. Reconstruction is treated as an inpainting task,
drawing inspiration from recent advances in diffusion models,
in particular the RePaint strategy [16]. The RIR set is first
recast into a grayscale image-like structure, where each col-
umn corresponds to one microphone response truncated to K
samples. Missing microphones are encoded by a column-wise
binary mask M, which ensures that measured data remain
unchanged while only the unmeasured positions are estimated.
The generative diffusion process starts from Gaussian noise
and iteratively refines the RIR image via a learned reverse-
diffusion operator Dθ. We employ OpenAI’s Denoising Dif-
fusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) architecture1 with mod-
ifications for RIR-matrix images. To improve precision in the
low-energy parts of the RIRs, the image is split into overlap-
ping 64 × 64 patches, normalized to the range [−1, 1], and
reassembled into ˆH, thereby preserving spatial and temporal
consistency across the array.
The complete procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1.

Experiment Setup: Experiments were conducted using sim-
ulated data generated with the Pyroomacoustics package.2 A
uniform linear array (ULA) with N = 16 microphones and an
inter-element spacing of 4 cm was used, yielding an aperture
of 0.64 m. The room dimensions were 6× 5.5×2.8 m, with a
reverberation time of T60 = 300 ms. Signals were sampled at
8 kHz, and each RIR was truncated to 2048 samples (256 ms).
The speech source was located 2 m in front of the array at
broadside, and 7-seconds utterances were employed.
To evaluate robustness in noisy conditions, two spatial noise
scenarios were considered: (i) a directional noise source placed
at a different room location, 2 m from the array, and (ii) diffuse
noise generated using a diffuse noise simulator.3 All noise
signals were generated as pink noise, with SNR levels ranging
from −10 to 10 dB. Additionally, spatially white noise was
added to the mixture at an SNR of 10 dB.
Partial RIR availability was determined using four masking
configurations:
• Mask 0: 12 microphones measured, 4 missing.
• Mask 1: 8 microphones measured, 8 missing.
• Mask 2, 3: 4 microphones measured, 12 missing.
All mask configurations are illustrated in Fig. 2.
For each scenario, the missing RIRs were reconstructed
using DiffusionRIR and then integrated into the MVDR beam-
forming framework.
Performance Metrics: Performance was evaluated using the
following measures:
• Signal-to-Interference Ratio (SIR) [dB], computed sep-
arately on speech-only and noise-only segments;
• Scale-Invariant Signal-to-Distortion Ratio (SI-SDR)
[dB], measured between the clean target signal and the
beamformer output;
• Speech Intelligibility (STOI), a perceptual intelligibility
score ranging from 0 to 1.

B. Null Steering and Interference Cancellation
Now we turn to another task, for which it is required to
steer a null towards an interference source. For example, as
described in [15], [17], the Generalized Sidelobe Canceller
(GSC) structure directs a spatial null to obtain a noise-
dominant reference signal, which is then used by the adaptive
noise cancellation filters (e.g., implemented via the Least
Mean Squares (LMS) adaptation) to suppress interference and
recover the target speech. We use the reconstructed RIRs to
impose spatial nulls in prescribed directions and demonstrate
that incorporating the reconstructed RIRs alongside the mea-
sured ones results in increased null depth. Note that, unlike
MVDR, this analysis does not require estimation of the noise
covariance matrix Φnn; however, the resulting null steering is
still applied to the microphone signals.

V. GENERALIZATION TO REAL DATA
In [10], DiffusionRIR was evaluated only on simulated
RIRs. The current study advances the experimental validation
by moving from simulated data to real-world acoustic mea-
surements.
Dataset: For real-world evaluation, we used the MeshRIR
dataset [19], a large-scale database of densely measured
Room Impulse Responses (RIRs). This dataset provides RIRs
sampled on three-dimensional grids, enabling high-spatial-
resolution studies. The dataset was recorded in a reverberant
room with dimensions 7.0 × 6.4 × 2.7 m and a reverberation
time of approximately T60 ≈300 ms. The loudspeaker was po-
sitioned approximately 2 m from the array plane. Recordings
were acquired at a sampling rate of 48 kHz and subsequently
downsampled to 8 kHz to align with previous experiments.
Each RIR was truncated to 2048 samples (256 ms), capturing
the direct path and early reflections.
We focused on the S1-M3969 subset of MeshRIR, compris-
ing 21 × 21 × 9 microphone grid (a total of 3969 positions)
spanning a cube of size 1 × 1 × 0.4 m. From this grid, we
extracted a horizontal plane of 441 microphones (21 × 21)
and designed two array configurations for evaluation:
1) Three Rows: 63 microphones corresponding to the first
three parallel rows of the grid.
2) Frame: 41 microphones located along the top and left
edges of the plane, forming a L-shaped boundary.
These configurations are illustrated in Fig. 3.
For each setup, we applied random masking by removing
a subset of microphones, thereby simulating missing mea-
surements. The remaining microphones were used as inputs
to DiffusionRIR to reconstruct the missing responses. This
design allows us to systematically evaluate reconstruction
performance under different spatial sampling patterns.

VI. DISCUSSION
We addressed the problem of reconstructing RIRs at un-
measured microphone positions and evaluated DiffusionRIR
on real-world acoustic measurements. The results demonstrate
strong transfer from simulated to measured environments,
while preserving spatial structure and reverberation charac-
teristics. These findings indicate that DiffusionRIR captures
underlying acoustic structure beyond idealized simulation as-
sumptions and remains effective on real-life data.
Building on this validation, we integrate the interpolated
RIRs into classical microphone array processing tasks and
show that their incorporation yields significant performance
improvements.
