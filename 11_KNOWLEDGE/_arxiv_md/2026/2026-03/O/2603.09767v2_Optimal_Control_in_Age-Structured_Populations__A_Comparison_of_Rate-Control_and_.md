Abstract
This paper investigates the dynamics and optimal harvesting of age-structured
populations governed by McKendrick–von Foerster equations, contrasting two
distinct harvesting mechanisms: rate-control and effort-control. For the rate-
control formulation, where harvesting acts as a direct additive removal term,
we derive first-order necessary optimality conditions of Pontryagin type for the
associated infinite-horizon optimal control problem, explicitly characterizing the
adjoint system, transversality conditions, and control switching laws. In contrast,
the effort-control formulation introduces harvesting as a multiplicative mortal-
ity intensity dependent on aggregate population size. We demonstrate that this
aggregate dependence structurally alters the optimality system, formally gen-
erating a nonlocal coupling term in the adjoint equation that links all ages
through the total stock. By combining rigorous variational control derivations,
and explicit stationary profiles, this work clarifies the profound mathemati-
cal and bioeconomic distinctions between additive and multiplicative harvesting
strategies.
Keywords: Age-structured population dynamics; Optimal harvesting; Infinite-horizon
optimal control; McKendrick-von Foerster equation; Pontryagin maximum principle

1 Introduction
Age-structured population models provide a classical framework for describing the
evolution of heterogeneous populations in which the demographic state depends explic-
itly on age. In continuous time, such dynamics are typically formulated through
McKendrick–von Foerster type transport equations [1, 2], which have become a stan-
dard mathematical tool in population dynamics, demography, resource management,
and harvesting theory. A systematic mathematical treatment of these models is devel-
oped in the foundational works of Webb [3, 4], Iannelli [5], Metz et al. [6], and Iannelli
and Milner [7]. Within this framework, deriving optimal harvesting policies under
dynamical constraints becomes particularly central.
The literature relevant to the present paper falls into several closely related strands:
the general theory of structured population dynamics and semigroup formulations
[3–11]; steady-state analysis for structured populations [10, 12–21]; optimal control
and harvesting of age-structured systems [22–29]; infinite-horizon optimal control and
transversality [30–32]; and bioeconomic formulations of harvesting [33–38]. A more
detailed review of these contributions is given in Section 2.
In this paper we focus on two harvesting mechanisms in age-structured models.
The first is a rate-control formulation, in which harvesting enters the state equation as
a direct removal term. The second is an effort-control formulation, in which harvesting
acts as an additional mortality intensity and the instantaneous yield is proportional to
both effort and stock abundance. First, for the infinite-horizon harvesting problem, we
derive necessary optimality conditions of Pontryagin type, including the adjoint sys-
tem, transversality condition, switching relations for the distributed harvesting control
and the boundary inflow control, and complementary slackness for the state constraint.
We then compare rate-control and effort-control formulations to highlight the struc-
tural differences between them. In particular, we exhibit the nonlocal coupling term in
the effort-control adjoint equation induced by aggregate population dependence and
thereby make explicit how the choice of harvesting mechanism changes the analytical
structure of the optimality system. The contributions of the paper can be summarized
as follows:
(i) We derive necessary optimality conditions for an infinite-horizon age-structured
harvesting problem. They include the adjoint equation, boundary and transversal-
ity conditions, switching conditions for the controls, and complementary slackness
relations associated with the nonnegativity constraint on the state.
(ii) We clarify the mathematical and economic distinction between rate-control and
effort-control mechanisms by comparing the way they enter both the state dynam-
ics and the profit functional, and by exhibiting the nonlocal adjoint term induced
by aggregate population dependence in the effort-control setting.

ics, steady-state analysis, optimal control, and bioeconomic harvesting models. In
Section 3 we introduce the models and standing assumptions. In Section 4 we formu-

3 Model and assumptions
We consider age-structured population models on the age interval [0, A], where A ∈
(0, ∞) denotes the maximal age and t ≥0 is time. The state variable x(t, a) ≥0
represents the population density of individuals of age a at time t. Throughout the
paper, a denotes age and t denotes time. The boundary input p(t) ≥0 describes the
inflow of newborn or newly introduced individuals at age a = 0.
Our aim is to compare two harvesting mechanisms within a common age-structured
framework. The first one is a rate-control model, in which harvesting acts as a direct
removal term. The second one is an effort-control model, in which harvesting enters as
an additional mortality intensity and may depend on the aggregate population size.

For the rate-control model this leads to a linear transport problem, while the effort-

control structure. These two features are precisely what distinguish the mathematical
structure of the two harvesting mechanisms.
The two model classes play different roles in the sequel. For Model R, we study
both steady states and an infinite-horizon discounted harvesting problem. In partic-
ular, the rate-control framework is used to derive necessary optimality conditions of
Pontryagin type. For Model E, our purpose is more limited. We use the effort-control
formulation to exhibit how multiplicative harvesting and aggregate dependence mod-
ify the structure of the state equation, the objective functional, and the corresponding
adjoint system. This will make clear why the effort-control case naturally generates a
nonlocal term in the formal dual equation.

By juxtaposing a rate-control formulation with an effort-control formulation, we have
highlighted the profound ways in which the choice of management variable dictates
the analytical and structural properties of the resulting optimal control problem.

Bioeconomic and Ecological Insights
The analytical differences between the two models are closely mirrored in their bioeco-
nomic behavior, as evidenced by our stationary reductions and numerical simulations.
The rate-control mechanism is mathematically aggressive; the explicit affine Volterra
representation shows that direct removal can rapidly drive specific age classes to zero.
This requires strict nonnegativity constraints and heavily relies on bang-bang con-
trol strategies to prevent localized population collapse. The corresponding yield curve
grows steeply but abruptly levels off as state constraints become active.
By contrast, the effort-control mechanism is inherently self-regulating. The explicit
multiplicative exponential profile ensures that the extraction scales proportionally with
the available stock. Consequently, the yield curve is strictly concave, and population
depletion is less severe at equivalent nominal harvesting intensities. This mathemat-
ical behavior aligns with classical bioeconomic intuition: effort-based management
naturally cushions against overexploitation because diminishing stock abundance
simultaneously reduces the instantaneous catch.
Limitations and Future Directions
While our results clarify the structural dichotomy between rate and effort controls,
several avenues for future research remain. First, the derivation of the adjoint system
for Model E presented here is primarily formal. Establishing a rigorous, functional-
analytic proof of the infinite-horizon maximum principle for this nonlinear, nonlocally
coupled system—particularly concerning the precise formulation of the transversality
conditions at infinity—remains a formidable mathematical challenge.
Second, our framework assumes purely deterministic population dynamics. Inte-
grating stochastic demographic fluctuations or environmental noise into these struc-
tured models is a critical next step. Recent advances in the well-posedness of stochastic
ecological systems suggest a viable pathway for generalizing the present optimal
harvesting results to noisy, uncertain environments.
Finally, extending the current single-species framework to multi-species models
with age-structured predator-prey dynamics or spatial heterogeneity would allow for
the investigation of optimal harvesting policies under complex ecological interactions.
The nonlocal adjoint structures identified in this paper will likely play a central role
in resolving the optimality systems of such high-dimensional bioeconomic models.
