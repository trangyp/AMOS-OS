GRADUALIST DESCRIPTIONALIST SET THEORY
DAVID SIMMONS
Abstract. We introduce a formal language GDST (gradualist descriptionalist set theory) with a family
of interpretations indexed by ordinals, as well as a sublanguage NMID (the language of not necessarily
monotonic inductive definitions), and show that the assertion that all propositions in NMID have well-
defined truth values is equivalent to the existence for each k ∈N of a sequence of ordinals η0 < . . . < ηk
such that for each i < k, ηi is ηi+1-reflecting, a notion we introduce which implies being Πn-reflecting for
all n ∈N (and in particular being admissible and recursively Mahlo).
1. Introduction
The following questions are closely linked:
• What mathematical objects exist?
• What mathematical questions have definite answers?
In [3, Section 4], the author proposed a possible answer to these questions in the form of an interpreted
formal language GDST, “gradualist descriptionalist set theory”, the motivating intuitions of which are
that
• Gradualism: The universe of sets cannot be viewed as a totality at any given point in time; rather,
it is always growing. Cf. [1, p.503] and the references therein.
• Descriptionalism: Every mathematical object should be describable in principle, where by “describ-
able” we mean something like “assigned a natural number (or equivalently a string in a countable
language) that uniquely identifies it”. Descriptionalism is somewhat similar to predicativism (cf.
e.g. [6] for an overview), and also to Nik Weaver’s “constructive countablism” [5].
See Section 2 for a precise definition of GDST. The semantics of GDST can be taken in two ways:
either “at face value”, referring to a gradualist descriptionalist view of the constructible universe L, or
“embedded in a metatheory”. In this note we will utilize both points of view, but focus on the second,
using Kripke–Platek set theory KP as our metatheory.
In Section 2 we introduce the language GDST. In Section 3 we introduce an axiomatization of GDST
and compare it to the axiomatization KP of ST. In Section 4 we deal with the question of whether there
is a canonical ordinal at which to halt the constructive hierarchy in the context of GDST. In Section
5 we introduce the notion of reflecting ordinals and compare them with other large ordinal properties;
in particular, we show that reflecting ordinals are admissible. In Section 6 we introduce a sub-language
of GDST, the language of not necessarily monotonic inductive definitions NMID, and show that the
existence of k-fold reflecting ordinals for all k ∈N implies that propositions in the language NMID have
well-defined truth values. In Section 7 we prove the converse of this assertion, thus justifying the existence
of k-fold reflecting ordinals as being in a sense predicative.

The philosophical content of the dichotomy observed in Corollary 4.6 requires some explanation. We are
using KP as our metatheory, but in some ways this is a bad choice because KP does not have a canonical
model, and so the truth values of statements like the hypothesis of Corollary 4.6 depend on the choice
of model. (“The entire universe of sets” is not a good answer to “what is the canonical model” from a
gradualist point of view, since the universe is always being expanded.)
Thus, Lθ∅is only a “canonical model” of GDST in the sense that the universe of sets is a “canonical
model” of KP, which is to say, not quite. The following remarks may help illustrate this point:
• θ∅is defined as being the largest -descriptionalist ordinal. The concept of an ordinal being -
descriptionalist is independent of the choice of model as long as we restrict to models of the form
Lα, but the concept of a “largest” such ordinal is not, as it requires quantifying over all ordinals
greater than θ∅. The class of ordinals greater than θ∅is a proper class and is therefore always
growing, meaning that quantification over it is not necessarily well-defined.
• If we assume that there exists an uncountable ordinal, then θ∅can alternately be defined as the
largest -descriptionalist ordinal less than ω1, where ω1 is the smallest uncountable ordinal. This
gets rid of the quantification over the proper class of ordinals greater than θ∅, but it introduces a
new improper quantification, since an ordinal is defined to be “uncountable” if there is no bijection
between it an N, requiring us to quantify over the class of bijections, which is again a proper class.
Put more concretely, the issue is that an ordinal α may be uncountable with respect to the model
Lβ of KP for some β > α, but then countable again with respect to some larger model Lγ with
γ > β.
