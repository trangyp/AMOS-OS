Graphs of group actions and group actions
on trees
Florian Lehner, Christian Lindorfer, R¨ognvaldur G. M¨oller,
and Wolfgang Woess
April 2, 2026
Bass–Serre theory provides a powerful framework for studying group ac-
tions on trees. While extremely effective for structural questions in group
theory, it is less suited to the systematic construction of group actions with
prescribed local behaviour. Motivated by local-to-global constructions such
as the Burger–Mozes universal groups and local action diagrams, we develop
an analogue of Bass–Serre theory for group actions.
The central object of study in our are graphs of group actions, combinato-
rial structures similar to graphs of groups from Bass–Serre theory, encoding
compatible local permutation actions on a base graph. From these we can
construct groups which act on tree-like graphs called scaffoldings and hence
also on trees.
We prove uniqueness and universality results for the resulting groups and
show that our framework unifies and generalises (among other known con-
structions) both graphs of groups and local action diagrams. Remarkably, we
are able to encapsulate the full generality of the former while still allowing
for efficient construction of groups with certain local properties like in the
latter.
Introduction
Combining simpler pieces in a tree-like way can be a very powerful tool, as evidenced by
beautiful results in group theory [15, 18, 21, 23, 24], graph theory [6, 7, 9, 19, 20], and
many other mathematical fields such as computational complexity [1, 8], and probability
theory [4].
Free products with amalgamation of two groups and HNN-extensions are important
examples of such constructions in geometric group theory; Bass–Serre theory [21] uses
the concept of a graph of groups to describe groups that one gets by repeated use of these
two constructions. The fundamental theorem of Bass–Serre theory states that we can
associate to every graph of groups its fundamental group, a group which naturally acts

on a tree. Conversely, every group acting on a tree is the fundamental group of a graph
of groups. This correspondence has been uniquely useful when it comes to analysing
groups acting on trees and in theory it can be used to construct any group acting on
a tree by giving its corresponding graph of groups. However, as observed by Reid and
Smith [18], the construction of groups with certain properties using the graph-of-groups
construction can be difficult.
To overcome these difficulties, a complementary approach of defining groups by local
actions (that is, actions of vertex stabilisers on the neighbourhood of the fixed ver-
tex) has emerged. Two important constructions following this principle are the univer-
sal group construction of Burger and Mozes [5] and Smith’s Box product construction
[22]. Similarly to amalgamated free products and HNN-extensions in Bass–Serre theory,
these two constructions can be combined to define a local-action complement to classical
Bass–Serre theory, see [18]. The central object in the resulting theory are local action
diagrams which have some striking similarities to graphs of groups. To every local action
diagram we can associate a universal group which , similarly to the fundamental group
of Bass–Serre theory, acts on a tree. It is worth pointing out that this universal group
always has Tits’ independence property (P), and conversely, every group acting on a tree
with property (P) is the universal group of some local action diagram.
The aim of this work is to develop an analogue of Bass–Serre theory for group actions.
This unifies and generalises known constructions including the aforementioned graph of
groups [21] and local action diagram [18] constructions and many others [2, 5, 10, 13, 22].
The central notion in our theory is a combinatorial structure called a graph of group
actions. This is directly modelled on the concept of a graph of groups but the definition
of the universal group of a graph of group actions is more closely related to the local-
to-global approach of local action diagrams. More precisely, a graph of group actions
consists of a connected graph ∆, called the base graph, together with permutation group
action at each vertex and each arc together with an embedding of the group action at
an arc into the group action at the terminal vertex of the arc. To each graph of group
actions we can associate its universal group which acts on a tree-like graph (called the
scaffolding for the graph of group actions); an action of the universal group on a tree
can be obtained by taking a quotient of the scaffolding.
Within this framework, one can recover both the fundamental group of a graph of
groups in Bass–Serre theory by letting all groups act regularly on themselves, as well as
the theory of local action diagrams by taking all arc groups to be trivial groups acting
on a one-element set. We note that in the case of Bass–Serre theory, the scaffolding
is closely related (but not quite equal) to a Cayley graph of the resulting fundamental
group.
It is worth pointing out that we can also add more structure to the sets the vertex
and edge groups act on. For instance, if at each vertex we have an action of a group of
automorphisms on some graph, we can recover the notion of tree amalgamation of graphs
introduced by Mohar in [13]. A variant of Mohar’s tree amalgamation which respects
given subgroups of the automorphism groups of the constituent graphs was introduced
by Hamann et al. [10, Section 5]. Our construction extends the constructions in [13] and
[10] in very much the same way as the graph of groups and their fundamental groups

The rest of this paper is structured as follows. In the first section basic terminology and
basic concepts are explained. Our terminology for graphs is similar to the terminology
used in Serre’s book [21], except that we allow edges that are self reverse, i.e. a = a.
Section 2 contains the definition of graphs of group actions, scaffoldings and the uni-
versal group of a graph of group actions. At first the definition of a graph of group
actions is limited to the case where the base graph has no self-reverse arcs; the general
definition where edge inversions are permitted is deferred to Section 5 where it is also
shown that results from the special case without inversions carry over to the general case
by a subdivision argument.
In Section 3 it is shown that scaffolding graphs do exist for such graphs of group
actions and that the definition of the universal group makes sense. In Section 4, it is
then shown that any two scaffoldings are isomorphic in a strong sense which also implies
that the group (and its action on the scaffolding graph) defined by a graph of group
actions is unique up to conjugation. The technicalities of proving that are made simpler
by excluding the possibility of thee being self-reverse arcs in the base graph.
As mentioned above, in Section 5 graphs of group actions where the base graph has
self-reverse arcs are defined. Concepts defined in Section 2 are extended to this case
and the main result of Section 4 is generalised. This gives us Theorem 5.6 that is used
to show that any two scaffoldings for the same graph of group actions give isomorphic
actions of the universal groups on the respective scaffoldings.
In Section 7 basic properties of the actions of the universal group on a scaffolding and
the associated tree are discussed.
As mentioned above, the definition of a graph of group actions and their universal
groups combine ideas from Bass–Serre theory and ideas from local-to-global construc-
tions. In Section 8 it is shown how a graph of groups can in a simple way be turned into
a graph of group actions so that the action of the universal group of this graph of group
actions on the associated tree is the same as the action of the fundamental group of the
graph of groups on the associated tree. In the subsequent section it is then shown that
the universal groups of Burger and Mozes can be defined in terms of graphs of group
actions. We note that our construction is more general, because if one thinks in terms
of actions on trees then the universal groups of Reid and Smith’s local action diagram
correspond to (P)-closed groups, whereas our construction also gives groups which do
not satisfy property (P); some examples are given in the appendix.
The final section of this paper gives an alternative view of the universal group. A
graph of groups actions can contain a lot of information that is immaterial when it
comes to considering the action on the associated tree. In Section 10 it is shown how it
is possible to remove some of that surplus information to get a simpler graph of group
actions that gives the same group action on a tree as the original graph of group actions.
In addition, it is shown that one could define the action of the universal group on the

1.1. Graphs
Our notation for graphs is similar to the notation in Serre’s book [21]. A graph Γ consists
of a set of vertices VΓ, a set of arcs AΓ and maps AΓ →VΓ × VΓ; a 7→(o(a), t(a)) and
AΓ →AΓ; a →a such that a = a and o(a) = t(a) for every a ∈AΓ. For an arc a the
vertex o(a) is called the origin of a, the vertex t(a) is called the terminus of a and the
arc a is called the reverse of the arc a. An arc a such that o(a) = t(a) is called a loop.
If a is a loop then it is possible that a ̸= a, but also that a = a; in the latter case we
call the arc a self-reverse. The arcs a and a form together an edge in the graph; the set
of edges will be denoted by EΓ. In the case where a = a the corresponding edge is said
to be self-reverse.
The same notation is used for digraphs except that then we do not have the map
taking an arc to its reverse. A digraph Γ is defined as a quadruple (VΓ, AΓ, o, t), where
VΓ is the set of vertices, AΓ is the set of arcs, and o and t are maps AΓ →VΓ. As
above we call o(a) the origin of the arc a and t(a) is called the terminus of a. Note that
every graph is also a digraph.
We say that distinct vertices u and v (in a graph or a digraph) are adjacent, or
neighbours, if there is an arc a such that {o(a), t(a)} = {u, v}.
The set of vertices
adjacent to a vertex v in a graph Γ is denoted by NΓ(v), or just N(v) if there is no
danger of confusion.
A graph is called simple if it has no loops and the map AΓ →VΓ×VΓ; a 7→(o(a), t(a))
is injective. An arc a in a simple graph can be represented by an ordered pair of vertices
(u, v) where u = o(a) and v = t(a), and the corresponding edge can be represented by
an unordered pair {u, v}. For a digraph (or a graph), we can also define the underlying
simple graph as the simple graph that has the same vertex set as the digraph and two
distinct vertices are adjacent if and only if they are adjacent in the digraph.
A path of length n in Γ (a graph or a digraph) is a sequence v0, . . . , vn of distinct
vertices such that vi and vi+1 are adjacent for i = 0, . . . , n−1. If we do not insist on the
vertices being distinct then we talk about a walk. A graph is said to be connected if for
any two vertices u and v there is a path starting with one of them and ending with the
other. A simple cycle of length n is a walk v0, . . . , vn such that v0 = vn and the vertices
v0, . . . , vn−1 are all distinct.

1.2. Group actions
For a set X we denote the symmetric group on X, the group of all permutations of X,
by Sym(X). A group action (G, X) consists of a group G, a (non-empty) set X and
a homomorphism ρ: G →Sym(X). For g ∈G and x ∈X we write g(x) = (ρ(g))(x).
A group action is faithful if the kernel of the action, the subgroup K = {g ∈G |
g(x) = x for all x ∈X}, is trivial, or equivalently, if the homomorphism ρ is injective.
A faithful group action (G, X) is called a permutation group action; in this case we call
G a permutation group on X and think of G as a subgroup of Sym(X).
The orbit of a point x ∈X is the set Gx = {g(x) | g ∈G}. The action is said to
be transitive if Gx = X for one, and hence every, x ∈X or, in other words, the action
is transitive if for any two points x, y ∈X there exists an element g ∈G such that
g(x) = y. The stabiliser of x ∈X is the subgroup Gx = {g ∈G | g(x) = x}. For a set
Y ⊆X the pointwise stabiliser of Y is G(Y ) = {g ∈G | g(y) = y for all y ∈Y } and the
setwise stabiliser is G{Y } = {g ∈G | g(Y ) = Y }.
In the case the action of G on X is not faithful we can regard GX = G/G(X) as a
permutation group on X and say that it is the permutation group on X induced by G.
For a subset Y of X we define GY as the permutation group on Y induced by G{Y }.

1.3. Topological groups and the permutation topology
When G is a group acting on a set X it possible to endow G with the permutation
topology, see for instance [26] and [14]. In this topology the family of all subgroups of
the form G(F), where F is a finite subset of X, forms a neighbourhood basis for the
identity. A subgroup is thus open if and only if it contains the pointwise stabilizer of a
finite subset of X. Another way to describe this topology is to think of X as having the
discrete topology and the permutation topology as the compact-open topology on G. The
compact-open topology has the property that the action map G×X →X; (g, x) 7→g(x)
is continuous. If the action of G on X is faithful then G is totally disconnected.
A sequence {gi} of elements in G converges to an element g ∈G in the permutation
topology if and only if for each element x ∈X there is a number Nx such that if
i ≥Nx then gi(x) = g(x). We say that G is closed in the permutation topology if GX is
closed in Sym(X) with respect to the permutation topology on Sym(X). The topological
properties of compactness and co-compactness have natural descriptions in terms of the
action of G on X,

7.2. Properties of the permutation topology
When a group G acts on a set X one can endow G with the permutation topology as
explained in Section 1.3. In this section we study the interplay between the properties
of the actions given in the gga and the properties of the permutation topologies the
universal group gets from the action on a scaffolding and the action on the associated
tree.
The first lemma establishes a connection between the topologies arising from these
two actions, respectively.
Lemma 7.10. Let G be a gga, G its universal group and Σ a scaffolding for G. Set
T = TG. The topology arising from the action of G on the tree T is contained in the
topology arising from the action of G on a scaffolding.
Proof. Consider the topology arising from the action of G on T. A neighbourhood basis
of the identity is given by the groups G(F) where F is a finite set of vertices in T. Each
vertex in T corresponds to a vertex bundle in Σ. From each vertex bundle corresponding
to a vertex of T that is in F select a vertex and let F ′ be the set of these vertices. Clearly
G(F ′) ≤G(F) and the subgroup G(F ′) is open in the topology on G arising from its action
on the scaffolding Σ. Thus the subgroup G(F) is open in the topology arising from the
action on T and the result follows.

compact groups, we next focus on these two properties.

8. Graphs of groups: Bass–Serre theory
The Bass–Serre theory of groups acting on trees is one of the corner stones of geometric
group theory. The basic concepts in Bass–Serre theory are graphs of groups and the
fundamental group of a graph of groups. The definition of a gga is, as explained in the
introduction, modelled in the definition of a graph of groups, but the the definition of
a universal group of a gga does not resemble the definition of the fundamental group of
a graph of groups. The aim in this section is to show how the fundamental group of a
graph of groups can be constructed as the universal group of a gga.

Definition 8.1. A graph of groups consists of a connected, non-empty graph ∆, without
self-reverse arcs, and for each vertex v in ∆there is a given group G(v) and for each arc
a there is a given group H(a) such that H(a) = H(a) and for each arc a in ∆there is
also given an injective group homomorphism θa : H(a) →G(t(a)).
To each graph of groups we can construct a goup acting on a tree, the fundamental
group, as follows.
Start with a graph of groups. Use the indicies of the subgroups θa(H(a)) in G(t(a))
to define a graph ∆+ similar to the augmented base graph defined in Section 2. Then
construct a Star-covering tree T for ∆+. with π+ : T →∆+ the associated Star-covering
map. As in Section 3.2 we may assume that π+ is compatible with the graph structure
of ∆and we get a graph morphism π : T →∆. This tree is clearly isomorphic to the
tree constructed in [21, Section 5.3]. The fundamental group of a graph of groups acts
on this tree such that quotient graph is equal to ∆. The stabilizer of a vertex v on T
is isomorphic to G(π(v)) and the stabilizer of an arc a in T is isomorphic to G(π(a)).
Conversely, given a group G acting without inversion on a tree T one can construct a
graph of groups such that the action of the fundamental group on the associated tree is
isomorphic to the action of G on T, see [21, Section 5.4].
From a given graph of groups we construct a gga G such that the underlying graph is
also ∆. For a vertex v in ∆the group action at v is the regular action of G(v) on itself
and for an arc a the group action is the regular action of H(a) on itself. The embedding
of (H(a), H(a)) into (G(t(a)), G(t(a))) is given by the group homomorphism θa. Let
T = TG denote a Star-covering tree for ∆+ and let π : T →∆be the associated map.

that GJwK fixes all the neighbours of JwK in T. Suppose JuK is a vertex in T adjacent

[u] that is adjacent to x. Thus g fixes y. Since GJuK acts regularly on [u] we see that g

trivially on T. It follows that if g is an element that fixes the vertex JwK in T then the
action of g on the T is determined by the action of g on [w]. Thus GJwK is isomorphic

action of the fundamental group of the graph of groups on its associated tree is isomorphic
to the action of the universal group of the gga on its associated tree. In particular the
fundamental group of the graph of groups is isomorphic to the universal group of the gga.

9.2. Smith’s box product
The box product was introduced by Smith in in [22] as a means to construct uncountably

Property (P) (discussed in section 9.4 below) and Tits’ theorem [25, Th´eor`eme 4.5] lie

Definition 9.3. Let M and N be non-trivial permutation groups acting on sets X and
Y , respectively. Let T be the (|X|, |Y |)-semi-regular tree, that is, an infinite tree where
every vertex in one part of the bipartition has degree |X|, and every vertex in the other

cg(c|Star(v))−1 is in M if v ∈VX and is in N if v ∈VY . The box product of M and N is
defined as the permutation group U(M, N) acting on VY .

in T+ we label one of the edges that has those vertices as end vertices with “+” and
the other with “−”. In both examples above, we see that if one “+” edge is mapped
to a “−” edge then every “+” edge is mapped to a “−” edge. In the first example and
edge with end vertex u is mapped to an edge of the opposite sign if and only if the local
action at u has odd parity, but in the second example this only holds for vertices in T+
that are in π−1(v).
In these two examples we were playing with the homomorphism Sd →C2 in the
cases when the universal group had just one or two orbits on the associated tree. it is
straightforward to use these ideas when the groups acting at the vertices map surjectively
onto some other groups and also when we want more than two orbits on the vertex set
of the tree.
