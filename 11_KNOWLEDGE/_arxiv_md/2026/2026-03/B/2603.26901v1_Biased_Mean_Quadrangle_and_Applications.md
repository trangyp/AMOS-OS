Abstract
This paper introduces biased mean regression, estimating the biased mean, i.e., E[Y ] + x, where
x ∈R. The approach addresses a fundamental statistical problem that covers numerous appli-
cations.
For instance, estimation of factors driving portfolio loss exceeding expected loss by a
specified amount (e.g., x = $10 billion) or estimation of factors impacting a specific excess release
of radiation in the environment (nuclear safety regulations specify different severity levels).
The estimation is done by minimizing the so-called superexpectation error. We establish two
equivalence results that connect the method to popular paradigms: (i) biased mean regression
is equivalent to quantile regression for an appropriate parameterization and is equivalent to the
ordinary least squares for x = 0; (ii) in portfolio optimization, minimizing superexpectation risk,
associated with the superexpectation error, is equivalent to CVaR optimization. The approach is
computationally attractive, as minimizing the superexpectation error is reduced to linear program-
ming (LP), offering algorithmic and modeling advantages. It is a good alternative to the ordinary
least squares (OLS) regression.
The approach is based on the Risk Quadrangle (RQ) framework, which links four stochastic
functionals—error, regret, risk, and deviation—through a statistic. For the biased mean quadrangle,
the statistic is the biased mean. We study properties of the new quadrangle, such as subregularity,
and establish its relationship to the quantile quadrangle. Numerical experiments confirm theoretical
statements and illustrate the practical implications.

Introduction
Motivation.
One of the fundamental goals in statistical modeling is to identify covariates driving
significant excesses from baseline behavior—that is, to explain overperformance or underperformance
relative to E[Y ] by a user-chosen margin x in the units of Y . Let Y denote the response (regressant)
and X = (X1, . . . , Xd) the covariates (regressors, factors). The question we target is:
Which factors in X explain why Y exceeds (or falls short of) its mean E[Y ] by a fixed margin x > 0?
This problem recurs across various domains. Here are some examples.
• Finance: with Y a portfolio loss and its driving factors X, which factors are responsible for losses
exceeding the expected loss by x = $10 billion?

• Material design: Let Y denote a target performance metric for a candidate material (e.g., the
ultimate tensile strength (UTS) measured in MPa) and let X collect design and process features

patients beating the average recovery by a clinically meaningful margin?
• Supply chain and operations: with Y delivery time and X capturing supplier reliability and
transportation costs, which factors explain deliveries better than average by x = 2 hours?
A standard tool for biased estimates is quantile regression [Koenker and Bassett, 1978], which
models conditional quantiles indexed by a confidence level α ∈(0, 1). While powerful, quantile re-
gression does not specify how far above (or below) the mean the estimate should be (by design, the
quantile regression estimates quantiles). By contrast, the biased mean regression (developed in this
paper) specifies the margin of practical interest x directly in the units of Y . The bias is specified
relative to the expectation E[Y ]. This yields estimates that are easy to understand (“factors leading
to under/overperformance the average by exactly x units”) and easy to align with policy or design
constraints.
Importantly, the biased-mean and quantile regression are not in conflict. We show that for each
bias x there exists a corresponding quantile level α that produces the same estimate, and conversely
(under mild conditions).
Thus, the biased mean approach can be seen as a reparameterization of
quantile regression: the user selects a margin x in target units rather than a probability level α. This
reparameterization offers an alternative modeling perspective, and through the RQ integrates with risk
and deviation measures used in portfolio optimization, management, and control.
Risk Quadrangle Basics.
Risk management, statistical estimation, and stochastic programming
are often treated as distinct disciplines; yet, many of their core concepts — random losses, objective
functionals, and constraints — share the same mathematical structure and properties. The Risk Quad-
rangle (RQ) [Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2013] provides a unified framework for combining four stochastic
functionals–error, regret, risk, and deviation linked by a functional, called statistic. Further are some
important theoretical and practical implications of the RQ framework.
1. Construction of efficient numerical algorithms for optimization of elements of the quadrangles,
in particular risk measures [Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2000; Rockafellar et al., 2008; Rockafellar
and Royset, 2014, 2015; Malandii et al., 2024; Assness et al., 2026].
2. Various equivalence statements related to optimization of elements of quadrangles – for example,
the equivalence between expectile and CVaR portfolio optimization [Malandii et al., 2024], be-
tween ν-SVR and ε-SVR [Malandii and Uryasev, 2022], and between CVaR regressions based on
CVaR2 and Rockafellar errors [Golodnikov et al., 2019]. For a detailed review on risk-adaptive
approaches in stochastic programming, see [Royset, 2025].
3. The relation between the error and deviation functionals (see Definition 2.8) implies the error-
shaping decomposition of regression [Rockafellar et al., 2008]. This decomposition establishes
the equivalence between error minimization and deviation minimization with a constraint on
the associated statistic, and is a part of the general [Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2013, Regression
Theorem].

Contributions.
This paper introduces a new quadrangle — the biased mean quadrangle — and
investigates its mathematical properties and practical implications. Its statistic is the mean plus a real
parameter (bias). The associated risk measure — the superexpectation risk —generalizes the well-known
mean-absolute risk [Shapiro et al., 2014]. The corresponding error, termed the superexpectation error,
is a stochastic compound functional that balances the averages of the positive and negative parts of
the random variable. As in other quadrangles, the regret and deviation components are obtained from
the risk and error via the standard RQ identities (see Definition 2.8).
In particular, we emphasize the following results.
1. Reparametrization of quantile regression. We show that biased-mean regression using the super-
expectation error is a reparametrization of quantile regression. In particular, quantiles can be
represented as biased means with a prescribed bias x ∈R. We prove the equivalence of these
two regressions; see Theorem 4.1.
2. OLS as an LP. When the bias parameter is zero (x = 0), the resulting (piecewise-linear) mean
quadrangle is regular–and, in fact, coherent (see Corollary 3.4). This quadrangle serves as a
coherent alternative to the standard mean quadrangle [Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2013, Exam-
ple 1′]. In particular, we show the equivalence between OLS and the regression induced by the
superexpectation error of this new mean quadrangle (piecewise-linear version), which we term
the superexpectation regression (see Theorem 4.3). Moreover, the superexpectation regression
admits an LP formulation, offering computational and modeling advantages (See Subsection 4.3
and Subsection 5.4).
3. Equivalence of deviation minimization. We prove the equivalence between CVaR deviation and
superexpectation deviation minimization (see Proposition 3.7) and showcase its practical signif-
icance in the case of portfolio optimization (see Example 3.1 and Subsection 5.1).
4. Subregularity. We prove that the biased mean quadrangle is subregular (see Definition 2.9 and
Proposition 3.1). The notion of subregularity, first proposed in Rockafellar [2024]; Grechuk et al.
[2026], extends the concept of regularity introduced in Rockafellar and Uryasev [2013] and can be
viewed as an alternative to the well-known notion of coherence [Artzner et al., 1999]. The biased
mean quadrangle also provides, to our knowledge, the first example of a subregular quadrangle
with concrete practical relevance.
Outline.
Section 2 provides definitions and theorems needed for the subsequent sections. Section 3
introduces the biased mean quadrangle and examines its properties — including subregularity, its re-
lationship to the quantile quadrangle, and related implications. Section 4 formulates and analyzes
generalized regression problems within the biased mean quadrangle framework. In particular, it es-
tablishes the equivalence between quantile regression and biased mean regression. We then specialize
to the zero-bias case and show equivalence between OLS and superexpectation regression (i.e., biased
mean regression with zero bias). The section concludes with a discussion of sparse regression. Section 5
conducts several numerical case studies showcasing the effectiveness and flexibility of the framework.
Section 6 concludes the paper.

Rockafellar and Uryasev [2013] introduced a novel concept known as the Risk Quadrangle (RQ),

6
Conclusions
This paper introduced the biased mean quadrangle (BMQ) and developed an associated family of
estimation and decision tools centered on a biased mean statistic. The BMQ’s risk—the superexpectation
risk—extends mean-upper-semirisk, while its error—the superexpectation error—balances averages of
the positive and negative parts of a random variable Y in a tunable, directionally asymmetric manner.
These choices yield models that directly target practically meaningful events (exceeding or falling
short of the mean by a margin x in the units of Y ) and integrate with regret and deviation via the RQ
identities (Definition 2.8).
On the theoretical side, we established: (i) subregularity of the BMQ (Definition 2.9, Proposi-
tion 3.1), thereby providing, to our knowledge, the first subregular quadrangle with concrete applied
relevance; (ii) equivalence of deviation minimization between CVaR and superexpectation deviations
(Proposition 3.7), with implications for portfolio optimization (Example 3.1); and (iii) a reparameteriza-
tion of quantile regression in terms of biased means, proving that superexpectation-based biased-mean
regression and quantile regression yield the same solutions for an appropriate mapping between the
margin x and the quantile level α (Theorem 4.1). As a special case, setting x = 0 recovers a coherent,
piecewise-linear mean quadrangle for which the induced superexpectation regression is equivalent to
OLS and admits an LP formulation (Corollary 3.4, Theorem 4.3), offering modeling transparency and
computational advantages.
From a practical standpoint, the unit-calibrated parameterization by x enhances interpretability
(“factors of beating the average exactly by x”) and alignment with domain policies. Our numerical
experiments further indicate that, in small-sample high-dimensional regimes (n ≪d), the SE-based
sparse regression is particularly effective, while for larger n the MSE-based formulation is solved to
proven optimality more rapidly by off-the-shelf solvers. Altogether, the BMQ provides a principled
bridge between statistical estimation and risk-sensitive optimization.
