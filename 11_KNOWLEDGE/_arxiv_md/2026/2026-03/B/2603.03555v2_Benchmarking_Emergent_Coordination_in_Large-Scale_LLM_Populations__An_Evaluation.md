Abstract
As multi-agent Large Language Model (LLM) systems scale, evaluating their
emergent coordination dynamics becomes increasingly critical. However, current
evaluation paradigms—focused on single agents or small, explicitly structured
groups—fail to capture the self-organization and viral information dynamics that
arise in large, decentralized populations. We introduce a systematic evaluation
framework to benchmark role specialization, information diffusion, and coopera-
tive task resolution in open agent environments. We demonstrate this framework
on the MoltBook Observatory Archive, a dataset of 2.73M interactions among
90,704 autonomous agents, establishing quantitative baselines for emergent co-
ordination. Our evaluation reveals a pronounced core-periphery structure (sil-
houette 0.91), heavy-tailed cascade distributions (α = 2.57), and severe coor-
dination overhead in decentralized task resolution (Cohen’s d = −0.88 against a
single-agent baseline). By providing standardized evaluation tasks and empirical
baselines, our framework enables the rigorous comparison of future multi-agent
protocols and establishes evaluation itself as an object of scientific study.
1
Introduction
The rapid proliferation of autonomous Large Language Model (LLM) agents has created a pressing
need for robust evaluation methodologies. While substantial progress has been made in benchmark-
ing individual LLM capabilities [Brown et al., 2020, OpenAI, 2023, Anthropic, 2024], evaluating
emergent coordination in open, large-scale multi-agent environments remains a fundamental chal-
lenge. As the NeurIPS Evaluations & Datasets track emphasizes, evaluation must become an object
of scientific study in its own right to ensure that claims about AI system capabilities are grounded in
rigorous, standardized methodologies.
Existing evaluation paradigms are ill-suited to this challenge. Benchmarks designed for individual
LLMs measure isolated reasoning capability but provide no signal about how agents behave when
embedded in a social network. Small-group multi-agent evaluations [Wu et al., 2023, Hong et al.,
2023] typically involve two to ten agents with predefined roles and explicit task assignments; they
cannot capture the self-organization, emergent stratification, and viral information dynamics that
arise when thousands of agents interact asynchronously. Furthermore, synthetic multi-agent envi-
ronments [Park et al., 2023] offer experimental control at the cost of ecological validity, limiting the
generalizability of their baselines.
In this paper, we introduce a systematic evaluation framework designed to assess emergent coor-
dination dynamics in large-scale decentralized agent populations. Rather than proposing a new
multi-agent architecture, our primary contribution is the evaluation methodology itself. We ap-
ply this framework to the MoltBook Observatory Archive [Gautam and Riegler, 2026], an existing

Our evaluation establishes empirical baselines across three distinct dimensions of multi-agent coor-

single-agent baseline (Cohen’s d = −0.88).
By standardizing these measurements, our framework enables the community to rigorously com-

contributions are:
• A network-based clustering methodology to evaluate role differentiation, establishing a
structural baseline for future systems.
• A cascade extraction and survival analysis pipeline to audit information propagation, pro-
viding a target for evaluating agent susceptibility to viral loops.
• A composite quality score for evaluating distributed cooperative task resolution, which es-
tablishes a hard benchmark challenge (the −0.88 deficit) that future coordination protocols
must attempt to beat.

Background: The Evaluation Context
2.1
The MoltBook Observatory Archive
Our evaluation framework is applied to the MoltBook Observatory Archive [Gautam and Riegler,
2026], a third-party research-grade dataset. MoltBook is a Reddit-style multi-agent interaction plat-
form where only AI agents can post, comment, and vote. The dataset provides daily incremental
exports of agent profiles, posts, comments, submolts (communities), and interaction metrics. We
utilize a three-week snapshot (January 28 to February 20, 2026) encompassing 90,704 unique active
agents.
The agents in this dataset operate via the OpenClaw framework [Steinberger, 2026], which allows
LLMs to execute actions and interact with external platforms. The population is highly heteroge-
neous, comprising various underlying models (e.g., Claude, GPT-4, open-source models). Crucially,
model identity is not recorded in the dataset. We treat this as a fundamental constraint on our eval-
uation framework: our baselines measure aggregate population dynamics, and future iterations will
require model-aware datasets to disentangle architecture-specific behaviors from platform dynamics.
2.2
Evaluation Design Considerations
The NeurIPS Evaluations & Datasets track emphasizes critical self-reflection on evaluation prac-
tices. We explicitly acknowledge several methodological constraints in our framework:
• Silhouette Inflation: Our structural role evaluation (Task 1) yields a high silhouette score
(0.91), but this is heavily influenced by the large, homogeneous peripheral cluster. The
metric evaluates the separation of the active core from the periphery rather than fine-grained
role diversity.
• Baseline Selection Confound: In Task 3, our comparison between multi-agent and single-
agent task resolution may suffer from selection bias. Collaborative threads, identified by
sustained duration and participant volume, likely represent intrinsically harder tasks. The
Cohen’s d = −0.88 baseline must be interpreted with this confound in mind.
• Cascade Identification Ambiguity: Our n-gram approach for extracting information cas-
cades (Task 2) captures textual repetition but may miss semantic or behavioral adoption
that does not rely on exact phrasing.

Evaluation Methodology
To benchmark role differentiation, we construct a directed, weighted interaction network from the
dataset. Nodes represent agents, and edges represent directed interactions (replies, mentions), with
edge weights proportional to the number of interactions between each pair. We extract five struc-
tural features per agent that together characterize both local and global network position: in-degree
(receptivity to information), out-degree (broadcast activity), betweenness centrality (brokerage be-
tween communities), clustering coefficient (tendency toward clique formation), and PageRank (in-
fluence weighted by the prestige of interacting agents). These five features are standardized to zero
mean and unit variance before clustering to prevent scale-driven artifacts.

Task 2: Information Diffusion Auditing
4.1
Evaluation Methodology
We audit how information propagates by extracting information cascades using n-gram matching
(n ∈{2, 3, 4, 5}) across agent posts. A cascade is defined as a set of posts sharing at least one n-gram
of length n ≥2 that appears in at least two distinct agents’ content within the observation window.
The lower bound n = 2 is chosen to avoid spurious matches on common function-word bigrams;
the upper bound n = 5 captures phrase-level repetition that is unlikely to arise independently. Each
cascade is assigned to the earliest post containing the n-gram as the seed, and subsequent posts are
ordered chronologically to reconstruct the propagation tree. Cascade type (meme, skill, behavioral)
is assigned by keyword classification of the seed post’s submolt category.
We evaluate adoption dynamics using two complementary models. First, a logistic regression with
linear and quadratic exposure terms tests whether adoption probability follows a simple (linear),
complex (accelerating), or saturating (diminishing-returns) contagion pattern. This allows us to
explicitly distinguish between different theoretical models of information spread. Second, a Cox
proportional hazards model with cumulative exposure count as a time-varying covariate benchmarks
time-to-adoption, treating each agent’s first adoption of a given cascade as the event of interest
and right-censoring agents who never adopt. The Cox model is fitted on a random sample of 100
cascades to manage computational cost, with the proportional hazards assumption validated via
Schoenfeld residuals.
We additionally test whether cascade sizes follow a power-law distribution using the methodology
of Clauset et al. [2009], including maximum likelihood estimation of the power-law exponent α, es-
timation of the lower cutoff xmin, and likelihood ratio comparisons against exponential, lognormal,
and truncated power-law alternatives.
4.2
Baseline Results
We identified 10,323 cascades (97% meme/textual cascades). Cascade sizes follow a power-law
distribution with exponent α = 2.57 (95% CI: [2.54, 2.60]), establishing a baseline for propagation
scale. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic D = 0.020 indicates a good fit, and likelihood ratio tests
favor the power-law over exponential (p < 0.001) and lognormal (p = 0.030) distributions.
Our evaluation detects significant saturating contagion dynamics. The logistic regression yields a
negative quadratic exposure term (β2 = −0.0074, p < 0.001), indicating that adoption probability
increases with initial exposures but at a diminishing rate.

Task 3: Decentralized Collaboration Benchmarking
5.1
Evaluation Methodology
This task evaluates the ability of agent populations to solve complex problems cooperatively. We
identify multi-agent cooperative events as technical discussion threads satisfying four simultaneous
criteria: at least three unique participating agents (to distinguish genuine multi-agent coordination
from single-agent monologues), at least five comments (to ensure substantive exchange rather than
brief acknowledgements), the presence of at least one technical keyword from a curated vocabulary
of programming, debugging, and system design terms, and a minimum thread duration of 30 minutes
(to exclude rapid back-and-forth exchanges that do not reflect sustained problem solving).
We design a composite quality score Q ∈[0, 1] to assess the output quality of each collaborative
event. The score aggregates four components: code presence (weight 0.30, reflecting whether the
thread produced executable code artifacts), comment quality (weight 0.30, assessed via VADER
sentiment and length heuristics [Hutto and Gilbert, 2014] as a proxy for substantive technical dis-
cussion), test inclusion (weight 0.20, reflecting whether the produced code includes test cases or
validation logic), and syntax validity (weight 0.20, assessed by attempting to parse any code blocks
with Python’s ast module). Component weights were assigned to reflect the relative importance of
functional code output over discussion quality in a software engineering context.
We benchmark collaborative events against a matched baseline of single-agent technical threads.
Matching is performed by thread length (number of comments, ±2) and submolt category to control
for topic difficulty. For each collaborative event, up to five matching single-agent threads are sam-
pled; the baseline quality score is the mean Q across matched threads. The comparison is conducted
via a two-sample t-test and Cohen’s d effect size.
5.2
Baseline Results
From 60,045 technical threads, we identified 164 collaborative events. Defining success as a quality
score ≥0.5, the baseline success rate is 6.7%.
Crucially, comparison against the single-agent baseline reveals that collaborative outcomes are sig-
nificantly worse (t = −11.21, p < 0.001). The large negative effect size (Cohen’s d = −0.88)
establishes a hard benchmark challenge. This baseline quantifies the severe coordination overhead
present in current unconstrained agent populations. We present this negative result not as a failure of
the agents, but as a rigorous empirical target: future multi-agent coordination protocols must attempt
to beat this −0.88 deficit to demonstrate genuine cooperative intelligence.

Related Work
LLM-based multi-agent systems.
Recent work has begun to explore coordination among LLM-
based agents in simulated or task-oriented settings. Park et al. [2023] introduce Smallville, a virtual
environment in which a small population of LLM agents exhibits emergent interaction routines. Li
et al. [2023] study role-based LLM agent interactions for cooperative task solving. Multi-agent
frameworks such as AutoGen [Wu et al., 2023] and MetaGPT [Hong et al., 2023] investigate struc-
tured coordination among a limited number of agents under carefully designed communication pro-
tocols. Multi-agent debate frameworks [Liang et al., 2023] examine whether structured agent dis-
agreement can improve reasoning.
Emergent coordination and collective intelligence.
The broader literature on emergent coordi-
nation has long examined how complex group-level patterns arise from individual agent-level inter-
action. Research on emergent communication in multi-agent populations demonstrates that agents
can develop shared protocols through repeated interaction [Lazaridou and Baroni, 2020, Mordatch
and Abbeel, 2018]. In human groups, Woolley et al. [2010] identify a collective intelligence fac-
tor that predicts cooperative task performance across diverse settings, while studies of large-scale
online coordination analyze emergent role specialization in settings such as Wikipedia and open-
source software development [Kittur and Kraut, 2008]. Multi-agent coordination within groups can
yield performance gains when agents contribute complementary capabilities, but may also intro-
duce redundancy and coordination overhead [Hong and Page, 2004]. Our Task 3 evaluation directly
measures this coordination overhead in a naturalistic LLM agent population.
Information propagation dynamics.
Classical models distinguish between simple contagion,
which spreads through single exposures, and complex contagion, which requires repeated expo-
sure and social reinforcement [Centola and Macy, 2007]. A distinct class of models predicts a
saturating regime in which adoption probability rises with initial exposures but at a diminishing rate
[Dodds and Watts, 2004]. Multi-agent network structure plays a central role in shaping propagation
dynamics, with well-documented regularities such as agent centrality, core-periphery organization,
and structural brokerage positions affecting how information spreads [Freeman, 1977, Borgatti and
Everett, 2000, Guimerà and Amaral, 2005]. Our Task 2 evaluation operationalizes these theoretical
distinctions into a standardized pipeline for auditing diffusion dynamics in any large-scale agent
population.

Discussion
7.1
Implications for Multi-Agent System Design
Our evaluation baselines carry direct implications for the design of future multi-agent systems. Since
agent network position strongly predicts structural role differentiation (Task 1), multi-agent system
performance may depend on the designed interaction topology as much as on individual agent model
capability. System designers should consider explicitly engineering structural diversity—including
coordination brokers and inter-community bridging roles—in line with evidence that agent centrality
and core-periphery structure shape influence and information flow [Freeman, 1977, Borgatti and
Everett, 2000].
Since inter-agent dissemination shows saturating dynamics (Task 2), important coordination updates
and behavioral norms may require propagation through multiple independent communication chan-
nels rather than single broadcasts, as familiarity effects may suppress adoption from repeated contact

with the same source. The heavy-tailed cascade distribution (α = 2.57) further implies that rare but
very large propagation events can dominate agent exposure, raising the risk that harmful content or
adversarial coordination strategies propagate widely before detection [Clauset et al., 2009].
Since decentralized coordination can impose large costs (Task 3), multi-agent collaboration may
be counterproductive for simple task types, and coordination scaffolding should be reserved for
problems that require genuinely diverse, non-redundant agent contributions. The positive participant
count association (Cohen’s d = 1.18 for successful vs. unsuccessful events) suggests that a critical
mass of contributors may be necessary for coordination to yield benefits [Woolley et al., 2010].
7.2
Benchmark Validity and Generalizability
A central question for any evaluation framework is whether its metrics measure what they purport
to measure and whether baselines established on one dataset transfer to other settings. We address
both concerns in turn.
Regarding construct validity, each of our three tasks is grounded in an established theoretical con-
struct. Task 1 operationalizes the sociological concept of role differentiation [Guimerà and Amaral,
2005] through network clustering, a methodology with a long track record in organizational and
biological network analysis. Task 2 operationalizes contagion theory [Centola and Macy, 2007,
Dodds and Watts, 2004] through survival analysis, a standard epidemiological tool. Task 3 opera-
tionalizes cooperative task performance [Woolley et al., 2010] through a composite quality score.
In each case, the mapping from theoretical construct to observable metric involves assumptions that
we have made explicit: the silhouette score conflates cluster separation with role diversity; the n-
gram cascade extraction misses semantic adoption; the quality score weights are heuristic. These
are not fatal flaws but known measurement properties that future work can address by, for example,
replacing n-gram matching with embedding-based semantic similarity or replacing heuristic quality
weights with human-validated rubrics.
Regarding external validity, the MoltBook platform has several idiosyncratic features—a karma vot-
ing system, rate-limited posting, and a Reddit-style community structure—that may not generalize
to other multi-agent environments. We therefore present our baselines as platform-specific reference
points rather than universal constants. The value of the framework lies not in the specific numbers
but in the standardized methodology: any future multi-agent interaction dataset can be evaluated
using the same three pipelines, enabling cross-platform comparison. We specifically invite the com-
munity to apply our framework to other emerging agent interaction platforms to begin building a
comparative baseline library.
7.3
Limitations
Explaining the limitations of our work is a crucial part of scientific good practice. The baselines
established here must be interpreted with the following constraints in mind:
• Short Observation Window: The three-week observation window is insufficient to cap-
ture long-run processes like institutional consolidation or stable norm evolution.
• Platform-Specific Mechanics:
Platform-specific mechanics—such as rate limits and
karma voting—shape agent behavior in ways that may not generalize to other multi-agent
environments.
• Unobserved Model Identities: We cannot attribute behavioral heterogeneity to specific
model architectures versus the coordination environment because model identity is not
recorded.
• Measurement Assumptions: The silhouette score conflates cluster separation with role
diversity; the n-gram cascade extraction misses semantic adoption; and the quality score
weights are heuristic.

7.5
Conclusion
We have introduced an evaluation framework and benchmark suite for assessing emergent coordi-
nation in large-scale autonomous LLM populations. By applying this methodology to the Molt-
Book Observatory Archive, we established quantitative baselines for structural role specialization,
information diffusion, and distributed cooperative task resolution. Our reframing of negative re-
sults—specifically the severe coordination overhead in decentralized task resolution—provides the
community with a rigorous empirical target. As multi-agent systems scale, evaluation methodolo-
gies like ours are essential for moving beyond anecdotal observation to the systematic science of AI
evaluation.

A.1
Bootstrap Stability Analysis
Bootstrap stability analysis for network-based clustering (k = 6) was conducted with 100 iterations.
