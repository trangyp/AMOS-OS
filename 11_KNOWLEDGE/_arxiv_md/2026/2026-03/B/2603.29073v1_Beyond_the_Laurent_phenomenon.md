Beyond the Laurent phenomenon
Andrei Zabolotskii
Abstract
In a cluster algebra, a subset of initial cluster variables can be specialised in such a way
that all elements of the resulting algebra become polynomial in the remaining variables.
1
Introduction
Let AQ ⊂Q(x1, . . . , xn) be the cluster algebra associated with a quiver Q. The celebrated Lau-
rent phenomenon [6] is the surprising fact that all elements of AQ are actually Laurent polynomi-
als in x1, . . . , xn with integer coefficients, i.e., AQ is a subalgebra of Z[x1, . . . , xn, x−1
1 , . . . , x−1
n ].
In this note, we prove another fact which takes the Laurent phenomenon to the next level in
the following sense: in AQ, when some of the initial cluster variables x1, . . . , xn are specialised in
a certain controlled way, all elements of the resulting algebra become polynomial (not Laurent
polynomial) in certain initial cluster variables. This is made concrete in Theorem 1.
We give some standard definitions from cluster algebra theory in section 2 (which a reader
familiar with this area can skip) and prove our result in section 3.
As an application, in
section 4 we generalise the known description of Conway–Coxeter friezes by cluster algebras of
type An and sketch a proof that all not-necessarily-positive tame integer frieze patterns arise
from cluster algebras of type An specialised this way.
2
Cluster algebras
Let Q be a finite quiver without loops or oriented 2-cycles. We identify the set Q0 of vertices
of Q with the numbers {1, . . . , n}. For each vertex j ∈Q0, we introduce a variable xj; together
(x1, . . . , xn) form the initial cluster and the individual xjs are the initial cluster variables.
The cluster algebra AQ associated with Q is a Z-algebra generated by certain elements of
Z[x1, . . . , xn, x−1
1 , . . . , x−1
n ] (details below), called cluster variables, with an additional structure
on the set of cluster variables: they are grouped into overlapping sets called clusters. A cluster
and the corresponding quiver together are called a seed [9].
Since their introduction in [6], cluster algebras have attracted immense interest because of
numerous connections to other areas of mathematics, from quiver representations to integrable
systems. While it is impossible to review all these connections in this short note, this section
aims to show what is the Laurent phenomenon and why it is considered impressive.
For a chosen vertex j ∈Q0, a procedure called mutation µj can be applied to a seed. Under
µj, the set of vertices of the quiver Q0 is unchanged, while the arrows change as follows: all
arrows incident to the vertex j change direction; then all directed 2-paths with central vertex j
are completed to triangles; then any resulting oriented 2-cycles are removed. As for the action

One important family of cluster algebras is the cluster algebras of type An, where n is a
positive integer. The cluster algebra of type An is AQ where Q is a path with n vertices and
any orientation of arrows; all such paths are mutation equivalent, i.e. related by sequences of
mutations.

3
The polyamorous phenomenon
Specialisations of initial cluster variables to 1s have been studied since [7], most recently in [10,
Section 4.2] in the context of friezes of cluster algebras. However, the following definition also
allows specialisation to −1s.
Definition 1. A specialised cluster algebra is the Z-algebra arising from a cluster algebra
associated with a quiver Q by choosing an initial cluster and specialising some of the initial
cluster variables to 1s or −1s. A specialised cluster algebra is associated with a partially labelled
quiver, where the vertices of Q corresponding to specialised cluster variables are labelled by the
values assigned to these variables.
Typically, a specialised cluster algebra does not have the structure of a cluster algebra; it is
just a ring generated by certain Laurent polynomials, unless all specialisations are to 1s (and
sometimes to −1s, in certain degenerate cases) [1, Lemma 6.6].
A specialisation of a cluster algebra is determined by the chosen initial cluster and special-
isation relations of the form xj1 = a1, xj2 = a2, . . . (e.g. x2 = 1, x5 = −1), which we will
collectively refer to as σ, with the specialised cluster algebra denoted AQ|σ and the special-
isation of a single element x ∈AQ denoted x|σ. We identify specialisation relations σ with
the corresponding partial labelling of vertices of Q. The initial cluster variables not assigned
a value by specialisation will be referred to simply as variables in AQ|σ.

Remark 1 (motivation for Definition 1). Choosing an initial cluster and then specialising
all initial cluster variables to 1s is a standard operation on cluster algebras giving rise to so-
called unital frieze patterns, in particular to all Conway–Coxeter friezes; see Theorem 2 below.
Specialising only to the units of Z guarantees that the elements of the specialised cluster algebra
always remain Laurent polynomials with integer coefficients; cf. [8, Remark 2.2]. However,
generalising Definition 1 to specialisation to any nonzero values would make sense too, and
there exist specialisations that also ensure the integer coefficients, for example, specialising the
cluster algebra of type A2 with x1 = 2, x2 = 3 results simply in the ring Z.
Remark 2. The definitions and results of this section can be formulated in the more general
setting of cluster algebras of geometric type associated with skew-symmetrisable matrices, which
have been introduced in [6].
Definition 2. In a specialised cluster algebra, a variable is polyamorous if all elements of the
algebra are polynomial in that variable. The vertex in the partially labelled quiver correspond-
ing to a polyamorous variable is also called polyamorous. The specialised cluster algebra itself
is called polyamorous when all variables in it are polyamorous.
The name indicates that these variables “love polynomials”.
The set of vertices adjacent to a given vertex in a quiver together with the vertex itself is
called the neighbourhood of that vertex.
Definition 3. In a partially labelled quiver associated with a specialised cluster algebra, a
neighbourhood of a vertex v is a polycule if v is not labelled, each neighbour of v is labelled,
and the number of arrows connecting v with the vertices labelled with −1 is odd.

Corollary 1. If Q has at least one pair of vertices j, k ∈Q0 with an odd number of arrows
between them then there exist specialisation relations σ such that AQ|σ is polyamorous and the
set of its variables {xj1, . . . , xjℓ} is nonempty. Moreover, AQ|σ = Z[xj1, . . . , xjℓ].
Proof. Label k by −1 and the rest of the vertices in the neighbourhood of j (except for j
itself) by 1 or −1 such that the neighbourhood becomes a polycule (e.g. all by 1).
Label
some other vertices (but not j) by ±1 in an arbitrary way. Then also label all vertices whose
neighbourhoods are not polycules by ±1 in an arbitrary way (this doesn’t affect polycules). All
vertices that remained unlabelled, including j, are polyamorous due to Theorem 1. Labels then
determine the specialisation relations. Finally, since AQ|σ is polyamorous, it is a subalgebra of
the polynomial algebra Z[xj1, . . . , xjℓ], and since the variables of AQ|σ belong to the set of its
generators, AQ|σ contains the entire polynomial algebra.
Corollary 2. Suppose Q has no multiple arrows and no isolated vertices (e.g. any Dynkin
quiver of type A, D, E except for A1) and A is a subset of Q0 with pairwise disjoint neighbour-
hoods. Then there exists a labelling σ of the vertices Q0 \ A which makes all vertices from A
polyamorous.
Proof. The neighbourhood of each vertex from A can be labelled independently since they are
all disjoint. The conditions on Q ensure that each neighbourhood can be made a polycule,
which allows us to apply the theorem.

We will now consider tame frieze patterns over Z, which generalise Conway–Coxeter friezes
by allowing zero and negative entries in the nontrivial rows; they are also known as [not-
necessarily-positive] integral friezes. The polyamorous phenomenon enables us to establish a
connection between tame frieze patterns over Z and cluster algebras of type An generalising
the analogous result for Conway–Coxeter friezes that was outlined above.
Theorem 2. Consider the cluster algebra of type An, for some n. Specialise it such that the
resulting specialised cluster algebra is polyamorous. Specialise further the remaining variables
to any values from Z. The resulting values of cluster variables form a tame frieze pattern over
Z. Moreover, any tame frieze pattern over Z arises this way.
Outline of proof. It is the last part of the theorem that is difficult. In fact, any frieze
pattern over Z arises from a closed path P in the Farey graph F [11, 12]. It can be shown

that any closed path in F of length greater than 4 has a pair of non-consecutive vertices that
are adjacent in F. This allows us to associate with P a polygon dissected into triangles and
quadrilaterals by a (non-unique) maximal collection of diagonals M.
The diagonals in M
correspond to the values 1 or −1 in the nontrivial rows of the frieze and are labelled by these
values. It turns out that any quadrilateral R in the dissection corresponds to a polycule in the
associated quiver and to a point of self-intersection in P, so one pair of opposite vertices in R
corresponds to a 0 in the frieze and another to some number that depends on P. Adding the
diagonal between that latter pair of vertices, labelled by the corresponding frieze element, to
the dissection, and doing this for each rectangle R, we obtain the dissection of a polygon into
triangles by labelled diagonals (this is similar to the combinatorial model introduced in [5]).
Then the diagonals in the dissection determine the choice of the cluster, the diagonals in M
correspond to the initially specialised cluster variables, and the rest of the diagonals correspond
to the polyamorous variables.
The full proof of Theorem 2, together with the necessary background information that was
omitted here, will be presented in [13].
