Beyond bouba/kiki: Multidimensional semantic signals are deeply 
woven into the fabric of natural language 
Gexin Zhao1,* 
1Columbia University, New York, NY, USA.  
*Corresponding author. Email: gz2393@cumc.columbia.edu 
Abstract 
A foundational assumption in linguistics holds that the relationship between a word's sound and 
its meaning is arbitrary. Accumulating evidence from sound symbolism challenges this view, yet 
no study has systematically mapped the multidimensional semantic profile of every phonological 
unit within a language. Here we show that individual letter-phonemes in English carry 
structured, multidimensional semantic signals. Using a minimal-pair paradigm spanning all 220 
pairwise letter contrasts, three large language models independently recover consistent 
phoneme–meaning associations across nine perceptual dimensions. These associations are 
systematically predicted by articulatory-phonetic features, with manner and place of articulation 
mapping onto distinct semantic dimensions. Behavioral data from English speakers confirm 
these patterns at rates well above chance (80.8%), and preliminary cross-linguistic evidence 
from five typologically diverse languages suggests that core mappings generalize beyond 
English. Our findings indicate that sound–meaning iconicity is not an occasional curiosity but a 
pervasive, structured property of the phonological signal — one so systematic that large 
language models recover it when given only text input, without exposure to speech or 
articulation during the task.  
Keywords: sound symbolism, articulatory gesture, cross-modal correspondence, bouba/kiki, 
large language models, iconicity

INTRODUCTION 
Ferdinand de Saussure’s principle of the arbitrary sign—the claim that the relationship between 
a word’s form and its meaning is fundamentally conventional—has served as a cornerstone of 
modern linguistics for over a century 1. This principle, reinforced by Hockett’s duality of 
patterning 2, holds that individual phonemes are meaningless units that acquire significance only 
through combination into morphemes and words. Yet accumulating evidence challenges this 
view. The bouba/kiki effect 3,4 demonstrates that speakers spontaneously associate certain 
speech sounds with particular shapes. Blasi et al. 5 extended this observation to a global scale, 
documenting systematic sound–meaning associations across 6,452 languages that survived 
controls for historical relatedness and areal diffusion. 
 
Converging evidence suggests these associations have deep biological roots. The bouba/kiki 
effect is robust across 25 languages and 10 writing systems 6, and naïve baby chicks 
spontaneously match sounds to shapes 7, implicating evolutionarily ancient cross-modal 
mechanisms that predate language itself. Moreover, deaf and hard-of-hearing individuals retain 
sound-symbolic sensitivity through articulatory channels 8, suggesting that the proprioceptive 
experience of speech production—not just auditory input—grounds form–meaning 
correspondences. 
 
Despite these advances, the field remains fragmented 9,10. Most studies examine a handful of 
phoneme–meaning pairings at a time, typically along a single dimension such as size or shape. 
Although recent work has shown that sound symbolism operates across multiple perceptual 
domains 11 and that phonemes can evoke associations along several semantic dimensions 
simultaneously 12, no study has systematically mapped the full multidimensional structure of 
phonemic semantic associations across all pairwise contrasts in a language.

Here we address this gap through a converging-evidence approach that combines large-scale 
computational modeling, articulatory-phonetic prediction, and cross-linguistic human behavioral 
validation. We show that individual phonemes carry structured, multidimensional semantic 
signals: three independent large language models recover consistent phoneme–meaning 
associations from text input alone, articulatory-phonetic features predict these associations, and 
naïve listeners detect them across languages. 
RESULTS 
Study 1: Language models reveal multidimensional semantic structure in letter contrasts 
To map the landscape of letter–meaning associations, we constructed 4,427 minimal nonword 
pairs spanning all 220 pairwise letter contrasts (~20 pairs per contrast). In each pair, two 
pseudowords differed by exactly one target letter while all frame letters were held constant (e.g., 
brev/brov, contrasting e vs. o; see Methods). If sound-symbolic associations are pervasive 
enough to shape lexical statistics, they should be detectable by large language models given 
only text input—with no speech or articulatory information provided in the task 1314,15. Three large 
language models—GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4, and Gemini 2.5 Flash—independently rated 
every pseudoword on nine semantic dimensions: size, shape (round/spiky), brightness, texture 
(smooth/rough), speed, temperature, pleasantness, weight, and elevation. 
Every letter carried a distinct, multidimensional semantic profile (Fig. 1A). Aggregating pair-level 
Cohen's d values to the letter level revealed systematic patterns: i was associated with 
smallness, brightness, speed, and lightness, whereas o was associated with largeness, 
darkness, and heaviness. Principal component analysis of the 26 × 9 Cohen's d matrix yielded a 
strikingly low-dimensional structure: two components captured 87.6% of total variance. PC1 
(58.1%) separated heavy, dark letters (g, o) from light, bright ones (i, y); PC2 (29.5%) separated 
round, slow letters (m, o) from angular, fast ones (x, q) (Fig. 1B, C). The nine dimensions

showed structured covariation mirroring embodied perceptual experience (fig. S1A): size and 
weight were near-perfectly correlated (r = .95), as were brightness and elevation (r = .85). 
Pleasantness was strongly inversely related to texture/roughness (r = –.95) and moderately to 
weight (r = –.65), suggesting that the semantic space of letters inherits the evaluative structure 
of sensory experience. 
The underlying pair-level effects were pervasive. Pooling across three models, the mean 
absolute Cohen's d was 0.59 (5,940 pair–dimension–model observations), with 51% of effects 
reaching medium size (|d| > 0.5) and 27% reaching large size (|d| > 0.8). Effects were strongest 
for speed (mean |d| = 0.72), weight (0.63), and pleasantness (0.61), and weakest for 
temperature (0.34). Of 220 letter pairs, 212 (96%) produced significant differentiation on at least 
one dimension (one-sample t-test, FDR-corrected q < .05, requiring agreement by at least two 
of three models), and the typical pair differentiated on four to five dimensions simultaneously, 
with over three-quarters differentiating on three or more. 
These effects were remarkably stable across model architectures. Letter-level semantic profiles 
correlated at r = 0.87–0.91 between all model pairs (fig. S1B). Split-half reliability, assessed 
over 100 random splits of pseudoword carriers, yielded Spearman-Brown corrected r values of 
0.79 (temperature) to 0.92 (weight) across dimensions and models (fig. S1C), confirming that 
the observed effects reflect the target letter contrast itself rather than idiosyncratic properties of 
particular pseudoword frames. A dose–response pattern provided further evidence: nonword 
pairs containing two instances of the target letter produced systematically larger semantic 
differences than single-instance pairs across all nine dimensions (fig. S1D), with two-letter pairs 
showing 1.1× to 1.6× the effect magnitude, consistent with an additive model in which each 
letter occurrence contributes incrementally to a pseudoword's semantic profile.

Study 2: Pilot human behavioral validation 
To test whether naïve human perceivers detect the same phoneme–meaning associations 
identified by LLMs, we conducted a preregistered pilot study. Twenty-three native English 
speakers (19 after exclusion for attention-check failures) each judged 54 nonword pairs in a


## Page 6


Page 6 
forced-choice task (108 unique pairs across two counterbalanced sets, 12 per dimension). On 
each trial, participants saw two pseudowords and selected which better matched a given 
semantic pole (e.g., “Which sounds bigger?”), with the predicted answer determined by the 
LLM-derived profiles from Study 1. 
 
Human responses strongly confirmed the LLM predictions (Figure 2A-2B). Overall, participants 
selected the LLM-predicted nonword 80.8% of the time (chance = 50%; binomial p < 10⁻92), with 
17 of 108 pairs achieving perfect consensus. Accuracy varied across dimensions: size (89.5%), 
pleasantness and shape (both 87.7%) showed the strongest human–LLM alignment, followed 
by weight (85.1%), texture (82.5%), speed (79.8%), brightness and elevation (both 77.2%), 
while temperature (60.5%) was markedly weaker. All nine dimensions exceeded chance. 
 
The per-dimension pattern in human judgments paralleled the LLM results. Temperature—the 
weakest dimension in human accuracy—was also the weakest in the LLM data, showing the 
lowest cross-model agreement (r = 0.31–0.79), the lowest split-half reliability (ρ = 0.79), and the 
smallest mean effect size (|d| = 0.24). Conversely, dimensions grounded in well-established 
sound-symbolic correspondences—size, shape, and pleasantness—yielded the strongest 
effects in both humans and LLMs. This cross-method convergence suggests that the 
dimensional hierarchy reflects genuine variation in the strength of phoneme–meaning mappings 
rather than artifacts of either method.  
 
Study 3: Pilot cross-linguistic auditory validation 
Study 2 used written stimuli presented to English speakers, leaving open whether the observed 
effects depend on English orthographic conventions. To provide preliminary evidence for cross-
linguistic generality, we conducted a pilot study presenting nonword pairs as audio recordings to 
speakers of five typologically diverse languages.

Thirty nonword pairs selected for maximal LLM prediction stability and pronounceability were 
presented as synthesized audio stimuli (text-to-speech) to speakers of English, Spanish, 
Japanese, Chinese, and Arabic. On each trial, participants heard two nonwords contrasting a 
single phoneme pair (e.g., /b/ replaced by /z/) and selected which better matched a given 
semantic attribute (e.g., “Which feels heavier?” “Which feels sharper?”). All five language 
groups performed significantly above chance (all p < .001): English 84.2%, Spanish 77.6%, 
Japanese 77.0%, Mandarin 73.0%, and Arabic 76.7% (Fig. 2C). The consistency of above-
chance performance across five typologically unrelated languages—including tonal (Mandarin), 
pitch-accent (Japanese), and pharyngeal-consonant (Arabic) systems—provides evidence that 
the effect is not an artifact of English-specific orthographic or phonotactic knowledge.  
 
At the pair level, LLM prediction accuracy correlated significantly with human behavioral 
accuracy across the 30 pairs (r = .41, p = .024; Fig. 2D), confirming that the computational 
model captures meaningful variation in the strength of individual phoneme–meaning 
associations. However, while the overall effect magnitude was comparable across languages, 
pair-level correlations between language groups were generally low (mean r = .22, range –.25 
to .44), suggesting that the specific pairs producing the strongest effects may be modulated by 
native-language phonological experience (though these estimates should be interpreted 
cautiously given the small per-group sample sizes). Some pairs showed robust cross-linguistic 
consistency (e.g., r–x at 90–100% across all groups), while others varied substantially (e.g., j–t 
at 80–92% in English, Spanish, Japanese, and Mandarin but only 36% in Arabic). This pattern is 
consistent with a “universal core with language-specific modulation” model 5,6.

Study 4: Articulatory features predict the semantic dimensions of phoneme contrasts 
Studies 1–3 established that phonemes carry multidimensional semantic signals detectable by 
both LLMs and humans across languages. Study 4 tested whether these associations are 
grounded in articulatory phonetics. Using the PanPhon feature system 16, we extracted 
articulatory features for each letter’s canonical phoneme and built regression models predicting 
LLM-derived semantic scores from articulatory feature differences between letter pairs. 
 
The correlation matrix revealed pervasive associations between articulatory features and 
semantic dimensions (Fig. 3A). Among consonant contrasts, 87 of 135 feature–dimension 
correlations reached FDR-corrected significance (q < .05), with the sonorant feature showing 
the strongest effects on shape (r = –.64), texture (r = –.61), speed (r = –.57), and pleasantness 
(r = +.59). Place features (high, back) were strongly associated with shape and texture, and the 
continuant feature predicted weight (r = –.51), brightness (r = +.47), and elevation (r = +.45). 
Among vowel contrasts, only 9 of 45 individual correlations reached FDR-corrected significance, 
likely reflecting limited power with only 10 pairs rather than weak effects. 
 
Articulatory features predicted semantic profiles with high accuracy (Fig. 3B). For consonant 
contrasts (210 pairs), cross-validated R2 values ranged from .56 (temperature) to .84 (weight). 
For vowel contrasts (10 pairs), prediction accuracy was even higher (R2 = .79–.98). Vowel 
prediction accuracy was high across all dimensions, consistent with the well-established role of 
vowel space in sound symbolism 17. 
 
Feature class analysis revealed a systematic division of labor (Fig. 3C). For consonant 
contrasts, manner of articulation (sonorant, continuant, nasal) was the dominant predictor of 
weight (ΔR2 = .68), size (ΔR2 = .58), and brightness (ΔR2 = .56), while place of articulation

contributed most to size (ΔR2 = .44) and texture (ΔR2 = .33). Laryngeal features (voicing) made 
a notable contribution only to size (ΔR2 = .39) and weight (ΔR2 = .24). 
 
Representative scatter plots illustrate these feature–dimension mappings at the pair level (Fig. 
3D): sonorant contrasts predict shape (r = –.64), manner composites predict pleasantness (r 
= .66), place features predict temperature (r = –.54), and vowel height predicts size (r = –.97). 
These articulatory predictions were validated against seven classical hypotheses from the 
literature (Fig. 3E)3,18–23. Among consonant predictions, 14 of 16 were directionally consistent; 
among vowel predictions, 5 of 6 were consistent. Five of seven hypotheses—including the 
bouba/kiki shape distinction 3,6, pleasantness–sonority mapping 19, and weight–frequency 
correspondence 20—were strongly supported. The few inconsistencies arose because we 
separated consonant and vowel effects. When evaluated at the aggregate level, mirroring the 
methodology of the original studies, all 15 classic findings were directionally consistent with the 
LLM-derived associations (Fig. S2) 4,17–19,21–27.

DISCUSSION  
Our results suggest that sound–meaning iconicity extends well beyond the classical bouba/kiki 
contrast to encompass a broad range of phonemic distinctions and perceptual dimensions. 
The phoneme–meaning associations we observe exhibit coherent low-dimensional structure: 
two principal components explain most of the variance, and the dimensional clustering parallels 
the correlational structure of everyday perceptual experience. Study 4 showed that this structure 
is grounded in articulatory phonetics: manner of articulation dominates predictions of weight, 
size, and brightness, while place features contribute most to shape and texture, a finding 
consistent with Winter et al.'s 28 demonstration that manner of articulation (trilling) drives texture 
perception.  
 
Previous work has documented individual correspondences, vowel height with size 17, 
consonant manner with shape 3,27, trilling with roughness 28, but whether they reflect a unified 
system has remained unclear 9,10. Our results suggest that they do: every one of the 26 letters 
carries semantic information across multiple dimensions simultaneously, with manner and place 
of articulation projecting onto distinct but overlapping semantic domains, potentially extending 
even to abstract concepts such as time 29. This provides the first quantitative support for Sidhu 
et al.'s 12 concept of phoneme "pluripotentiality" across all pairwise contrasts in a language. This 
is also consistent with Lacey et al. 11 and Kumar et al. 30, who showed that distinct acoustic 
feature combinations underlie sound symbolism in different perceptual domains; our results 
suggest that these domain-specific combinations trace back to articulatory features. Together, 
these patterns reframe sound symbolism from a collection of isolated curiosities to a structured 
multidimensional system. Rather than a crude mapping in which "round sounds = everything 
soft and large," each semantic dimension extracts a different weighting of articulatory features, 
much as different visual pathways extract color, motion, and form from a single retinal image.

Page 13 
The inter-dimensional correlations we observe are not arbitrary but mirror the structure of 
embodied experience. Heavy objects tend to be large; bright environments tend to be elevated; 
smooth textures tend to be pleasant. That these ecological correlations are preserved in the 
phoneme–meaning space suggests that the sound-symbolic system encodes not isolated 
perceptual features but the topological structure of human sensorimotor experience. This is 
consistent with cross-modal correspondence research showing that perceptual dimensions are 
linked through shared bodily states 21: the same articulatory gesture that feels "heavy" (e.g., a 
voiced stop produced with full oral closure) also feels "large" and "dark," because these 
attributes co-occur in physical interaction with the world. In this sense, phonemes function as 
multisensory micro-signatures, each articulation simultaneously activating a structured 
constellation of perceptual associations grounded in the body. 
 
The convergence between LLM-derived and human-perceived phoneme–meaning associations 
has implications for the nature of sound symbolism in language. Although the LLMs we used are 
multimodal architectures, in our experiments they received only orthographic text as input and 
had no access to speech or articulatory information, yet they recover the same dimensional 
hierarchy observed in human judgments. This convergence is all the more striking given that the 
two methods rely on entirely different input channels: LLMs received written pseudowords, while 
human participants in Study 3 heard synthesized audio. Prior work has ruled out orthographic 
shape as the primary driver of sound symbolism, as the effect persists across writing systems6, 
in congenitally blind individuals31, and in preliterate infants32. Imai et al. 8 further showed that 
deaf individuals retain sound-symbolic sensitivity, ruling out auditory input as well. Together, 
these findings point to articulatory gestures as the core carrier of form–meaning 
correspondences. Our Study 4 provides direct evidence for this view: articulatory features alone 
predict phoneme–meaning associations with high accuracy, suggesting that the proprioceptive 
experience of speech production is what grounds the mapping. That LLMs can recover these

patterns from text alone is consistent with recent evidence that distributional statistics in 
language preserve sound-symbolic structure39,40, including iconicity ratings15. Notably, 
temperature was the weakest dimension in both LLM and human data. This may reflect the fact 
that temperature lacks a direct articulatory correlate: unlike size, which maps onto oral cavity 
volume, or shape, which maps onto constriction type, temperature has no obvious physical link 
to vocal tract gestures. A methodological caveat is warranted: proprietary LLMs pose 
reproducibility challenges41, as their training data and architectures may change across 
versions, though our three-model consensus design and human behavioral validation in Study 2 
partially mitigate this concern. 
 
All five language groups performed above chance, yet pair-level correlations between groups 
were low, consistent with a model of universal core associations modulated by language-
specific phonological experience. This pattern aligns with developmental evidence that infants 
begin with broad cross-modal sensitivities that are narrowed by native-language input 33,34,35, 
and with cross-linguistic ideophone research showing that articulatory features pattern to iconic 
meanings consistently across typologically diverse languages36 , and that the cumulative 
number of such correspondences within a word predicts human iconicity judgments20. That 
cross-modal correspondences also appear in non-human species 7 suggests an evolutionary 
foundation for these biases, one that spoken language may have recruited during its emergence 
37,38. 
 
One intriguing question is whether phoneme-level semantic profiles compose at the word level. 
If each phoneme carries a structured constellation of meaning, whole-word semantics might 
partially reflect the superposition of its component phonemes' profiles. Suggestively, words like 
"dig" and "drop" are composed of phonemes that our data associate with heaviness and 
downward movement, while "up" and "lift" contain phonemes associated with lightness and

Page 15 
elevation — though such examples are anecdotal and systematic testing across large lexicons 
is needed. 
 
In sum, our results suggest that individual phonemes carry multidimensional semantic 
information that is grounded in articulatory phonetics, recoverable from text statistics and 
auditory perception, and at least partially shared across unrelated languages. Adequately 
powered confirmatory studies will enable finer-grained analysis of cross-linguistic and cross-
dimensional variation. These findings support a view in which sound–meaning iconicity is not an 
occasional exception to arbitrariness but a structured property of the phonological signal, one 
rooted in deep cross-modal biases in human perception.

MATERIALS AND METHODS 
Ethics statement 
All studies involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of 
Helsinki. Studies 2 and 3 report pilot behavioral data collected as part of research design 
development. Both studies have been preregistered, and institutional review board approval at 
Columbia University is in progress for full-scale confirmatory data collection. All participants 
provided informed consent. No personally identifiable information was collected. 
Nonword generation 
Nonword pairs were generated programmatically in Python. Each pair consisted of two 
nonwords differing in exactly one target letter, with all remaining frame letters held constant. For 
each of 220 possible letter combinations (10 vowel–vowel, 210 consonant–consonant), 20 
nonword pairs were generated (10 single-occurrence, 10 double-occurrence), yielding 4,427 
pairs after supplementing underrepresented contrasts. All nonwords were 4–7 letters long, 
satisfied English phonotactic constraints, and were screened to exclude real words or near-
words (edit distance ≤ 1). 
Study 1: LLM semantic rating 
Three LLMs (GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Flash; accessed via their respective APIs in 
2025) rated each nonword on nine semantic dimensions using 0–10 scales: size (small–large), 
shape (round–spiky), brightness (dark–bright), texture (smooth–rough), speed (slow–fast), 
temperature (cold–hot), pleasantness (unpleasant–pleasant), weight (light–heavy), and 
elevation (low–high). Temperature was set to 0 for all models to ensure deterministic outputs. 
Each model processed all 4,427 pairs independently. Cohen’s d was calculated across all pairs 
sharing the same letter contrast. Statistical significance was assessed via permutation tests 
(10,000 iterations) with FDR correction. 
Study 2: Pilot human behavioral validation (preregistered)


## Page 17


Page 17 
Twenty-three native English speakers (ages 19–64) were recruited via Prolific for an online 
forced-choice task following a preregistered protocol; 19 passed all attention checks and were 
retained for analysis. Participants were compensated at a rate of $12/hour equivalent. On each 
trial, participants viewed a nonword pair and selected which better matched a specified 
semantic pole. One hundred eight pairs were tested across nine dimensions. Attention checks 
were embedded throughout. This pilot was conducted to validate the experimental paradigm 
and provide preliminary effect-size estimates for power analysis. A confirmatory study with N = 
200 is preregistered and will be conducted upon IRB approval. 
Study 3: Pilot cross-linguistic auditory replication (preregistered) 
Thirty nonword pairs selected for maximal LLM prediction stability and cross-linguistic 
pronounceability were presented as audio stimuli to speakers of English (N = 31), Spanish (N = 
12), Japanese (N = 9), Mandarin Chinese (N = 18), and Arabic (N = 11). This pilot followed a 
preregistered protocol and served to validate the auditory paradigm and estimate cross-
linguistic effect sizes. A confirmatory study targeting N ≥ 80 per language across 5–8 languages 
is planned. Audio stimuli were synthesized using Google Cloud Text-to-Speech (en-US, 
WaveNet voice, 24 kHz sampling rate), with each nonword embedded in a neutral carrier 
phrase to control for prosodic variation. Participants were recruited via Prolific and compensated 
at a rate of $12/hour equivalent. 
Study 4: Articulatory-phonetic prediction 
Articulatory features were extracted from the PanPhon database 16, which encodes each IPA 
segment as a vector of 24 binary articulatory features (e.g., sonorant, continuant, consonantal, 
voiced, nasal, high, back). For each letter pair, the signed difference in each articulatory feature 
was computed and entered as predictors in ridge regression models (scikit-learn RidgeCV, with 
the regularization parameter α selected via nested leave-one-out cross-validation from 50 log-
spaced values between 10⁻3 and 103) predicting LLM-derived semantic scores (three-model 
consensus Cohen’s d). Ten-fold cross-validation (consonant contrasts) and leave-one-out

Page 18 
cross-validation (vowel contrasts) assessed predictive accuracy (R2). Feature class 
contributions were evaluated by comparing full models against reduced models omitting all 
features of a given class (manner, place, or laryngeal) and computing the unique variance (ΔR2) 
attributable to each class. To validate predictions against established theory (Fig. 3E), we 
identified seven classical hypotheses from the sound symbolism literature and mapped each to 
specific articulatory feature × semantic dimension predictions based on the original studies. For 
each prediction, we computed the Pearson correlation between the relevant feature’s difference 
values and the LLM-derived semantic scores across all consonant pairs (CC, N = 210) or vowel 
pairs (VV, N = 10) separately. A prediction was scored as “consistent” if the sign of r matched 
the expected direction, and as “significant” if p < .05 (uncorrected, as these are confirmatory 
one-directional tests). VV was marked not applicable when a feature had no variance among 
the 5 English vowels (e.g., all vowels are [+sonorant]). For the broader literature comparison 
(fig. S2), we tested 15 classic findings by computing group-level mean Cohen’s d values or 
Spearman rank correlations (ρ) from LLM-derived per-letter dimension scores, using whole-
group comparisons (e.g., sonorants vs. obstruents) without separating CC and VV contributions, 
mirroring the methodology of the original studies. 
Statistical analysis 
All analyses were conducted in Python (v3.10) using SciPy, NumPy, and statsmodels. Effect 
sizes are reported as Cohen’s d for LLM analyses, computed as the mean rating difference 
between paired nonwords divided by the pooled standard deviation across all pairs sharing the 
same letter contrast. Percentage above chance is reported for human behavioral data. Multiple 
comparison corrections used the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR procedure (q < .05). Cross-
validation used 10-fold partitioning for consonant models and leave-one-out for vowel models, 
both with ridge regression. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the 26 × 9 
letter-level Cohen’s d matrix using singular value decomposition on z-scored values (scikit-learn 
PCA). Split-half reliability was assessed by randomly splitting pseudoword carriers within each

Page 19 
letter pair into two halves, computing Cohen’s d separately for each half, and correlating the 
resulting profiles; this was repeated 100 times, and Spearman-Brown corrected r values are 
reported.
