Black hole analogues in two-dimensional flows with constant shear
Alessia Biondi,1 Scott Robertson,1 and Germain Rousseaux1
1Institut Pprime, CNRS–Universit´e de Poitiers–ISAE-ENSMA. TSA 51124, 86073 Poitiers Cedex 9, France
(Dated: March 9, 2026)
We review the Analogue Gravity description of a unidirectional water wave system, assuming no
prior knowledge of General Relativity or differential geometry. In so doing, we generalize established
results concerning an effective curved spacetime for surface waves on irrotational 2D flows, by
including flows with constant shear. We show that such flows remain perfectly compatible with the
existence of an effective curved spacetime and, in particular, of a metric description.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Analogue Gravity [1] is a transdisciplinary field that aims to mimic field propagation in curved spacetime using
waves propagating in a laboratory-based system. It exploits a mathematical analogy between the wave equations
applicable to these vastly different cases, which tends to hold in the long-wavelength regime where the short-scale
physics is lost from view. Unruh was the first to suggest a concrete use of the analogy [2] by pointing out the possibility
of analogue Hawking radiation from “horizons” in the effective spacetime. In the past two decades, Analogue Gravity
has become an experimental field, with realizations in systems such as Bose-Einstein condensates [3–7], optical fibers
and waveguides [8, 9], polariton fluids [10–12], and surface waves on water [13–17].
The surface wave analogy was first expounded by Sch¨utzhold and Unruh [13] and has since led to several experi-
mental studies, including the observation of an analogue of the Hawking effect in subcritical flows [14, 15], rotational
super-radiance at the ergoregion of a rotating flow [16], and scattering of an infalling wave at a black-hole horizon [17].
It is thus a very relevant Analogue Gravity system, despite its classical, macroscopic nature. But it tends to be far
more complicated in practice than the simple theoretical descriptions would suggest, being subject to vorticity, viscos-
ity, friction, nonlinearities, and turbulence. All of these may be expected to distort the wave behavior away from the
gravitational analogy, potentially rendering the system less relevant. It thus behooves us to investigate these features
and to assess whether they might be incorporated into an Analogue Gravity framework.
Furthermore, it is important to ensure that the Analogue Gravity description is accessible to those not already
familiar with it, because the analogy at the heart of the subject is meant to be bidirectional: water wave physics
can help us to explore certain concepts from gravity, but at the same time, the application of such concepts to water
waves can help us to explore new and under-explored processes and regimes in hydrodynamics. Indeed, the regime
of interest to Analogue Gravity is typically quite different from that normally studied in hydrodynamics: the waves
considered are in the linear regime; their scattering is primarily induced by inhomogeneities in the background flow
(rather than by nonlinear wave interactions); and there exist waves of negative energy [17, 18], a crucial ingredient in
the analogues of the Hawking effect [19] and super-radiance [20, 21].
The purpose of this paper is two-fold. Its main purpose is to present, in a pedagogical manner aimed at the hydro-
dynamics community, the derivation of the wave equation for long-wavelength surface waves on a two-dimensional,
unidirectional flow, and to explain why it is of interest from an Analogue Gravity perspective. In this sense it is
mostly review. We also generalize the usual treatment to the case where the background flow has a constant shear
vorticity, and we will see that this case is still perfectly compatible with the existence of an analogue spacetime.
Previous works have found the dispersion relation of waves on a linear shear flow [22–25]. Here, our Analogue Gravity
viewpoint takes a different approach: we recover their long-wavelength behaviour, but we do so in the context of a
wave equation, which allows a quantitative analysis of wave scattering on inhomogeneous flows. Within Analogue
Gravity, we note that there exist previous studies that have examined the effects of vorticity on analogue rotating
black holes [26–30], focusing primarily on cases where the vorticity is oriented perpendicular to the fluid plane. Our
interest here, however, lies in a “hidden” vorticity arising from shear flow – one that leaves no obvious imprint on the
effective metric.
The paper is organized as follows. In Sec. II, the essential idea behind an effective spacetime is introduced, as well as
how it is expected to apply to waves in a moving medium. Section III is devoted to a derivation of the wave equation
on a channel flow in the appropriate limit of long-wavelength, which is identified with that expected of a scalar field in
an effective spacetime. In Sec. IV, the physical properties of the solutions of the wave equation are examined, and an
abstract field theory description is briefly considered. Section V considers the solution of the stationary background
which determines the effective spacetime, paying particular attention to the case where this spacetime contains the
analogue of a black-hole horizon, and applying the standard Hawking-Unruh prediction. Finally, in Sec. VI we look
at the scattering induced by inhomogeneities in the conformal factor, the main quantity affected by the presence of
vorticity. We conclude in Sec. VII.

II.
EFFECTIVE METRIC FOR WAVES IN MOVING MEDIUM
A.
Idea
The idea underpinning Analogue Gravity is that waves in a moving medium behave like fields propagating in a
curved spacetime. Apart from providing a mathematically analogous system that can be exploited experimentally, it
also provides a more intuitive picture to help understand the behavior of gravitational systems. Historically, it has
been especially oriented towards black holes, and we shall focus on black-hole analogues in this work.
To be more concrete, imagine an accelerating flow which, in the context of water waves, can be experimentally
realized by having it pass over an obstacle. In the upstream region, the speed of the flow is smaller in magnitude
than the speed of waves with respect to the flow. This means that waves may propagate in either direction, though
with different total speeds: the flow will boost the speed of waves co-propagating with the flow, and conversely it will
diminish the speed of waves counter-propagating against the flow. As the flow accelerates, this change of the wave
speed will become gradually more extreme. Eventually, we may reach a situation where, on the downstream side, the
flow speed is faster than the intrinsic wave speed. In this regime, all waves will appear to propagate with the flow,
even those that are counter-propagating because the flow speed completely dominates over the wave speed. All waves
are dragged inexorably downstream on this side, so that no waves can escape from this region. This is analogous to
the interior of a black hole, and the point at which the flow speed is critical – where it is equal to the wave speed –
is the analogue of the black hole horizon.
It is clear from the above picture that the analogue black hole arises through setting up a competition between two
velocities: the wave speed and the flow speed. The wave speed measured in the rest frame of the fluid is independent
of the propagation direction, and is related to the properties of the background flow. It plays the role of the speed of
light in General Relativity, except that it will generally depend on position due to inhomogeneities in the flow profile.
The flow speed has no clear analogue in General Relativity, because it relates to the motion of a preferred reference
frame (that in which the fluid is at rest), while it is a foundational principle of relativity theory that there exists no
preferred reference frame. Nevertheless, we will use the existence of the fluid frame to our advantage in identifying
how the analogue of the “spacetime interval” is expected to behave, and we will make the analogy with a particular
description of the gravitational black-hole spacetime that happens to have a similar form. 1

C.
Effective metric of “flowing” spacetime
Let us motivate the expected form of the effective metric describing the behavior of surface waves.
Imagine waves on static water. As already mentioned, we expect the wave speed to play the role of the speed of
light, and we would thus expect that the effective metric describing this system is exactly (2) with c now being the
speed of surface waves. If the system is inhomogeneous (due, say, to the presence of an obstacle on the bottom of the
channel), c may become position-dependent.

spatial contribution to the spacetime interval in Eq. (2) is measured with respect to the fluid – that is, in the preferred
reference frame where the fluid is at rest.

III.
DERIVATION OF THE WAVE EQUATION IN A CHANNEL FLOW
In this section, we derive the equation for surface waves in the long-wavelength/shallow-water limit, often described
by the Saint-Venant equations and characterized by the small parameter kh ≪1 where k is the wave number and
h is the water depth. Moreover, the equations shall be linearized, so we also assume ka ≪1 where a is the wave
amplitude. In this linear, non-dispersive regime, we can expect to find an equation of the form (8).

B.
Analogue Hawking effect
The passage from a subcritical region to a supercritical one means that the nature of the counter-propagating mode
changes, depending on where we are within the flow. On the subcritical side, it is able to propagate upstream, and
since u2 < c2 this wave must have positive energy. On the supercritical side, the flow overcomes the intrinsic wave
speed, carrying it downstream even though it is attempting to propagate upstream. As discussed in Sec. IV C, this
wave has negative energy. So the black-hole horizon splits the counter-propagating mode into two disconnected pieces
with opposite energies, each propagating away from the horizon. It thus appears energetically possible for pairs of
such modes to be continuously emitted from the horizon, since this would incur no overall energy cost.

VII.
CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION
We have reviewed the application of Analogue Gravity to the theory of shallow water waves, showing how the wave
equation has the same form as that for a scalar field in an effective (2+1)-dimensional spacetime. In doing so, we
have generalized the standard treatment of an irrotational flow to one with constant shear, deriving the change to
the effective metric and reproducing the long-wavelength limit of the dispersion relation derived in previous works.
This is, to our knowledge, the first demonstration that a flow with constant vorticity remains perfectly compatible
with the existence of an effective spacetime metric. Importantly, it alters the form of the conformal factor, which is
the key quantity governing the mixing of co- and counter-propagating waves on an inhomogeneous background. The
presence of shear vorticity is found to reduce the inhomogeneity of the conformal factor, and we have confirmed this
