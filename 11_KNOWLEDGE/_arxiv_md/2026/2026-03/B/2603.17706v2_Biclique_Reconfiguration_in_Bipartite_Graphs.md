Biclique Reconfiguration in Bipartite Graphs⋆
Yota Otachia, Emi Toyodaa
aNagoya University, Nagoya, Japan
Abstract
We prove that Balanced Biclique Reconfiguration on bipartite graphs is PSPACE-
complete. This implies the PSPACE-completeness of the spanning variant of Subgraph Re-
configuration under the token jumping rule for the property “a graph is an (i, j)-complete
bipartite graph,” which was previously known only to be NP-hard [Hanaka et al. TCS 2020].
Using our result, we also show that Connected Components Reconfiguration with two
connected components is PSPACE-complete under all previously studied rules, resolving an open
problem of Nakahata [COCOON 2025] in the negative.
Keywords:
combinatorial reconfiguration, biclique, token jumping, connected components
reconfiguration.
1. Introduction
In the framework of combinatorial reconfiguration on graphs, we are given two substruc-
tures of a graph (e.g., vertex subsets) with a certain property and are asked whether one can
transform one into the other via step-by-step modifications, while preserving the property in the
intermediate states. In the literature, reconfiguration problems of several important structures
have been studied extensively; e.g., independent sets, vertex covers, cliques, dominating sets,
matchings, and so on. See the surveys [6, 15] and the references therein.
In this paper, we study the reconfiguration problem of bicliques on bipartite graphs under
the token jumping model. A biclique, or more specifically a (p, q)-biclique, is a set of vertices
that induces a complete bipartite graph Kp,q.
We call a (p, p)-biclique a balanced biclique.
Bicliques, especially those in bipartite graphs, are natural and important objects with theo-
retical and practical applications [12]. Our main result is to show that Balanced Biclique
Reconfiguration on bipartite graphs, which we formally define later, is PSPACE-complete.
Previously, only the NP-hardness was known on bipartite graphs, while it was already known to
be PSPACE-complete on general graphs [4]. As a theoretical application of our result, we show
the PSPACE-completeness of Connected Components Reconfiguration with two con-
nected components by presenting a reduction from Balanced Biclique Reconfiguration
on bipartite graphs. This answers an open problem of Nakahata [14] in the negative.

“Although a hint is provided in [G&J] (“transformation from CLIQUE”), the NP-

In the field of parameterized complexity, the complexity of the problem of finding a large balanced
biclique on bipartite graphs parameterized by the solution size was a long-standing open problem.
Downey and Fellows [2, §33.1] listed this problem as one of the most infamous open problems.1
There, it is said that
“Almost everyone considers that this problem should obviously be W[1]-hard, and
almost everyone quickly finds the same easy (but erroneous) proof. It is rather an
embarrassment to the field that the question remains open after all these years!”
This problem was finally resolved by Lin [12], who proved its W[1]-hardness. This result received
both the best paper award and the best student paper award at SODA 2015 [11].
In the context of combinatorial reconfiguration, there is another example (not about bi-
cliques) that actually shows a case where the bipartiteness restriction cannot be overcome.
Independent Set Reconfiguration is one of the most well-studied problems in the field
of combinatorial reconfiguration. It is studied under three modification models, token addition
and removal (TAR) [7], token jumping (TJ) [10], and token sliding (TS) [5], and known to
be PSPACE-complete under all of them on general graphs. The complexity of Independent
Set Reconfiguration on bipartite graphs was open for some years, and Lokshtanov and
Mouawad [13] resolved it in a somewhat surprising way: they showed that on bipartite graphs,
it is PSPACE-complete under TS, but NP-complete under TJ and TAR. Thus, under some
models, restriction to bipartite graphs makes the problem strictly easier than on general graphs
(assuming that NP ̸= PSPACE).

1.2. Our results
The main result. As mentioned above, our main result is to completely determine the complexity
of Balanced Biclique Reconfiguration on bipartite graphs by showing that it is PSPACE-
complete. We now formally define the problem. Let G = (V, E) be a graph. A vertex set S ⊆V
is a (p, q)-biclique if G[S] is isomorphic to the complete bipartite graph Kp,q.
A sequence
S0, . . . , Sℓof vertex sets is a TJ-sequence of (p, q)-bicliques if each Si is a (p, q)-biclique and
|Si \ Si+1| = |Si+1 \ Si| = 1 for 0 ≤i ≤ℓ−1. We call such a move from Si to Si+1 a TJ-move.
Now we can formulate the problem Balanced Biclique Reconfiguration as follows.
Problem. Balanced Biclique Reconfiguration
Input. A graph G and two (p, p)-bicliques S and S′ of G.
Question. Is there a TJ-sequence of (p, p)-bicliques from S to S′ in G?
Our main result is formalized as follows.
Theorem 1.1. Balanced Biclique Reconfiguration is PSPACE-complete on bipartite
graphs.
We prove Theorem 1.1 in Section 3. There, we present a reduction from Clique Recon-
figuration [7, 8] to Balanced Biclique Reconfiguration. We first follow the reduction
of Johnson [9] from the problem of finding a large clique to that of finding a large balanced
biclique. Although this already gives a correspondence between cliques in a general graph and
balanced bicliques in a bipartite graph, there remain two obstacles:
1. the obtained bicliques are locked; i.e., no TJ-moves can be applied to them;
2. the bicliques corresponding to two TJ-adjacent cliques differ in a large number of vertices.
At first glance, the first issue looks severe. However, there is a trick to unlock the bicliques by
reducing their size by 1. This is a simple but crucial step, and showing its correctness is the most
nontrivial part of the proof. The second issue is easier to handle by applying a rather standard
technique of simulating a single step in the original problem (Clique Reconfiguration) by
several steps in the target problem (Balanced Biclique Reconfiguration).
The second result: an application of the main result. As a theoretical application of the main
result, we obtain the following theorem, which will be proved in Section 4.
Theorem 1.2. Connected Components Reconfiguration with two connected components
of the same size is PSPACE-complete under both CJ and CS rules even on co-bipartite graphs.
We defer the formal definitions for Theorem 1.2 to Section 4. Roughly speaking, Connected
Components Reconfiguration asks to reconfigure a vertex set that induces a graph with
specific sizes of connected components. It is a generalization of Independent Set Recon-
figuration as it can ask all connected components to have size 1. Nakahata [14] introduced
Connected Components Reconfiguration, showed several positive and negative results,
and asked whether Connected Components Reconfiguration admits a polynomial-time
algorithm when the number of connected components in the reconfigured set is a constant.
Theorem 1.2 answers this question in the negative assuming P ̸= PSPACE.

2. Preliminaries
Let G = (V, E) be a graph. For S ⊆V , we denote by G[S] the subgraph of G induced by S,
and by E(S) = {{u, v} ∈E | u, v ∈S} its edge set.
A clique is a set of pairwise adjacent vertices and a k-clique is a clique of size k.
An
independent set is a set of pairwise non-adjacent vertices. A graph G = (V, E) is bipartite if
its vertex set V can be partitioned into two independent sets A and B. To emphasize that G
is bipartite with a bipartition {A, B}, we sometimes write G = (A, B; E). Note that if G is
connected, its bipartition is unique.
The complement of a graph G = (V, E) is ¯G = (V, {{u, v} | u, v ∈V, u ̸= v, {u, v} /∈E}).
The complement of a bipartite graph is a co-bipartite graph. The bipartite complement of a
bipartite graph H = (A, B; E) is the bipartite graph with the same vertex set A ∪B and the
edge set {{a, b} | a ∈A, b ∈B, {a, b} /∈E}.
Let S0, . . . , Sℓbe a TJ-sequence of (p, q)-bicliques in a connected bipartite graph G =
(A, B; E) such that |S0 ∩A| = p and |S0 ∩B| = q. Observe that if |p −q| ̸= 1, then |Si ∩A| = p
and |Si ∩B| = q hold for every i. This implies that every TJ-move involves vertices only in A
or only in B; that is, either (Si \ Si+1) ∪(Si+1 \ Si) ⊆A or (Si \ Si+1) ∪(Si+1 \ Si) ⊆B for
0 ≤i ≤ℓ−1. In our reductions, we consider the cases where |p −q| ̸= 1 and thus have such
side-preserving TJ-moves only.
To simplify the presentation of reconfiguration steps, we sometimes write S −v, S + v, and
S −u + v instead of S \ {v}, S ∪{v}, and (S \ {u}) ∪{v}, respectively.

4. Connected Components Reconfiguration with two connected components
Let G = (V, E) be a graph. For S ⊆V , let ccG(S) be the set of the vertex sets of connected
components of G[S] and let µG(S) be the multiset of the orders of connected components of
G[S]; that is, µG(S) = {{|X| : X ∈ccG(S)}}.2 Let M be a multiset of positive integers. If S ⊆V
satisfies µG(S) = M, then we call S an M-set.
For Connected Components Reconfiguration, the two rules CJ (component jumping)
and CS (component sliding) are studied. A sequence S0, . . . , Sℓof vertex sets is a CJ-sequence
of M-sets in G if each Si is an M-set and |ccG(Si) \ ccG(Si+1)| = |ccG(Si+1) \ ccG(Si)| = 1 for
0 ≤i ≤ℓ−1. We call such a move from Si to Si+1 a CJ-move. A CJ-sequence S0, . . . , Sℓ
of M-sets is a CS-sequence of M-sets if it additionally satisfies the following condition for
0 ≤i ≤ℓ−1: the unique elements Xi ∈ccG(Si) \ ccG(Si+1) and Xi+1 ∈ccG(Si+1) \ ccG(Si)
form a connected graph; that is, G[Xi ∪Xi+1] is connected. Furthermore, if it also satisfies
|Xi \ Xi+1| = |Xi+1 \ Xi| = 1 for 0 ≤i ≤ℓ−1, then the sequence is a CS1-sequence of M-
sets. We define CS-moves and CS1-moves analogously. It is known that there is a CS-sequence
between two M-sets if and only if there is a CS1-sequence between them [14].

The next lemma is the key observation showing that the (p, q)-bicliques in a bipartite graph
G are exactly the {{p, q}}-sets of its complement ¯G.
Lemma 4.1. Let G = (A, B; E) be a bipartite graph and p, q be positive integers. Then, S is a
(p, q)-biclique of G if and only if S is a {{p, q}}-set of ¯G.
Proof. For the only-if direction, observe that if S is a (p, q)-biclique of G with bipartition {X, Y },
then cc ¯G(S) = {X, Y }, and thus µ ¯G(S) = {{p, q}}.
To prove the if direction, assume that cc ¯G(S) = {X, Y } and (|X|, |Y |) = (p, q).
If X
intersects both cliques A and B of ¯G, then X is a dominating set of ¯G, which contradicts that
cc ¯G(S) = {X, Y }. Thus X cannot intersect both A and B. By symmetry, we may assume that
X ⊆A. Now, since A is a clique of ¯G and ¯G has no edge between X and Y , it follows that
Y ⊆B. The discussion so far implies that, in the original graph G, X and Y are independent
sets and there are all possible edges between them; that is, X ∪Y (= S) is a (p, q)-biclique
of G.
Lemma 4.2. Let G = (A, B; E) be a bipartite graph and T and T ′ be (p, p)-bicliques of G.
There is a TJ-move from T to T ′ in G if and only if there is a CS1-move from T to T ′ in ¯G.
Proof. Assume that T = X ∪Y and T ′ = X′ ∪Y ′ such that X, X′ ⊆A and Y, Y ′ ⊆B. Since
T and T ′ are bicliques of G, it follows that cc ¯G(T) = {X, Y } and cc ¯G(T ′) = {X′, Y ′}. Since
A and B are cliques of ¯G, both X ∪X′ (⊆A) and Y ∪Y ′ (⊆B) are connected in ¯G. Since
|X| = |Y | = |X′| = |Y ′| (= p), we can see that |(X ∪Y ) \ (X′ ∪Y ′)| = |(X′ ∪Y ′) \ (X ∪Y )| = 1
if and only if all conditions below are satisfied:
• either X ̸= X′ and Y = Y ′, or, X = X′ and Y ̸= Y ′;
• if X ̸= X′, then |X \ X′| = |X′ \ X| = 1;
• if Y ̸= Y ′, then |Y \ Y ′| = |Y ′ \ Y | = 1.
Therefore, there is a TJ-move from T = X ∪Y to T ′ = X′ ∪Y ′ in G if and only if there is a
CS1-move from T to T ′ in ¯G.
Lemmas 4.1 and 4.2 imply that, for a bipartite graph G, a sequence S0, . . . , Sℓis a TJ-
sequence of (p, p)-bicliques in G if and only if S0, . . . , Sℓis a CS1-sequence of {{p, p}}-sets in ¯G.
This proves Theorem 1.2 with CS since CS1 and CS are equivalent [14].

is a CJ-move from T to T ′; that is, |{X, Y }\{X′, Y ′}| = |{X′, Y ′}\{X, Y }| = 1. By symmetry,

5. Concluding remarks
In this paper, we showed that Balanced Biclique Reconfiguration on bipartite graphs
is PSPACE-complete, resolving a previously unsettled problem of Hanaka et al. [4].
Using
this hardness result, we showed that Connected Components Reconfiguration with two
connected components is PSPACE-complete under both CJ and CS rules, answering an open
problem of Nakahata [14] in the negative.
Token sliding. In this paper, we only considered the token-jumping model for the biclique recon-
figuration problem. Indeed, the token-sliding model is not meaningful for Balanced Biclique
Reconfiguration on bipartite graphs, since any TS-move on a bipartite graph maps a (p, p)-
biclique to a set with p −1 vertices on one side and p + 1 vertices on the other side.
For general graphs, we can see that the proof of Hanaka et al. [4, Theorem 12] implies that
Balanced Biclique Reconfiguration under the token-sliding model is PSPACE-complete.
Although their theorem is stated for the token-jumping model, its proof works for the token-
sliding model as well.
Flexible variant. One may also consider the flexible version of the biclique reconfiguration prob-
lem that takes as input a graph G, a (p, q)-biclique S, and a (p′, q′)-biclique S′, where (p, q)
and (p′, q′) are not necessarily the same, and asks if there is a TJ-sequence (or a TS-sequence)
of (any) bicliques from S to S′. For general graphs, we can use the proof of Hanaka et al. [4,
Theorem 12] again. Although their proof is designed for the case where the shape of allowed
bicliques is fixed, it actually works without such a restriction.
For bipartite graphs, we may show that it is at least NP-hard under TJ via a reduction from
Independent Set Reconfiguration under TJ on bipartite graphs [13], because almost all
bicliques of a bipartite graph H = (A, B; E) are independent sets of its bipartite complement
H′: the only exceptions are bicliques S of H that are indeed independent sets K|S|,0 of H that
intersect both A and B; such independent-set bicliques of H form bicliques K|S∩A|,|S∩B| of H′.
In a reduction, we can avoid such exceptional bicliques by adding to both sides of the graph a
large number of vertices adjacent to all vertices in the other side and including them into both
initial and target sets. For the token-sliding model on bipartite graphs, we did not find a direct
connection from the flexible variant to known problems.
