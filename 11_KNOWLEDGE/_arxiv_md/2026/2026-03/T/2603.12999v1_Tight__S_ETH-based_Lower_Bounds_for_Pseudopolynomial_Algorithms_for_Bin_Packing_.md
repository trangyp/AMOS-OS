Tight (S)ETH-based Lower Bounds for Pseudopolynomial
Algorithms for Bin Packing and Multi-Machine Scheduling
Karl Bringmann∗
Anita Dürr∗
Karol Węgrzycki†
Abstract
Bin Packing with k bins is a fundamental optimisation problem in which we are given a set of
n integers and a capacity T and the goal is to partition the set into k subsets, each of total sum
at most T. Bin Packing is NP-hard already for k = 2 and a textbook dynamic programming
algorithm solves it in pseudopolynomial time O(nT k−1). Jansen, Kratsch, Marx, and Schlotter
[JCSS’13] proved that this time cannot be improved to (nT)o(k/ log k) assuming the Exponential
Time Hypothesis (ETH). Their result has become an important building block, explaining the
hardness of many problems in parameterised complexity. Note that their result is one log-factor
short of being tight. In this paper, we prove a tight ETH-based lower bound for Bin Packing,
ruling out time 2o(n)T o(k). This answers an open problem of Jansen et al. and yields improved
lower bounds for many applications in parameterised complexity.
Since Bin Packing is an example of multi-machine scheduling, it is natural to next study
other scheduling problems.
We prove tight lower bounds based on the Strong Exponential
Time Hypothesis (SETH) for several classic k-machine scheduling problems, including makespan
minimisation with release dates (Pk|rj|Cmax), minimizing the number of tardy jobs (Pk||ΣUj),
and minimizing the weighted sum of completion times (Pk||ΣwjCj). For all these problems,
we rule out time 2o(n)T k−1−ε for any ε > 0 assuming SETH, where T is the total processing
time; this matches classic nO(1)T k−1-time algorithms from the 60s and 70s.
Moreover, we
rule out time 2o(n)T k−ε for minimizing the total processing time of tardy jobs (Pk||ΣpjUj),
which matches a classic O(nT k)-time algorithm and answers an open problem of Fischer and
Wennmann [TheoretiCS’25].

Introduction
Bin Packing is a fundamental optimisation problem that has many applications both in theory
and practice and is widely studied in theoretical computer science, mathematical optimisation, and
operations research. In this problem, given n items a1, . . . , an ∈N, a capacity T ∈N, and a number
of bins k ⩾2, the task is to partition the items into k subsets, each summing to at most T. Note
that this is the decision version of the problem (in the optimisation version the goal is to minimise
the number of bins k). In this paper we study k-Bin Packing, where the number of bins k is fixed
(a closely related view is to study the parameterised complexity of Bin Packing with parameter k).
Since the problem is NP-hard already for k = 2, we focus on pseudopolynomial-time algorithms,
i.e. we allow polynomial time in terms of n and T. k-Bin Packing can be solved in O(nT k−1) time
by a simple dynamic programming algorithm that dates back to the 60s [45, 40]. This running time
has not been improved, despite significant interest in the problem.
A natural special case of k-Bin Packing is the k-way Partition problem, where the goal is
to partition a given set of n positive integers into k subsets of equal sum. In fact, we prove that the
two problems are equivalent with respect to the questions studied in this paper (see Theorem 8.1),
so the reader may think of either of these problems in what follows.
Another important special case is the Subset Sum1 problem, as it is equivalent to 2-Bin
Packing by a standard reduction. The vast literature on Subset Sum includes a classic O(nT)-
time dynamic programming algorithm [5] and a more recent improvement to time e
O(n + T) [10].2
Lower bounds for Subset Sum are a recent success story of fine-grained complexity theory: The
Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) can be used to prove conditional lower bounds that are tight
up to constants in the exponent, and such an ETH-based lower bound ruling out time 2o(n)T o(1)
was shown in [15, 34]. More precise lower bounds that even determine the optimal constant in the
exponent can be shown using the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH), and indeed a tight
SETH-based lower bound ruling out time 2o(n)T 1−ε for any ε > 0 was shown by Abboud et al. [2].
1.1
Fine-grained Complexity of Bin Packing
In this paper we ask whether a similarly precise understanding as for Subset Sum can be obtained
for k-Bin Packing. The state of the art lower bound for k-Bin Packing was proven by Jansen,
Kratsch, Marx, and Schlotter [33], and shows that it cannot be solved in time (nT)o(k/ log k) assuming
ETH. Jansen et al. also asked whether their result can be improved to rule out time (nT)o(k), thus
avoiding the log-factor loss of their reduction. Our first main result answers their question by indeed
providing a tight ETH-based lower bound, in fact with a better dependence on n:
Theorem 1.1. Assuming ETH, k-Bin Packing cannot be solved in time 2o(n)T o(k).
This shows that any algorithm for k-Bin Packing requires either exponential time 2Ω(n), which
matches the known 2O(n)-time algorithms [6, 42], or time T Ω(k), which matches the O(nT k−1)-time
dynamic programming algorithm. Since ETH ignores constants in the exponent, what we obtain
is the best possible ETH-based lower bound in terms of n and T. We prove our lower bound by a
creative new encoding of communication between bins, see Section 2 for a discussion.
The lower bound of Jansen et al. [33] has become an important starting point of reductions
to prove lower bounds in parameterised complexity, see, e.g. [7, 8, 9, 21, 24, 25, 27, 28, 36, 38].
However, the log-factor loss in their exponent is inherited by all of these lower bounds. Our tight

Towards more precise lower bounds?
Analogously to the success story of Subset Sum,
now that we have a tight ETH-based lower bound for k-Bin Packing it is natural to ask for
a tight SETH-based lower bound: Can we show that k-Bin Packing cannot be solved in time
2o(n)T k−1−ε for any ε > 0 assuming SETH (or some other popular hypothesis)? Alas, we leave this
as an intriguing open problem. It turns out that reductions to k-Bin Packing are significantly
more difficult than reductions to Subset Sum, see Section 2. Since there have been surprising
algorithmic breakthroughs for k-Bin Packing in some settings3, it is certainly possible that k-Bin
Packing can be solved in time 2o(n)T k−1−ε after all.
1.2
Fine-grained Complexity of Multi-machine Scheduling
k-Bin Packing can be reduced to several classic scheduling problems on k machines. As these
problems offer more structure for lower bounds, we can bypass the difficulties encountered in our
search for more precise lower bounds for k-Bin Packing, by proving, as our second main result,
tight SETH-based lower bounds for several multi-machine scheduling problems. We summarise our
results in Table 1.

problems on parallel machines. Generally, in scheduling problems the input consists of n jobs with

k uniform machines with given speeds s1, . . . , sk ∈(0, 1] where executing job j on machine i takes

Technical Overview
In this section, we give a high-level overview of our results.
2.1
ETH-hardness of k-Bin Packing
Jansen et al. [33] proved their lower bound for k-Bin Packing by a reduction from the Subgraph
Isomorphism problem: Given graphs G and H, find a subgraph of G that is isomorphic to H.
Their reduction maps an instance of Subgraph Isomorphism with n = |V (G)| and k = |E(H)|
to a Bin Packing instance with nO(1) items and O(k) bins of capacity T = f(k)nO(1), for some
computable function f.
Since current lower bounds exclude f(k)no(k/ log k)-time algorithms for
Subgraph Isomorphism assuming ETH [41], this implies that k-Bin Packing cannot be solved in
time (nT)o(k/ log k).
To avoid the barrier posed by the log(k)-factor gap between upper and lower bounds for Subgraph
Isomorphism [41], we directly reduce from the 3-SAT problem. Specifically, to simplify the exposition
of the reduction, we will reduce 3-SAT to the following variant of Bin Packing6:
Multiset k-way Partition with targets
Input:
Multiset of n integers X ⊆N; targets t1, . . . , tk ∈N.
Parameter:
T := max{t1, . . . , tk}
Question:
Is there a partition of X into X1, . . . , Xk such that Σ(Xℓ) = tℓfor every ℓ∈[k]?
This problem can be viewed as a variant of k-Bin Packing where (i) the given integers form a
multiset instead of a set, (ii) every bin has a different capacity instead of all bins having the same
capacity, and (iii) the load of every bin is required to match its capacity instead of only asking the
load to not exceed the capacity. We prove both problems to be equivalent, in the sense that if one
of them can be solved in time 2o(n)T o(k) then so can the other. (In fact, this equivalence extends
to several further problem variants obtained by toggling (i)-(iii), see Theorem 8.1. We note that
some of those reductions are folklore or can be found in the literature, but we include them for
completeness in Section 8.)
It suffices therefore to reduce 3-SAT to Multiset k-way Partition with targets. Let φ be
a 3-CNF formula with N variables and M clauses. By the Sparsification Lemma (Lemma 3.3) we
can assume M = O(N) and each variable appears in O(1) clauses. Fix k ⩾2. We will construct a
multiset X ⊆N and bin capacities t1, . . . , tk ∈N with n = |X| = O(N) and T := max{t1, . . . , tk} =
2O(N/k)NO(1) such that there exists a solution to Multiset k-way Partition with targets if
and only if φ is satisfiable. Hence, if Multiset k-way Partition with targets could be solved
in time 2o(n)T o(k) then 3-SAT could be solved in time 2o(N), contradicting ETH.
We remark that in this overview we treat k as if it would be a function of n tending to ∞and
we hide all other constants in the O(·) notation (e.g. when we write T = 2O(N/k)). In reality, k is
also a constant, so we need to be very careful with dependencies between different constants.
We view a solution to the Multiset k-way Partition with targets instance, i.e. a partition
X1, . . . , Xk of X such that Σ(Xi) = tℓfor all ℓ∈[k], as a packing of all items into k bins such that

(P1) Item z(i, α) can only be packed into the bin assigned to Ci, or into the dumpster bin.

These properties imply that for each of the first k −1 bins the packed items encode satisfying
assignments to the 1/(k −1)-fraction of clauses assigned to the bin. To enforce these properties,
we use a standard construction that adds O(N/k) bookkeeping bits to each item (see the I- and
II-blocks in Figure 2). It remains to ensure consistency:
(P3) All items packed into the first k −1 bins agree on the assignment to each variable.
Once we can enforce consistency, then the picked clause-assignment items together encode one
assignment to the N variables, and this assignment satisfies all clauses, which finishes the reduction.
However, enforcing consistency is the main challenge! Indeed, it requires checking for every pair of
clauses Ci and Cj with a shared variable x that the corresponding packed items z(i, αi) and z(j, αj)
assign the same value to x, i.e. αi(x) = αj(x). Since Ci and Cj may be assigned to different bins, we
need some way to communicate between bins, despite the fact that any particular item only adds
to the sum of a particular bin.
We solve this issue in a novel way, using communication channels and dummy items that com-
municate over these channels. For each pair of clauses Ci and Cj, with i < j, that have a shared
variable x, we assign a block of 3 bits as their communication channel. On this bit block, item
z(i, αi) takes value 00αi(x)2 and item z(j, αj) takes value 00αj(x)2 (where αi(x) and αj(x) are the
values assigned to x by αi and αj, z := 1−z denotes negation, and the subscript 2 indicates numbers
in binary). Let us focus on the case that Ci and Cj are assigned to different bins ℓi ̸= ℓj, as the case
of equal bins is easier. In this case, we also construct dummy items d(i, j, x), d′(i, j, x) ∈N that on
the communication channel take the values 0102 and 0112, respectively. With some bookkeeping,
we ensure that the dummy items can only be packed into bin ℓi or ℓj. If the assignment items
z(i, αi) and z(j, αj) agree on the value of x, i.e. αi(x) = αj(x), then the communication channel has
value 0002 in one of the items and value 0012 in the other. By pairing d(i, j, x) with the assignment
item of value 0012, and pairing d′(i, j, x) with the assignment item of value 0002, the communication
channel sums up to 0112 in both pairs. Thus, by setting the communication channel in both bin
capacities tℓi, tℓj to the value 0112 we enforce Property (P3). See Section 4 for details.
Recall that we can construct numbers with up to Θ(N/k) = Θ(M/(k −1)) bits. By the Spar-
sification Lemma, each variable appears in O(1) clauses, and thus there are O(M) = O(N) tuples
(Ci, Cj, x) which need a communication channel. Hence, we cannot afford to construct one com-
munication channel per tuple – instead we need to reuse communication channels. The crucial
observation is that two tuples (Ci, Cj, x) and (Ci′, Cj′, x′) can use the same communication channel,
i.e. the same bit block, if the bins assigned to Ci and Cj are distinct from the bins assigned to
Ci′ and Cj′. We thus need an assignment of communication tuples to channels that ensures this

2.2
Generalisation Attempt: Vector Subset Sum
The challenge in the reduction presented in the previous section was to ensure communication
between bins, despite each item contributing to the sum of only one bin.
This is also the big
obstacle preventing more precise lower bounds (for example, an SETH-based lower bound ruling
out time 2o(n)T k−1−ε).
To support our intuition that this is the big obstacle, in this section we consider a problem in
which each item contributes to the sum of every bin, and show a simple tight SETH-based lower
bound. Specifically, let us consider the k-dimensional Vector Subset Sum problem:
Vector Subset Sum
Input:
Set of n vectors X ⊆Nk, target vector t ∈Nk.
Parameter:
T := ∥t∥1
Question:
Is there a subset S ⊆X such that Σ(S) = t?
Here, vectors in the subset S can be viewed as items being packed into k bins simultaneously, such
that each coordinate of the item contributes to the load of the respective bin. This problem, can be
solved in O(nT k) time by a straightforward dynamic programming algorithm. We show that this
is essentially optimal assuming SETH:
Theorem 2.1. Assuming SETH, for any k ⩾1, Vector Subset Sum in k dimensions cannot be
solved in time 2o(n)T k−ε for any ε > 0.
We show this lower bound by proving equivalence of Vector Subset Sum with the Subset
Sum problem, which in turn is known to be SETH-hard by [2]. Our equivalence is quite simple and
fits on two pages, see Section C. Thus, precise lower bounds are much easier to prove for a problem
in which items contribute to every bin.

Lower bound for (Weak) Grouped k-way Partition
It remains to describe the SETH-
based lower bound for (Weak) Grouped k-way Partition. For this overview, we ignore the
differences between the two problems, and focus on Grouped k-way Partition. As for k-Bin
Packing, we first show a reduction to a problem variant on multisets and with given targets for
each bin load, and later prove an equivalence that removes multisets and targets.
We reduce from K-SAT, so let φ be a K-CNF formula with N variables and M clauses. We will
construct an equivalent Grouped k-way Partition instance. In contrast to the ETH-based lower
bound for k-Bin Packing where we could afford O(N/k) = O(N/(k −1)) bits per constructed
item, now we can only afford (1 + ε)N/(k −1) bits, in particular the overhead from bookkeeping

can only be εN/(k −1) bits. To minimise the bookkeeping, we group the variables x1, . . . , xN into
supervariables X1, . . . , XN/a for a large enough constant a. For each supervariable Xi, we create a
group Gi containing items z(i, α) for every assignment α ∈{0, 1}a of the variables in Xi. As in the
k-Bin Packing reduction, bin k is viewed as a “dumpster”, and each supervariable is assigned to one
of the first k −1 bins, so that each bin is assigned N/(a(k −1)) supervariables. We use average-free
sets from additive combinatorics to ensure consistency of assignments to supervariable Xi, similarly
to the Subset Sum lower bound [2].
The main point where we need to diverge from the Subset Sum lower bound is that we cannot
group clauses into superclauses, because different clauses might be satisfied due to (super-)variables
assigned to different bins. In that case, a superclause item would need to communicate with multiple
bins. To avoid this, we therefore construct items for single clauses, specifically we create an item
y(j, i, α) for every clause Cj, supervariable Xi, and assignment α ∈{0, 1}a to Xi that satisfies Cj.
We need to ensure picking exactly one item y(j, i, α) for each j, meaning that each clause points to
some supervariable that causes it to be satisfied. For every clause Cj, the items y(j, i, α) are put in
the group GN/a+j. Hence in total, we construct groups G1, . . . , GN/a+M.
The grouping into supervariables helps to reduce the number of bookkeeping bits that enforce
picking exactly one item z(i, α) for each clause Ci. However, since we cannot consider superclauses,
the number of bookkeeping bits to ensure picking exactly one item y(j, i, α) for each clause Cj
would be too much. Circumventing this is where we rely on the group structure of Grouped k-
way Partition: We add s(k −1) −1 dummy items to each group GN/a+j, which ensures that at
least one item y(j, i, α) must be packed into the first k −1 bins for each Cj. We furthermore make
the dummy items favourable, in the sense that any solution needs to pack all dummy items into the
first k −1 bins. This then enforces that exactly one item y(j, i, α) must be packed for each Cj. The
details of the reduction are deferred to Section 5.

Hardness Assumptions
The Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) and the Strong Exponential
Time Hypothesis (SETH) are hypothesis about the running time of algorithms for the k-SAT prob-
lem: Given a boolean CNF formula φ with N variables, where each clause consists of at most k
literals, the task is to decide whether there is a satisfying assignment for φ. The Exponential Time
Hypothesis (ETH) is the conjecture that there exists δ > 0 such that there is no algorithm solving
3-SAT in time O(2δN) [30]. The Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) states that for any
ε > 0, there exists an integer K ⩾3 such that K-SAT cannot be solved in time O(2(1−ε)N) [31].
We use the following standard tool by Impagliazzo, Paturi and Zane [31]:
Lemma 3.3 (Sparsification Lemma [31], [23, Lemma 16.17]). For every integer K ⩾3 and every
λ > 0, there exist a constant ∆(K, λ) > 0 and an algorithm that, given a K-SAT instance Φ with N
variables, produces K-SAT instances φ1, . . . , φr with r ⩽2λN such that Φ is satisfiable if and only
if at least one φi is satisfiable. Moreover, φi has N variables and in φi each variable appears in at
most ∆(K, λ) clauses, for each i. The algorithm runs in time 2λN|Φ|O(1), where |Φ| is the number
of bits needed to represent the input formula.
Finally, we observe that if the input integers are bounded in a certain range, then we can
leverage the following property. We will show that this assumption can be made without any loss
of generality in Theorem 8.1.

Conclusion and Future Work
In this work, we proved a tight ETH-based lower bound for k-Bin Packing, answering an open
question posed by Jansen, Kratsch, Marx, and Schlotter [33].
Our main result (Theorem 1.1)
rules out time 2o(n)T o(k) for k-Bin Packing under ETH, improving upon Jansen et al.’s bound of
(nT)o(k/ log k) by eliminating the logarithmic factor loss in the exponent.
The hardness of k-Bin Packing has long been recognised as a central bottleneck in param-
eterised complexity. The log k-factor gap in the lower bound of Jansen et al. [33] was inherited
by numerous reductions to other problems, making it a fundamental barrier to establishing tight
complexity bounds. Our result resolves this issue and yields improved ETH-based lower bounds for
a wide range of problems in parameterised complexity, as detailed in Section B.
While our ETH-based lower bound for k-Bin Packing is tight, we leave as an intriguing open
problem whether a tight SETH-based lower bound can be established, i.e. whether k-Bin Packing
can be solved in time 2o(n)T k−1−ε for any ε > 0.
This may be a challenging task. One reason is that k-Bin Packing is closely related to the
Set Cover problem [42], but SETH-based lower bounds for Set Cover remain a notoriously open
problem [17]. Another reason is that there have been surprising algorithmic breakthroughs for k-
Bin Packing in some settings: Nederlof et al. [42] showed that for every k ⩾2 there exists εk > 0
such that k-Bin Packing can be solved in time O((2 −εk)n), thus shattering the hope of a lower
bound ruling out time O((2 −ε)n) for any ε > 0 for, say, 10-Bin Packing. It is conceivable that
a similar algorithmic breakthrough could also be possible for pseudopolynomial algorithms, and
k-Bin Packing could be solved in time 2o(n)T k−1−ε after all.
SETH Lower Bounds for Scheduling Problems
We demonstrate that this barrier can be
overcome for several multi-machine scheduling problems that are harder than k-Bin Packing.
Specifically, we prove tight SETH-based lower bounds for the scheduling problems Pk|rj|Cmax,
Pk||ΣwjCj, Pk||Lmax, Pk||ΣUj, and Pk||ΣpjUj (see Table 1). This establishes optimality of several
classical algorithms from the 60s and 70s [40, 45, 39].
After Abboud et al. [2] proved an SETH-based lower bound for Subset Sum, a fruitful research
direction has emerged: improving the dependence on n in the running times of pseudopolynomial-
time algorithms [10, 13, 22, 43, 37, 12, 11]. For instance, Fischer and Wennmann [22] improved
the running time for Pk||ΣpjUj from O(nT k) to e
O(n + T k). This raises the natural question: can

similar improvements be achieved for the other problems we study? Specifically, can Pk|rj|Cmax,
Pk||ΣwjCj, and Pk||ΣUj all be solved in time e
O(n+T k−1)? Similarly, to Subset Sum, one can also
study the running time dependence parameterised by pmax, the largest processing time. Perhaps all
Pk|rj|Cmax, Pk||ΣwjCj, and Pk||ΣUj can be solved in time e
O(n + (pmax)k−1)?
Finally, another interesting research direction is to study tight FPTASes for these scheduling
problems. Indeed, our (S)ETH-based lower bounds opens the possibility of tight FPTASes. For
example Pk||Cmax admits an approximation scheme running in time O(n) + (1/ε)O(k) as shown
by Jansen et al. [35]. Prior to our work, Chen et al. [16] showed that a running time of nO(1) +

Further Implications of ETH-Tight Lower Bound for Bin Packing
We list problems for which hardness results were proven via a reduction from k-Bin Packing, using
the (nT)o(k/ log k) lower bound for k-Bin Packing by Jansen et al. [33]. Since we strengthen that
lower bound in Theorem 1.1, we directly strengthen the lower bounds for the following problems.
Lafond et al. [38] studied the Cluster Editing problem on cographs, where the task is to edit at
most k edges in a given cograph G such that the resulting graph is a disjoint union of at most p
cliques.
They showed that the problem can be solved in nO(p) time and by a reduction from
Bin Packing ruled out time f(p) · no(p/ log p) assuming ETH for any computable function f(·). By
replacing the Bin Packing lower bound of Jansen et al. [33] with our Theorem 1.1, their reduction
immediately yields a higher lower bound.
Corollary B.1. There is no f(p)·no(p)-time algorithm for Cluster Editing on cographs parameterised
by the number of cliques p for any computable function f(·), unless ETH fails.
Javadi and Nikabadi [36] designed a kt · nO(k)-time algorithm for the k-Sparsest Cut problem in
graphs of treewidth t, where the task is to partition the vertices of a graph into k parts S1, . . . , Sk to
minimise maxi∈[k] |δ(Si)|/|Si|, where δ(Si) is the set of edges with exactly one endpoint in Si. They
complemented this result by showing a reduction from Bin Packing, which, by Jansen et al. [33]

rules out time no((k+t)/ log(k+t)) assuming ETH [36, Theorem 4.3]. By using Theorem 1.1 in their
reduction, we improve their lower bound.
Corollary B.2. There is no f(k, t)·no(k+t)-time algorithm for k-Sparsest Cut parameterised by the
number of parts k and the treewidth t of the input graph, for any computable function f(·), unless
ETH fails.
Blažej et al. [7, Theorem 1.2] reduced Bin Packing to the Eulerian Strong Component Arc

Corollary B.3. There is no f(v)·no(v)-time algorithm for Eulerian Strong Component Arc Deletion

Bliem et al. [9] considered a problem of EEF-Allocation, where we are given a set of n agents
and a set of m resources, the task is to find an allocation of the resources to the agents that is
