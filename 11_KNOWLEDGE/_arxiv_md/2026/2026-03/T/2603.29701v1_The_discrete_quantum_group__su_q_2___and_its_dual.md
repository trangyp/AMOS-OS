The discrete quantum group suq(2) and its dual
A. Van Daele (∗)
Abstract
Discrete quantum groups were introduced as duals of compact quantum groups by Podle´s
and Woronowicz in 1990 [10]. They have been studied intrinsically in [5] and [16]. In
a more recent note [27], we have given a slightly updated treatment, viewing the duality
between discrete and compact quantum groups as a special case of the more general duality
of algebraic quantum groups (as obtained in [17]) in [27].
Along these lines, we start in this paper with the discrete quantum group suq(2), not
constructed as the dual of the compact quantum group SUq(2) but rather from the Hopf
algebra deformation of the enveloping algebra of the Lie algebra of SU(2), as given by
Jimbo [6]. The passage to the discrete quantum group as studied in [16, 27] is not com-
pletely trivial as we will see. This is a known phenomenon.
We consider the dual of this discrete quantum group in the sense of duality of algebraic
quantum groups and see that this indeed is the compact quantum group SUq(2).

0. Introduction
When (A, ∆) is a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra, the dual space A′ of A is again a Hopf
algebra for the product and coproduct dual to the coproduct and product of A.
The
result is no longer true for infinite-dimensional Hopf algebras. In that case, it is possible
to consider the Sweedler dual, but there is no guarantee that this is large enough. When
(A, ∆) is a Hopf algebra with integrals, one can define a dual in the framework of multiplier
Hopf algebras. In fact this is possible for any multiplier Hopf algebra with integrals and
the dual is again a multiplier Hopf algebra with integrals.
We call these objects here
algebraic quantum groups.
Both the compact and discrete quantum groups can be seen as special cases of algebraic
quantum groups. Moreover the duality between compact and discrete quantum groups
then is a special case of the duality of algebraic quantum groups. Properties of compact
and discrete quantum groups and their duality follow from the general results of the duality
for algebraic quantum groups. In fact, in a way the general theory here is sometimes
simpler than these special cases.
Historically, compact and discrete quantum groups where studied before the theory of
algebraic quantum groups was developed. And unfortunately, the above point of view
didn’t make its way when discrete and compact quantum groups were investigated further.
Also compact quantum groups were developed prior to discrete quantum groups. In the
first place discrete quantum groups were introduced as duals of compact quantum groups.
And as a result, also in later work, discrete quantum groups were mostly viewed as duals
of compact quantum groups.
To illustrate these facts, we consider the compact quantum group SUq(2) as developed
by Woronowicz in [32]. He called it the Twisted SU(2) group. At the same time we have
the works on quantum groups by Drinfel’d [3] and Jimbo [6]. Their quantum groups are
deformations of the enveloping algebra of the Lie algebras of the classical Lie groups. In
particular, they have the deformation of the enveloping algebra of the Lie algebra su(2)
of the Lie group SU(2). The Drinfel’d and Jimbo approaches are similar to each other
but different from the work of Woronowicz. Apparently Woronowicz was at the time of
writing [32] not aware of the work of Drinfel’d and Jimbo.
In fact the approaches are dual to each other. The dual of the compact quantum group
SUq(2) is obtained in [10] as a discrete quantum group. This discrete quantum group is
intimately related with the deformations of the enveloping Lie algebra of SU(2) but there
is still a fundamental difference. The dual of SUq(2) in the sense of [10] is not a Hopf
algebra but rather a multiplier Hopf algebra whereas Drinfel’d and Jimbo have genuine
Hopf algebras.
Essentially what we do in this paper is clarify the connection between the two objects.
This connection is without any doubt well known, but I have the impression that it has
not yet been worked out in a rigorous way. We will discuss this further in the paper.

1. Discrete quantum groups
We start in this section with the definition of a discrete quantum group. There are several
possibilities and we refer to the appendix in the more recent paper [27] where we discuss
these various approaches.
Discrete quantum spaces
Let I be an index set. Associate to each member α of I a natural number n(α) and the
∗-algebra Aα of n(α) × n(α) complex matrices (with the usual ∗-algebra structure). Let
A be the (algebraic) direct sum of these algebras. Elements of A are written as (aα)α∈I
where aα ∈Aα for each α ∈I. By assumption only finitely many components aα are
non-zero. This direct sum A is again a ∗-algebra in the obvious sense. Observe that the
algebra has no unit, except when I is a finite set, but that the product is non-degenerate
(as a bilinear form). We say that the algebra A is a direct sum of matrix algebras. The
algebras Aα are considered as sitting inside A.
The identity in Aα will be denoted by 1α and it will also be considered as a minimal
central projection in A.
We will consider the multiplier algebra M(A).
It can be defined for any algebra with
a non-degenerate product and it is characterized as the largest algebra with identity in
which A sits as an essential ideal (see e.g. [15]). If A is a ∗-algebra, then so is M(A). In
this particular case, it is easy to see that elements of M(A) can be written as (aα)α∈I
where aα ∈Aα for each α ∈I, but without further restrictions. This algebra is called the
direct product of the algebras Aα.
We also consider the tensor product A ⊗A of A with itself and the multiplier algebra
M(A ⊗A) of A ⊗A. The algebra A ⊗A is the direct sum of the tensor product algebras
Aα ⊗Aβ where α, β ∈I and of course the multiplier algebra M(A ⊗A) is the direct
product of all these algebras. So elements in M(A ⊗A) are written as (aαβ)α,β∈I with
aαβ ∈Aα ⊗Aβ. When only finitely many components are non-zero, we get the elements
of A ⊗A.

2. The Hopf ∗-algebra At
In this paper, we treat the discrete quantum group suq(2). It has been studied in the
literature within the classical framework of Hopf algebras, see e.g. the work of Drinfel’d
[3] and Jimbo [6] and of many others. However, mostly the approach does not treat these
as discrete quantum groups in the operator algebra framework. This will be explained
further. On the other hand, in [32] the compact quantum group SUq(2) is studied while
in [10] the dual of SUq(2) is obtained as a discrete quantum group. Both developments
were independent of each other.
In this section we do the preliminary work. We start with the Hopf ∗-algebra (At, ∆)
that is a deformation of the enveloping algebra of the Lie algebra of SU(2), inspired by
the work of Jimbo in [6]. We need a detailed understanding of the finite-dimensional ∗-
representations of the underlying ∗-algebra At. In the next section, we use this information
to construct from it a discrete quantum group (as in [16, 27]).
The material we consider in this section has been treated at various places in different
forms but we here are interested in the way we need it to construct the discrete quantum
group as reviewed in the first section of this paper. We will come back to this in the
beginning of the next section.

6. Conclusion and further research
Although the results we have proven in this paper are expected, it turns out to be more
difficult to give an exact proof of these results. But this is precisely the aim of this note.
A few properties are still not completely clear. First we have the existence of the integral
on the Hopf ∗-algebras Ac
t that we have in Proposition 2.10. As we found in Proposition
2.13, it is relatively easy to show that there can not be a positive integral. But the proof
of that fact can not be used to prove the non-existing of a (possibly non-positive) integral.
Another question one can ask is whether or not there is an approach were results are
obtained in a way, closer to the original properties of the Hopf ∗-algebra At.
The ∗-homomorphism, from the quantum SUq(2) to our dual B, as mentioned in Propo-
sition 5.5 is shown to be surjective in Proposition 5.6, it is expected to also be injective
(and hence a ∗-isomorphism), but we did not prove this.
The cointegral h in the discrete quantum group suq(2) gives the integral φB on the dual
B. It would be interesting to use this to get concrete formulas for this integral.
Then there are the possible side results. We have considered, not only the representations
πn but also the representation π′
n and the related Definition 4.10, thus obtaining the
product of suq(2) with Z2.
This could be investigated further.
Also we did not look
further into the Hopf ∗-algebra Ac
t with c = 0 (see Remark 2.12). It could be interesting
to see if some of the techniques used in the paper shed some new light on this special case.
