THE DAVENPORT–LEWIS–SCHINZEL PROBLEM ON THE
REDUCIBILITY OF f(X) −g(Y )
ANGELOT BEHAJAINA, JOACHIM K¨ONIG, AND DANNY NEFTIN
Abstract. We solve the problem of Davenport–Lewis–Schinzel (DLS), originating in
the 1950s, regarding the reducibility of f(X) −g(Y ) ∈C[X, Y ]. This yields an almost-
complete solution to the Hilbert–Siegel problem: For a polynomial map f whose compo-
sition factors avoid only very specific low-degree polynomials, we explicitly describe over
which integers the fibers of f are reducible. We further apply the solution to stability of
iterates of f in arithmetic dynamics, and to solving the functional equation f(X) = g(Y )
in X, Y ∈C(z).
1. Introduction
Reducibility of polynomials is a central topic of interest in number theory, cf. [Sch00]. In
a prominent paper [Sch63], Schinzel poses nine problems concerning reducibility of poly-
nomials. As Zannier notes in [Sch07, Part E], the first three are “substantial, involving
several mathematical fields,” owing to their relation to monodromy, combinatorial group
theory, and the classification of finite simple groups (CFSG). The first problem originates
in the late 1950s [Ehr58], cf. [Cas68, Pg. 2], and is first stated by Davenport–Lewis–
Schinzel 1 in [DLS61]. We henceforth refer to it as the DLS problem. The second prob-
lem concerning the irreducibility of (f(X) −f(Y ))/(X −Y ) was solved by Fried [Fri70,
Thm. 1], while the third problem concerning the reducibility of separated polynomials
f(X1, . . . , Xm) −g(Y1, . . . , Yn) ∈Q[X1, . . . , Yn] was reduced to the DLS problem [DS64,
Thm. 2]. As we shall see below, the DLS problem arises naturally in several topics includ-
ing Hilbert’s irreducibility, low degree points in fibers, stability in arithmetic dynamics,
functional equations, Kronecker equivalence [M¨u98], intersections of lemniscates [Pak23a],
expanding polynomials [Tao12, Tao15], and sum-product estimates [BT12, Pf. Thm. 6].
The DLS problem is deceptively easy to state:
“For which polynomials f, g ∈C[X] of degree ≥2, is f(X) −g(Y ) ∈C[X, Y ] reducible?”
This is equivalent to the reducibility of the curve f(X) = g(Y ), as well as to the reducibility
of the fiber product of the maps f, g : P1
C →P1
C. Trivially, (a) f(X) −f(Y ) has a diagonal
factor X −Y , and (b) if f1(X) −g1(Y ) ∈C[X, Y ] is reducible, then so is the substitution
f1(f2(X)) −g1(g2(Y )) for f2, g2 ∈C[X] \ C with (deg(f2), deg(g2)) ̸= (1, 1). A pair f, g for
which f(X) −g(Y ) ∈C[X, Y ] is reducible but is not of the form (b) is called minimally
reducible. Among such pairs, those not arising from (a) via linear substitutions are called
nontrivial. The problem is then to classify the nontrivial minimally reducible pairs f, g.

The first nontrivial pair (f, g) = (T4, −T4) was given by Davenport, Lewis, and Schinzel
[DLS61], where Tn is the degree-n Chebyshev polynomial satisfying Tn(X + 1/X) =
Xn + 1/Xn.
The close relation of the DLS problem with monodromy groups of poly-
nomials was then revealed by Cassels–Guy [Cas68], proving the existence of nontrivial
pairs where deg(f) = deg(g) is 7 or 11.
The degree-7 polynomials, for example, arise
from the inequivalent actions of their monodromy group Mon(f) = PSL3(2) on points
and lines in the Fano plane, or equivalently, on lines and hyperplanes in F3
2, arising from
an outer automorphism of PSL3(2).
Explicit such polynomials were known by Klein’s
work [Kle79b, Kle79a], and an extended family was given by Birch [Cas68, Pg. 14]. After
extensive work on the case where f, g ∈C[X] are indecomposable polynomials (i.e., can-
not be written as a composition of two polynomials of degree > 1), e.g. [Sch67, Tve68],
Fried showed that the reducibility of f(X) −g(Y ) ∈C[X, Y ] for such f, g implies that
deg(f) = deg(g) is 7, 11, 13, 15, 21, or 31. This was announced in [Fri73] contingent on
the CFSG, and the proof is given in [Fri86b]. As above, the source of all such examples
lies in outer automorphisms of Mon(f). The indecomposable polynomials of these degrees
were then given by Cassou-Nogu`es–Couveignes [CNC99]. Throughout many of the above
stages, it was speculated and sometimes conjectured that no further minimally reducible
pairs exist.
The potentially-existing minimally reducible pair of polynomials with ≥4
branch points is also referred to as a “Cassels monster” [Cas68, Pg. 15].
For decomposable polynomials, the problem has so far remained largely open. Illus-
trating the difficulty of the decomposable case, Fried posed the (2, 3)-problem [Fri86b], cf.
[Fri12, Prob. 7.31],[Fri87, Pg. 15]: given an elliptic curve Y 2 = p(X), where p ∈C[X]
is simply-branched of degree 3, is there a substitution Y 7→g(Y ), X 7→f(X) for f, g ∈
C[X] \ C such that g(Y )2 = p(f(X)) is reducible over C? More generally, for integers
m, n ≥2, not both 2, the (m, n)-problem is stated similarly, replacing Y 2 and p(X) by
simply-branched polynomials of degrees m, n, resp., with disjoint branch loci. The decom-
posable case has been further studied including in determining when f(X)−g(Y ) ∈C[X, Y ]
admits a quadratic factor by Bilu [Bil99]; and in studying when f(X) −(α ◦f)(Y ) is re-
ducible for α ∈C[X] of degree 1 by Avanzi–Zannier [AZ03] and Fried–Gusi´c [Fri12, FG12].
Further references and context can be found on MathOverflow [Rur12, Tao12].
So far, all known minimally reducible pairs arise from the pair (T4, −T4) or one of the
above indecomposable pairs. On the other hand, little was known about the existence of
decomposable minimally reducible pairs: the (2, 3)-problem has remained widely open, and
it was even unclear whether minimally reducible pairs could be of unbounded degree.
We prove there exist no further examples, thereby solving the DLS problem:
Theorem 1.1. Let f, g ∈C[X] be of degree > 1. Then f(X)−g(Y ) is reducible in C[X, Y ]
if and only if one of the following holds for some f1, g1, µ ∈C[X] \ C with deg(µ) = 1:
(1) f and g have a common left composition factor h ∈C[X] of degree at least 2, that is,
f = h ◦f1 and g = h ◦g1;
(2) f = (µ ◦h1) ◦f1 and g = (µ ◦h2) ◦g1, where (h1, h2) is one of the pairs of polynomials
of degrees 7,11,13,15,21, or 31 given in [CNC99, §5];
(3) f = (µ ◦T4) ◦f1 and g = (µ ◦(−T4)) ◦g1.

We next discuss consequences to some of the topics mentioned in the first paragraph.
Reducible fibers and the Hilbert–Siegel problem. For f ∈Q[X] of degree d ≥2, Hilbert’s
Irreducibility Theorem (HIT) asserts the existence of infinitely many a ∈Z such that the
fiber f−1(a) ⊆C is irreducible2 over Q, that is, [Q(α) : Q] = d for any α ∈f−1(a). The
Hilbert–Siegel problem [Fri86a, Pg. 2], cf. [Fri12, §7.1.3], asks to determine, up to a finite
set, the set of integral exceptions for Hilbert’s theorem:
Redf(Z) := {a ∈Z | f−1(a) is reducible over Q}.
The problem is closely related to the DLS problem: Indeed, as in the proof of HIT, the
problem reduces to determining the values sets g(Q) for which g(Q)∩Redf(Z) is infinite, or
equivalently to determining when is the curve f(X) = g(Y ) reducible for a Siegel function
g ∈Q(X), that is, a rational function whose value set g(Q) contains infinitely many
integers. For a polynomial g ∈Q[X], this is equivalent to the reducibility of f(X)−g(Y ) ∈
Q[X, Y ], that is, to the DLS problem over Q.
Clearly, Redf(Z) contains every integer in f(Q), and furthermore every integer in f1(Q)
for a decomposition f = f1◦f2 in Q[X] with deg(f1) > 1. The problem is then to determine
whether Redf(Z) \ S f1(Q) is finite, where the union runs over all left composition factors
f1 of f with deg(f1) > 1. For indecomposable f ∈Q[X] of degree > 5, the finiteness
of Redf(Z) \ f(Q) was shown in [Fri86a, Thm. 1.2] by Fried, extending [Fri74]. Degree-5
examples f ∈Q[X] for which this set is infinite were constructed by D`ebes–Fried [DF99].
Several variants of the problem have been since considered, see §1.2. However, the original
Hilbert–Siegel problem has so far remained largely open for decomposable polynomials.

Stability in arithmetic dynamics. Stability of polynomials under iterates is a central topic
in arithmetic dynamics [BIJ+19, §19]. A polynomial f ∈Q[X] is called stable over a ∈Q,
if the fibers over a of the n-fold iterates f◦n := f ◦· · · ◦f are irreducible for all n ∈N.
In particular, when the fiber over a of f◦(n−1) is irreducible whereas the one of f◦n is not,
f◦n is called newly reducible over a; cf. [CCF+12] and [IJO+21] for investigations of this
phenomenon. It is natural to ask whether f◦n can be newly reducible over infinitely many
a ∈Z. In other words, when is Redf◦n(Z) \ Redf◦n−1(Z) infinite? As a direct consequence
of Theorem 1.2, we obtain:
Corollary 1.3. Let f ∈Q[X] be a polynomial of degree > 1 that does not factor through
a polynomial of degree 2 or 4, and let n ≥2. Then Redf◦n(Z) \ Redf(Z) is finite.
Allowing quadratic factors, counterexamples with n = 2 exist, the simplest example
being f = X2, due to the factorization X4 + 4Y 4 = (X2 −2XY + 2Y 2)(X2 + 2XY + 2Y 2).
Functional equations. For which polynomials f, g ∈C[X] of degree ≥2, does the functional
equation f(X) = g(Y ) have a solution in rational functions X = X(z), Y = Y (z) ∈
C(z)? The question admits several variants and draws its motivation from number theory,
functional equations, Nevanlinna theory, and dynamical systems, see §1.2.
Attempts to answer this question and its variants naturally divide into two cases ac-
cording to the reducibility of f(X) −g(Y ) ∈C[X, Y ]. However, with the exception of the
cases g = cf for c ∈C×, and cases where deg(f) ≫deg(g), see [Pak23b, Thm. 1.3],[Pak09,
Thm. 1.2], and [Fri23], little appears in the literature on the reducible case of the question.
The combination of Theorem 1.1 with [AZ03, Thm. 1] gives the following simple res-
olution of the reducible case. First, we dispose of solutions that are not genuinely new.
Clearly, if f1(X) = g1(Y ), for f1, g1 ∈C[X], has a solution X(z), Y (z) ∈C(z) \ C, then
this is also a solution to (w ◦f1)(X) = (w ◦g1)(Y ) for w ∈C[X]. Such solutions satisfy
q(X(z), Y (z)) = 0 for an irreducible factor q(X, Y ) of f1(X) −g1(Y ) ∈C[X, Y ] (and not
only of f(X) −g(Y )). Say an irreducible factor q(X, Y ) of f(X) −g(Y ) ∈C[X, Y ] is right-
reduced if it is not a factor of f1(X) −g1(Y ) ∈C[X, Y ] for any decomposition f = w ◦f1,
g = w ◦g1 with w, f1, g1 ∈C[X] \ C and deg(w) > 1. A solution is reduced if it annihilates
a right-reduced factor. The goal is then to determine when reduced solutions exist.

1.1. On the proof. A major reason why the DLS problem was previously deemed inac-
cessible for arbitrary decomposable polynomials is the complexity of monodromy groups of
decomposable polynomials in comparison to indecomposable ones. This is especially true
for polynomials f with solvable monodromy Mon(f), where the “largeness” of monodromy
from [KNR24] does not apply, and no analogous constraints are known.
Instead, in the solvable case, we observe that the minimal reducibility of a pair f, g ∈
C[X]\C imposes heavy constraints on their monodromy. More specifically, writing f = h◦fr
and h = h2 ◦fr−1 for indecomposable fr−1, fr ∈C[X], the kernel K of the restriction map
Mon(f) →Mon(h) naturally embeds into Γd, where Γ := Mon(fr) and d = deg(h). If
deg(fr) > 2, we show that minimal reducibility forces K to be diagonal, that is, its d
projections to Γ are injective. When deg(fr) = 2, we show that K embeds in C2 × C2.
This is carried out in §4.
In contrast, we obtain lower bounds on the rank of K via the following two main ideas:
Firstly, we consider the monodromy group Γ2 := Mon(fr−1 ◦fr) of a two-step right-factor
of f. We use “largeness properties” for the kernel K2 = ker(Γ2 →Mon(fr−1)), developed
in the companion paper [BKN26]. If for example Mon(fr) ∼= Cq for a prime q, these show
that K2 contains an abelian subgroup of rank at least dr−1 −1, where dr−1 = deg(fr−1).

1.2. Related work. Several variants of the original Hilbert–Siegel problem have been
considered. Firstly, it is natural to consider degree-d maps f : X →P1

(smooth projective) curve X of positive genus. In the general case, where MonQ(f) =

for other cases.
For indecomposable f ∈Q[X] with deg(f) > 20, the analogous set
Redf(Q), of rational values with reducible fibers, is the union of f(Q) and a finite set, by the
combination of theorems of M¨uller [M¨u95] and Guralnick–Shareshian [GS07], see [KN24,
Thm. 5.4]. Similar results were shown for indecomposable rational functions f ∈Q(X)
of sufficiently large degree [Mon24, Thm. 1.2], using [NZ24]. The question also recently
arose in the context of algebraic points of fixed degree d in fibers of maps f : X →P1

over rational points, as considered by Derickx–Rawson [DR25]. Over fields k of positive
characteristic, except for the classification of f, g ∈k[X] for which f(X) −g(Y ) has a

Hilbert–Siegel, involving more general rational maps, remain wide-open.

2. Basic setup and Preliminaries
2.1. Basic setup. Throughout the paper, k is a field of characteristic 0 and k its algebraic
closure.
A map f : X →Y over k is a finite (dominant and generically unramified)
morphism of (smooth irreducible projective) varieties defined over k. This induces a field
extension k(X)/k(Y) via the pullback f∗: k(Y) →k(X), given by h 7→h ◦f. The degree
deg(f) of f is then defined as [k(X) : k(Y)]. We say f is indecomposable if it is of degree
> 1 and is not a composition of two maps of degree > 1. Say that the function field k(X)
has genus g if the curve4 X has genus g.
A rational function f ∈k(X) \ k, then induces an k(x)/k(t) of rational functions fields
such that f(x) = t. The degree of f is then max{deg(f1), deg(f2)}, where f = f1/f2 for
coprime f1, f2 ∈k[X]. We say f, g ∈k(X) \ k are linearly related over k if there exist
µ, ν ∈k(X) of degree 1 (also called linear fractionals) such that f = µ ◦g ◦ν. We shall
denote k-rational places of k(t) by t 7→a for a ∈P1(k) = k ∪{∞}.

Appendix A. Fried’s (m, n)-problem
As a consequence of Theorem 6.1, we have the following answer to Fried’s (m, n)-problem.
Corollary A.1. Let P, Q ∈C[X] be simply branched polynomials of degrees m := deg(Q) ≥
2 and n := deg(P) ≥max{m, 3} such that P and Q do not satisfy a relation P = Q ◦µ
with µ ∈C[X] linear. If n = 3, then assume moreover that the sets of finite branch points
of P and of Q are disjoint. Then Q(f(X)) −P(g(Y )) is irreducible for all f, g ∈C[X] \ C.
Note that the assumption P ̸= Q ◦µ is required for P(X) −Q(Y ) ∈k[X, Y ] to be
irreducible, and clearly holds if the branch loci of P and Q are disjoint.
Proof. Assume on the contrary that Q(f(X)) −P(g(Y )) ∈C[X, Y ] is reducible for some
f(X), g(X) ∈C[X] \ C. Let (hQ, hP ) be a corresponding minimally reducible pair, so that
in particular hQ is a left-factor of Q ◦f and hP is a left-factor of P ◦g.
Note first that, as in Remark 2.11, a simply branched polynomial of degree n ≥4 cannot
be the left-factor of a Ritt move. Therefore, when n ≥4, the polynomial hP must admit
(the simply branched, hence indecomposable) P as a left-factor. By Theorem 1.1, P is
either a left factor of Q ◦f, or P is one of the exceptional polynomials in Theorem 1.1(2).
The latter case cannot occur since the exceptional polynomials in Theorem 1.1(2) are not
simply-branched. In the former case, the same reasoning shows that P = Q ◦µ for a linear
µ ∈C[X], contradicting our assumptions.

theorem, and since the branch loci of Tm and −T2 are contained in that of T3, it follows

in the one of P. On the other hand, every non-linear left factor of Q ◦f must share at
