Growth and decay are system-level properties of chemical reaction networks (CRNs) relevant
from prebiotic chemistry to cellular metabolism. Their properties are typically analyzed through
the kinetics of particular models, which requires specification of the full set of kinetic laws and
parameters. In this work, we derive stoichiometry-based constraints on the growth (or shrinkage)
rate, in the balanced-growth regime of scalable CRNs.
The resulting bounds are controlled by
a topological quantity, the maximum amplification factor, defined via a von Neumann max–min
problem over feasible fluxes as illustrated by numerical tests on random-network ensembles of CRNs.
We argue for the relevance of our results in the context of origin of life studies, but also for designing
synthetic chemical reaction networks.
Autocatalysis and positive feedback are ubiquitous
in biochemical organization and in candidate prebiotic
chemistries: they enable self-maintenance, replication-
like amplification, and, in open settings, persistent
growth [1–3]. Stoichiometric frameworks have recently
made the identification of autocatalytic subnetworks op-
erational via purely structural criteria and algorithms
that identify minimal autocatalytic motifs and their em-
beddings inside large networks [4–7]. This progress raises
a natural quantitative question: once a network is topo-
logically capable of amplification, what controls how fast
it can grow or decay?
From a dynamical perspective, growth rates depend
on nonlinear kinetics and on rate constants that can
vary over orders of magnitude and are often poorly con-
strained. This creates a gap: topology-based notions of
autocatalysis certify feasibility, while kinetics determines
realized performance. Bridging this gap requires a mixed
kinetic–topological metric that quantifies amplification
strength. Prior structural results in mass-action settings
exist in restricted regimes (e.g., special coupling or dilute
limits) [8–10]; here we identify a broad balanced-growth
regime in which a single metric controls the achievable
growth rate.

Equation (8) turns the MAF α into a sharp, parameter-
sparse descriptor of growth. First, it yields a strict decay
certificate for non-autocatalytic networks: if α ≤1, then
Λ ≤0 independently of the rate constants and of the
choice of stable fixed point. Second, it imposes an ex-
plicit upper bound for autocatalytic networks: even under
arbitrary kinetic tuning, the balanced growth rate can-
not exceed ∥S−∥k(α −1). While Eq. (8) implies that any
growing network must be autocatalytic, i.e. Λ > 0 =⇒
α > 1, the converse does not hold: an autocatalytic net-
work need not exhibit positive growth. In other words,
the sign of Λ fundamentally depends on the kinetics when
α > 1.

Eq. (8) leads to a clear separation between stoichiometric
and kinetic structure of the network. Indeed, the growth

Discussion and outlook— The bounds of Eq. (8) turn
a purely structural quantity, the MAF α, into a quan-
titative control parameter for balanced growth. Opera-
tionally, one can compute α from stoichiometry [12, 14,
20], then estimate ∥S−∥k from input stoichiometries and
rough kinetic scales, to obtain an upper bound on bal-
anced growth.
An important observation is that, for a fixed topology,
varying rate constants changes Λ continuously, within
limits set by α. This suggests the following evolutionary
scenario for the improvement in chemical growth. One
route is kinetic tuning under fixed stoichiometric con-
straints, which moves Λ within the admissible interval
while the MAF α remains unchanged. The large degener-
acy of networks observed for certain MAF values suggest
the possibility of neutral evolution at fixed MAF value.
The
second
route
is
structural
modification
(adding/removing reactions, merging motifs, rewiring
autocatalytic components), which changes α itself and
thereby shifts the admissible bound.
In evolutionary
terms, this means a punctuated equilibrium with topo-
logical network changes playing the role of mutations
that alter the admissible growth bound, while subse-
quent kinetic adaptation and neutral evolution adjust Λ
within the available range [21–23].
Beyond this scenario, our result has also practical im-
plications for open chemical reactors because in a contin-
uously stirred reactor with dilution rate d, the population
scale following a possible transient satisfies ˙Ω= (Λ−d) Ω.
Then, sustained growth requires Λ > d, therefore Λ acts
as a dilution threshold above which the system is washed
out. Combined with Eq. (8), this yields a necessary struc-
tural condition for persistence against dilution: even with
optimal kinetic tuning, survival at dilution d is excluded
unless α > 1 and d lies below the corresponding growth
bound [24].
In
compartmentalized
systems
with
a
steady-
composition, the growth rate Λ controls the rate of
change of any extensive quantity besides that of the
global population [25]. The compartment volume is one
such quantity, and indeed, experiments have shown that
the compartment volume can inform on the autocatalytic
growth of small confined chemical species and that it
represents a proxy for fitness when coupling many such
compartments together [26].
These interpretations suggest several natural exten-
sions of the present work. First, it opens a route towards
network engineering: using only knowledge of stoichiom-
etry, one could design networks, that could potentially
drive growth above a target threshold, or induce a ro-
bust decay for α ≤1 (robust decay). Pursuing this pro-
gram requires composition rules for α: which elementary
network operations (adding a reaction, merging species,
etc.) increase or decrease α [27, 28], and by how much?
For related results in the economics literature, see [29].
Such a set of design rules would make α predictive un-
der network modification and enable sharp statements
about minimal structural changes needed to shift admis-
sible growth bounds.
A second direction is to close the gap between topolog-
ical capability (α) and dynamical realization (the steady
flux).
Identifying sufficient dynamical conditions that
force the steady flux to approach MAF-optimal patterns
would turn the universal bound into a quantitative es-
timate for restricted kinetic classes and clarify which
motifs control performance. At the same time, an im-

B3. MAF of reducible networks
Von Neumann in [6] and Theorem 9.10 in [4] prove that there exists a unique MAF for irreducible semi-autonomous
networks. Since autonomous networks are also semi-autonomous, irreducible autonomous networks have a unique
MAF.
We now turn to the case of reducible networks. If the network decomposes into disjoint irreducible components
that do not exchange mass—equivalently, if (S+, S−) can be brought into block-triangular form after a suitable
permutation—then the maximum amplification factor is simply the maximum of the MAFs of the individual irreducible
blocks.
More generally, a reducible network may consist of an irreducible core whose species feed one or more downstream
subnetworks. In this case, any flux through downstream reactions necessarily increases the consumption of species in
the core without providing compensating production within it. As a result, activating downstream subnetworks cannot
increase the uniform production-to-consumption ratio across species, and can only reduce the achievable amplification
factor. Optimal fluxes therefore concentrate on the dominant irreducible component.
In this sense, α functions as a “best-module” structural score and is determined solely by the irreducible component
with the highest MAF.
B4. MAF of minimal networks
Observe from the second formulation of the MAF (Equation S7) that the optimal solution x > 0 for a CRN (S+, S−)

S+x ≥α S−x.
(S9)

reactions otherwise α would not be the maximum value that satisfies the inequality. Given the subnetwork (S+, S−)
and x, we obtain the following generalized eigenvalue problem:

(S10)
with the constraint x > 0. The relation between solutions of Eq. (S7) and the generalized eigenvalue problem is
studied in Ref. [7].
An autonomous network is minimal if there is no subnetwork that is also autonomous. Since there is no species that
is never consumed, such a network must also be irreducible. It has been shown that minimal autonomous networks
have an equal number of species and reactions [1, 3, 8], and that their input matrix can be rearranged into a diagonal
form. Clearly, then, the network is irreducible and S−is non-singular. Using the results in [7], the problem of finding
MAF reduces to the generalized eigenvalue problem of Eq. (S10), which further reduces to the standard eigenvalue

(S11)
