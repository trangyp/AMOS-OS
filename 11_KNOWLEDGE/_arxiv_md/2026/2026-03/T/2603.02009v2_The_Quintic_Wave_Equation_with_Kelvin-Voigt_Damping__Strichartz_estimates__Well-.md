Abstract. This paper investigates the critical quintic wave equation in a 3D bounded domain
subject to locally distributed Kelvin-Voigt damping. The study tackles two major mathematical
challenges: the severe loss of derivatives induced by the localized thermo-viscous dissipation
and the aggressive nature of the critical nonlinear term.
First, we establish a robust well-
posedness theory for arbitrarily large initial data. By shifting the analysis to the frequency
space via a Littlewood-Paley decomposition , we employ Bernstein’s inequalities to lift the
damping term into an L2 framework, allowing Strichartz estimates to be applied effectively. In
the second part, we prove the uniform exponential stabilization of the energy. To overcome the
reduction of the residual to the H−2 level caused by the Kelvin-Voigt mechanism, we utilize the
microlocal defect measure framework. The core of our stabilization proof relies on combining
the critical Strichartz regularity L4

1. Introduction
1.1. The phenomenon of energy concentration. The concentration phenomena associated
with the "quintic wave" (generally described by the Nonlinear Quintic Schrödinger Equation
- NLS or the Semilinear Quintonic Wave Equation) refer to situations where the energy or
mass of the wave accumulates intensely in a very small region of space (a point or a small
neighborhood), often leading to what is called a "blow-up" (singularity in finite time). From
a physical point of view, this represents the collapse of a wave packet, while mathematically
it corresponds to a concentration of mass (norm) or energy, where the solution profile loses
compactness in Sobolev spaces.
Mass/Energy Concentration: Mathematically, a sequence of
solutions is said to concentrate if the mass (norm) or energy (norm) concentrates at a point
when the concentrated mass is often a discrete quantity, equal to the norm of the "ground
state". There is a threshold profile, namely, scattering threshold(the center-stable manifold),
that separates solutions that scatter (go to zero) from those that concentrate and collapse (blow-
up). Concentration is analyzed through scaling transformations (scaling invariant), where the
solution is rescaled to maintain a constant energy norm while the wave packet size goes to zero.
The phenomenon of "collapse" or blow-up in quintic wave equations (and other critical non-
linear wave equations) can perfectly occur in bounded domains. In fact, the presence of edges
(bounded domains) can facilitate blow-up compared to free space, as confinement prevents en-
ergy from dispersing to infinity, forcing it to concentrate. Here is the detailed analysis based on
mathematical physics:
1. Collapse in Bounded Domains.
From a physical point of view, in a bounded domain, the initial energy "trapped" in the
volume, combined with the non-linear focus (typical of the quintic wave), can overcome the
linear mechanisms of dispersion. The wave "collapses" because the energy concentrates at a
point (or region) in finite time, resulting in the singularity.
2. Mathematical View and Sobolev Spaces.
Mass/Energy Concentration: Mathematical collapse is the blow-up of the energy norm or
norms in Sobolev spaces, such as:
• As time approaches the collapse time, the solution ceases to be compact, meaning that
the gradient of the solution becomes infinite, concentrating at one or more points.
•
Loss of Compactness:
In bounded domains, immersion theorems (such as Rellich-
Kondrachov) guarantee initial compactness, but the quintic nature of nonlinearity is
"critical" in energy space. When the blow-up occurs, this compactness is violated, as the
solution profile becomes singular (tends to a Dirac delta-type distribution in the limit).
3. Factors Affecting Collapse:

Boundary: The shape of the domain and boundary conditions (e.g., Dirichlet at the edge)

Damping: If the system includes damping terms (damped wave equations), it is possible for

1.3. Literature Overview. The stabilization of wave equations with localized dissipation has
been extensively studied within the geometric control framework initiated by Bardos, Lebeau,
and Rauch [2], where sharp conditions linking observability, control, and decay were established.
Subsequent developments clarified the microlocal propagation mechanisms governing energy con-
centration, particularly near the boundary, through the analysis of generalized bicharacteristics

[5, 22, 23, 25, 26, 27, 21]. Particularly those involving inhomogeneous media, variable coeffi-
cients, or complex boundary configurations, the classical localized GCC fails due to the existence
of trapped rays. These are generalized bicharacteristics that oscillate indefinitely without ever
entering a standard localized control region, allowing high-frequency energy to concentrate and
consequently preventing uniform exponential decay.
In the presence of Kelvin–Voigt damping, however, the analytical structure changes signifi-
cantly. The damping operator introduces higher-order spatial effects which interact nontrivially
with geometric multipliers, frequently requiring additional regularity assumptions. This phe-
nomenon has been examined in various stabilization settings, including localized and degenerate
dissipation mechanisms (see [1]), [24].
From the dispersive viewpoint, Strichartz estimates play a decisive role in handling critical
nonlinearities. In domains with boundary, these estimates require refined harmonic analysis tools,
as developed by Blair, Smith, and Sogge [3], extending earlier Euclidean results of Keel and Tao
[19]. The interaction between boundary geometry and dispersive smoothing was further explored
by Burq, Lebeau, and Planchon [4], providing a fundamental framework for energy-critical wave
equations.
Microlocal defect measures and H-measures provide the natural language for describing high-
frequency energy concentration in such problems. These tools, introduced by Gérard [16] and
Tartar [31], have become central in stabilization theory, particularly in configurations where
compactness arguments fail or where dissipation degenerates.
The present work combines these perspectives. By integrating harmonic analysis, nonlinear
energy methods, and microlocal propagation techniques, we obtain a stability theory capable of
handling Kelvin–Voigt mechanisms at the critical level while preserving the geometric intuition
underlying classical control results.
1.4. Contributions of the present article. The study of the critical wave equation with
localized Kelvin-Voigt damping presents a notorious challenge: balancing the aggressive nature
of the quintic nonlinearity in 3 spatial dimensions with the severe loss of derivatives induced by
the viscous term div(a(x)∇ut). In the first part of this paper, we explore the classical route.
By relying on energy methods and seeking strong solutions, one can seemingly bypass the heavy
machinery of dispersive estimates.
However, this classical approach exacts a heavy toll: to
control the critical nonlinearity and guarantee uniqueness, the initial data must be assumed to
be strictly small, and the solutions are forced into the restrictive Shatah-Struwe class, requiring
u ∈L4(0, T; L12(Ω)).
To overcome this bottleneck and remove the small data restriction, the second part of our work
shifts the paradigm from the physical domain to the frequency space, deploying the modern arse-
nal of Harmonic Analysis. The application of dispersive techniques to boundary value problems
possesses a rich and profound history. The seminal works of Smith and Sogge [28] demonstrated
how wave dispersion interacts with domains and rough boundaries. Subsequently, the celebrated
contributions of the trio Burq, Lebeau, and Planchon [4] (BLP) solidified the use of spectral
projectors and Littlewood-Paley theory tailored to Dirichlet boundary conditions, allowing
Strichartz estimates to flourish outside of Rn. Furthermore, treating the singular damping terms
via the Duhamel formulation crucially relies on the foundational machinery of Christ and Kiselev
[10], which elegantly translates homogeneous dispersive bounds into retarded estimates, allowing
low-frequency perturbations to be seamlessly absorbed.
The core difficulty of the Kelvin-Voigt term lies in its H−1 regularity, which makes it inher-
ently hostile to the classical Strichartz estimates on the right-hand side of the wave equation.
Classical Strichartz estimates are calibrated for data and source terms with compatible scaling
and regularity. The Kelvin-Voigt operator introduces a derivative loss which is incompatible
with a direct application of the dispersive framework. The obstruction is therefore not merely
dispersive, but functional-analytic in nature.

By introducing a Littlewood-Paley decomposition, we separate the wave into macroscopic
(low-frequency) and microscopic (high-frequency) components. This dual-frequency mechanism
is the central stabilizing principle of our nonlinear analysis:
• The Low-Frequency Regime: At low frequencies, the spectral projection P≤N com-
bined with Bernstein’s inequalities converts spectral localization into effective derivative
gains. In this regime, the projected damping term behaves as a bounded perturbation
in L2, thereby completely neutralizing the derivative loss and allowing the Strichartz
estimates to perform flawlessly without destroying the regularity.
• The High-Frequency Regime: Conversely, at high frequencies, Bernstein’s inequality
cannot be applied without yielding unfavorable positive powers of the frequency thresh-
old N. To circumvent this severe derivative loss, we fundamentally shift our operator
strategy by keeping the localized damping within the principal part of the wave oper-
ator. Applying the high-frequency projector P>N generates a non-trivial commutator
[P>N, a(x)] with the variable damping coefficient. By leveraging the theory of smooth
spectral multipliers and Schur’s test, we rigorously demonstrate that this commutator
acts as a smoothing operator of order −1. This remarkable cancellation completely ab-
sorbs the spatial derivative of the Kelvin-Voigt term without any asymptotic loss. With
the damping safely incorporated on the left-hand side, the remaining high-frequency evo-
lution is ultimately controlled by the asymptotic smallness of the initial energy tail (as
N →∞) and a short-time perturbation argument, allowing us to seamlessly close the
critical Strichartz estimates.
This spectral strategy yields our second and main result: a robust and complete local well-
posedness theory. Through a delicate bootstrap argument—combining the smallness of the high-
frequency tail with the smooth behavior of the low-frequency component over a short time—we
completely bypass the need for small initial energy. Consequently, we establish the existence and
uniqueness of solutions for arbitrarily large initial data, providing a definitive structural answer
to the conflict between the critical nonlinear power and the Kelvin-Voigt dissipation.
Having established the global well-posedness framework, the third and final objective of this
paper is to prove the uniform exponential stabilization of the system.
The presence of the
Kelvin-Voigt damping div(a(x)∇ut) restricted to a localized region ω introduces severe obstacles
for the asymptotic analysis. Unlike frictional damping, the viscous nature of our dissipation
alters the regularity of the residual, reducing the convergence from the H−1 to the H−2 level.
Consequently, classical compactness-uniqueness arguments fail to provide the necessary strong
convergence to propagate the damping effects.
To bypass this obstacle, we employ the powerful framework of microlocal defect measures.
While the loss of derivatives prevents direct propagation everywhere, the microlocal structure
is perfectly preserved outside the damping region. The fundamental breakthrough in our sta-
bilization proof is the application of the sharp Unique Continuation Property (UCP) for the
wave equation with critical potentials, as established by Duyckaerts, Zhang, and Zuazua [14].
By exploiting the precise critical Strichartz regularity u ∈L4(0, T; L12(Ω)) inherited from our
well-posedness theory, we can close the defect-measure contradiction argument directly. This
completely eliminates the dependence on the truncation parameters without relying on artificial
secondary contradictions, bridging the Harmonic Analysis results from the first part of the paper
with the Control Theory goals of the second.
Finally, we demonstrate that our microlocal approach is entirely oblivious to volumetric con-
straints, relying solely on the dynamical interception of the generalized bicharacteristic flow. This
allows our stabilization results to be seamlessly coupled with the highly complex, “non-invasive”
damping geometries developed in [6, 7, 8]. In such configurations, the dissipative region can be
constructed with an arbitrarily small Lebesgue measure, thus providing a definitive and elegant
mathematical answer to the problem of trapped rays in inhomogeneous media.

6.5. Role of the CGC / GCC and Non-Invasive Geometries. Under the standard Geo-
metric Control Condition (GCC), uniform stabilization follows immediately since the defect
measure µ is transported along the bicharacteristic flow and trivially annihilated upon entering
the damping region ω.
However, in many physical scenarios—particularly those involving inhomogeneous media, vari-
able coefficients, or complex boundary configurations—the classical localized GCC fails due to
the existence of trapped rays. These are generalized bicharacteristics that oscillate indefinitely
without ever entering a standard localized control region, allowing high-frequency energy to
concentrate and consequently preventing uniform exponential decay.
To overcome this fundamental geometric obstruction without trivially damping the entire
domain, one can appeal to the highly ubiquitous, “non-invasive” damping geometries developed
in the works of Cavalcanti et al. [6, 7, 8]. The core strategy in these papers relies on the intricate
topological construction of a dissipative region ω that acts as a pervasive geometrical net.
Specifically, it is possible to design an open set ω satisfying two seemingly antagonistic prop-
erties:
• Dynamical interception: The region ω is strategically distributed such that it inter-
sects every single generalized bicharacteristic within a uniform finite time T0 > 0.
• Arbitrarily small volume: The Lebesgue measure of the damping region can be made
arbitrarily small, i.e., meas(ω) < ϵ for any given ϵ > 0.
From the microlocal perspective, this construction reveals a profound property of the defect
measure framework: it is completely insensitive to volumetric and metric considerations. The
measure µ strictly encodes high-frequency concentration along the dynamical flow. As long as
the damping set ω dynamically intercepts all invariant sets of the flow, the relation µ = 0 on ω
will systematically propagate along the bicharacteristics, ultimately forcing µ ≡0 everywhere in
the domain.
Therefore, even when the underlying non-homogeneous metric strictly forbids stabilization
via classical localized damping, the robust microlocal structure of the Kelvin-Voigt dissipation
presented in this paper can be seamlessly coupled with these geometrically complex, arbitrarily
small-measure damping sets to guarantee global exponential stabilization.
