Terahertz Channel Transmission in Dielectric
Waveguide Near PCB Substrate
Wenbo Liu, Jiabiao Zhao, Kefeng Huang, Boquan Xu, Peian Li, Yang Cao, Weidong Hu, Jianjun Ma

Abstract—The growing demand for high-capacity, low-loss short-
reach links in highly integrated electronic systems makes it
necessary
to
understand
how
terahertz
(THz)
dielectric
waveguides
behave
in
realistic
PCB-level
packaging
environments.
In
this
article,
we
investigate
the
channel
transmission of a 3D-printed polypropylene dielectric waveguide
placed near representative PCB substrates. Continuous-wave
THz measurements are carried out for bare, fully copper-clad,
and periodic copper-trace PCBs with different waveguide-PCB
separations, while terahertz time-domain spectroscopy is used to
characterize the dielectric properties of the substrate materials.
In
parallel,
an
equivalent
radiation-channel
model
and
simulations are employed to interpret the proximity-induced
excess attenuation. Most notably, direct contact with a bare PCB
produces severe and frequency-selective excess loss, whereas a
waveguide-facing
copper
layer
suppresses
substrate-assisted
leakage and reduces the excess loss, with the attenuation rapidly
decreasing as the clearance increases. These results reveal the
dominant near-field coupling mechanisms between dielectric
waveguides and nearby PCB structures, and provide practical
guidance for packaging-aware THz interconnect design.
Index
Terms—Terahertz
dielectric
waveguides;
short-reach
interconnects; printed circuit boards (PCBs); evanescent-field
coupling; packaging-aware transmission.

I. INTRODUCTION
The
relentless
scaling
of
data-centric
computing,
heterogeneous integration, and chiplet-based architectures
continues to raise the performance requirements for short-
reach interconnects in terms of aggregate throughput, latency,
energy
efficiency,
and packaging
density
[1].
As
data
movement increasingly becomes the dominant bottleneck in
high-performance
electronic
systems,
the
migration
of
interconnect technology toward the millimeter-wave and
terahertz (THz) regimes has emerged as a promising route to
overcome the bandwidth limitations of conventional electrical
links [2, 3]. In particular, beyond conventional chip-to-chip
and board-level interconnects, emerging THz systems also
require direct high-capacity transmission between antennas
and proximal chips, circuits, or electronic modules, such as
antenna-to-transceiver
feeds,
front-end-to-backend
routing
inside compact packages [2-4], and short-reach links between
distributed sensing, computing, and communication units.
However,
in
this
frequency
range,
especially
at
THz
frequencies,
metallic
transmission
media
suffer
from
increasingly severe conductor loss, dielectric loading, parasitic
coupling, and packaging-sensitive impedance discontinuities,
all of which degrade signal integrity and limit scalable
integration
[3,
5,
6].
Against
this
backdrop,
dielectric
waveguides have attracted growing attention as a compelling
THz guided medium because they confine electromagnetic
energy primarily in low-loss dielectric cores and surrounding
air
[7],
thereby
largely
avoiding
conductor-dominated
dissipation while retaining broadband and low-dispersion
transmission characteristics.
Over the past decade, extensive studies have established the
viability
of
polymer
and
silicon-based
THz
dielectric
waveguides in a variety of forms (see Table I), including
flexible
dielectric
fibers,
rectangular
dielectric
rods,
suspended-core and porous structures, and effective-medium-
cladded or photonic-crystal-guided platforms. These studies
have shown that dielectric waveguides can simultaneously
provide low transmission loss, reduced dispersion, compact
routing capability [8, 9], and compatibility with lightweight
implementations [10], which are highly attractive features for
next-generation interconnects and integrated THz subsystems.
In particular, polymer-based bare dielectric waveguides [11]
are compatible with low-cost fabrication routes such as 3D


## Page 2


printing and extrusion, can be interfaced with electronic THz
front-ends and dielectric-waveguide transitions [8, 12], and
avoid the optoelectronic conversion overhead required by
optical interconnects [13]. Furthermore, Gbps-class data
transmission
and
functional
routing
through
dielectric-
waveguide-based THz links have already been experimentally
demonstrated, confirming their practical relevance as short-
reach,
high-capacity
interconnect
media
[14].
These
developments collectively position THz dielectric waveguides
as an enabling technology for compact, highly integrated
electronic and photonic-electronic systems.
To date, prior studies have established the structural,
material, and system-level foundations of THz dielectric
waveguides, including waveguide geometry optimization,
low-loss material platforms, propagation loss and dispersion
characterization, bend-tolerant routing, and integrated link
demonstrations, as summarized in Table I. At the same time,
evanescent-field-based
investigations
have
mainly
been
explored in sensing and spectroscopy, where environmental
sensitivity is intentionally utilized [15-17]. In contrast, the
influence of realistic PCB-level packaging environments on
dielectric-waveguide
transmission
remains
insufficiently
understood
[18].
In
practical
systems,
bare
dielectric
waveguides are rarely isolated in free space, but are instead
routed in close proximity to PCB substrates, copper traces,
solder-mask layers, and other structural elements. Because the
guided mode of an air-cladded dielectric waveguide inherently
supports an evanescent field extending into the surrounding
region, nearby dielectric and metallic structures can perturb
the modal boundary conditions, redistribute the field [19], and
introduce additional leakage or absorption channels, thereby
causing excess transmission loss. These proximity-induced
effects are particularly important at THz frequencies, where
the
wavelength,
waveguide
dimensions,
and
packaging
tolerances are all comparable, so that even sub-millimeter
variations in clearance can substantially alter the transmission
characteristics [9, 20].
In this article, we try to investigate the transmission
characteristics
of
bare
polypropylene
(PP)
dielectric
waveguides in the vicinity of representative PCB substrates
over the
220-325
GHz
band. We
aim
to
establish
a
quantitative understanding of PCB-induced excess attenuation
and to provide a practical basis for waveguide placement and
PCB co-design in compact THz interconnect systems.
II. COUPLING PERFORMANCE OF DIELECTRIC WAVEGUIDE WITH
PCB
In
investigating
the
near-field-coupling-induced
(NFC-
induced) transmission degradation under practical packaging
conditions, we employ a polymer-based rectangular dielectric
waveguide designed for operation over the 220-325 GHz band,
as illustrated in Fig. 1. Polypropylene (PP) is selected as the
guiding material because it combines low dielectric loss, weak
dispersion, low density, and good processability in the THz
regime [21]. Over the frequency range of interest, PP exhibits
an approximately constant refractive index of n ≈1.51 with
only weak frequency dependence [22]. This makes it suitable
for broadband waveguiding. The waveguides are fabricated by
high-precision fused deposition modeling (FDM) 3D printing
using an Ultimaker 2+ Connect system with an XYZ position
control resolution of 12.5×12.5×5 μm3 and a layer resolution
of 200-20 micron. This fabrication route provides sufficient
geometric fidelity and reproducibility while maintaining low
cost and mechanical flexibility. An air-cladded configuration
is intentionally adopted, since it represents the most relevant
scenario for bare dielectric interconnects routed in close
proximity to surrounding packaging structures rather than
embedded in an idealized homogeneous enclosure.
The cross-sectional dimension of the dielectric waveguide is
a key design parameter because it jointly determines modal
content,
field
confinement,
mechanical
stiffness,
and
sensitivity to the external environment [23]. Therefore, the
waveguide geometry cannot be selected from a single-mode
criterion alone, but must instead be optimized by balancing
electromagnetic and mechanical considerations [24]. If the
cross section is reduced to the sub-millimeter scale, higher-
order modes can be effectively suppressed [25] and quasi-
single-mode propagation can be obtained within the 220-325
GHz band, as in the case of a 0.6×0.6 mm2 guide (see Fig. S1
in
the
Supplemental
Document).
However,
such
small
dimensions also weaken modal confinement, enlarge the
evanescent-field extent, and reduce structural rigidity [25-27],
thereby increasing susceptibility to proximity perturbation,
bending, and alignment errors. In contrast, increasing the cross
section improves confinement and mechanical robustness, but
simultaneously allows more higher-order modes to enter the

Considering these competing requirements, a square PP
dielectric waveguide with a cross section of 1 × 1 mm2 is
adopted in this work. The waveguide is configured in a U-
shaped routed geometry consisting of one 160 mm horizontal
section and two 150 mm vertical sections, connected by two
rounded 90 degree bends with a curvature radius of 20 mm ,
giving a total guided path length of approximately 523 mm.
Under this bending condition, the additional bending loss is
negligible [21]. The fabricated waveguide also maintains a
relatively low surface roughness, helping suppress additional
scattering and transmission perturbation caused by boundary
irregularities [29]. At this size, the fundamental hybrid-mode
pair remains nearly degenerate and clearly separated from the
higher-order modal branches, and its field distribution stays
predominantly single-lobed and well confined within the
waveguide
core
(see
Fig.
S1,
Fig.S2,
Fig.S3
in
the
Supplemental Document). By comparison, the higher-order
modes possess lower effective refractive indices, indicating
weaker confinement and hence greater susceptibility to
radiation and bend-induced attenuation [30, 31]. Under the
present horn-based end-fire excitation and U-shaped routed
geometry, propagation is expected to be governed mainly by
the fundamental hybrid-mode pair, with any residual higher-
order-mode contribution further attenuated during propagation.
Ensure the measured interaction with the nearby PCB is
dominated primarily by the fundamental mode. At the same
time,
the
larger
cross
section
improves
stiffness
and
dimensional
stability,
which
enhances
experimental
repeatability and reduces deformation-induced uncertainty
(see Fig. S4 in the Supplemental Document). Relative to
smaller waveguides, the stronger field confinement also
reduces the spatial extent of the evanescent field and thus
moderates sensitivity to nearby PCB structures (see Fig. S5 in
the
Supplemental
Document).
This
geometry
therefore
provides a practical compromise among modal simplicity,
structural robustness, and environmental tolerance for the
packaging-oriented study conducted here.
For
experimental
measurement,
an
unmodulated
continuous-wave (CW) THz channel is established over the
220-325 GHz band. The transmitter consists of a signal
generator (Ceyear 1465D), a frequency multiplier (Ceyear
82406D), and a standard-gain horn antenna (Ceyear 89901S),
while
the
receiver
employs
an
identical
horn
antenna
connected to a calibrated power sensor (Ceyear 87106B) and a
power meter (Ceyear 71718). As illustrated in Fig. 1(a) and
(b), the transmitter and receiver are placed on the same side of
the experimental platform, and the dielectric waveguide is
routed by two 90 °
bends, so that the received signal is
dominantly guided rather than directly coupled through a free-
space line-of-sight path.. THz radiation is coupled into the
waveguide through horn-based end-fire excitation, which
provides stable excitation of the dominant guided mode. To
minimize parasitic reflections and unintended free-space
scattering, all supporting fixtures are covered with absorbing
materials[32]. A PCB sample is then positioned parallel to one
side of the bare dielectric waveguide, defining an effective
interaction length of 10 cm, while the waveguide-PCB
separation is adjusted by a high-resolution translation stage to
enable quantitative measurement of separation-dependent
attenuation. In addition to the bare and fully copper-clad PCB
cases, four periodic copper-trace PCBs with a trace width of 1
mm and trace spacings from 1 to 4 mm are designed and
fabricated. The trace width is selected to be comparable to the
waveguide size, while the spacing is varied to tune the copper
coverage and conductive continuity of the PCB surface. In this
way, the effects of metallization geometry can be examined
under a controlled millimeter-scale parameter set. By varying
the relative orientation between the waveguide axis and the
trace direction, as well as the lateral alignment between the
waveguide and the trace or gap regions, the effects of
metallization geometry and copper coverage on near-field
coupling can be examined.

To quantify this near-field-coupling-induced (NFC-induced)
transmission degradation, three waveguide-PCB separation
distances, g= 0, 0.5, and 1 mm, are investigated. A 10×10 cm2
thin PCB plate is used in the measurements, which are
performed indoors at 24 °C and 30% relative humidity. Since
the reference and PCB-loaded transmissions are recorded over
the same path under identical ambient conditions, atmospheric
attenuation does not imprint on the extracted excess loss and is
canceled by the subtraction procedure. Furthermore, the PCB-
induced excess loss is extracted by subtracting the reference
transmission measured without the PCB from the transmission
measured in the presence of the PCB, thereby isolating the
attenuation specifically introduced by the nearby substrate.
Two PCB configurations with identical overall dimensions are
first considered: a bare PCB without metallization and a
single-sided PCB with full copper coverage on the waveguide-
facing side. Both samples are composed of an identical FR4
substrate with a thickness of 1.06 mm and solder-mask layers
of 30 μ m; in the metallized case, a 20 μ m copper layer is
inserted between the solder mask and the FR4. From the
waveguide-facing side, the layer sequence is therefore solder
mask-copper
layer
(if
present)
-FR4-solder
mask.
This
comparison allows the effects of dielectric loading and
metallic
shielding
on
waveguide
transmission
to
be
distinguished in a controlled manner.
The
dielectric
properties
of
the
PCB
materials
are
characterized using a T-SPEC 800 terahertz time-domain
spectroscopy (THz-TDS) system operating over 0.1-3 THz.
The system employs a voice-coil-driven high-speed delay line,
enabling a 116 ps time-domain scan within 0.1 s and
corresponding to a spectral resolution of approximately 8 GHz
[34]. Within the 220-325 GHz band of interest, the extracted
real part of the refractive index remains nearly constant, n ≈
2.23-2.27, while the imaginary part gradually decreases from
approximately 0.1 to 0.05 with increasing frequency (see Fig.
S6 in the Supplemental Document). No sharp spectral
resonance is observed in this range, indicating that the PCB
substrate behaves as a broadband lossy dielectric rather than a
resonant
absorber.
In
addition,
repeated
THz-TDS
measurements confirm that the thin solder-mask coating
introduces only a negligible change in the effective dielectric
response,
so
that
the
dominant
substrate
contribution
originates from the FR4 itself. To support the experimental
results, full-wave simulations are using a two-dimensional
finite-element calculation. A frequency-parametric sweep over
220-325 GHz is performed, with input and output boundary-
mode
analyses
followed
by
frequency-domain
field
calculation. Scattering boundary conditions are applied at the
outer boundaries to suppress artificial reflections, and the
maximum mesh size is limited to one-eighth of the local
wavelength to accurately resolve both the guided mode and its
evanescent field. To match the experiment, the waveguide-
PCB interaction is modeled as a 100 mm parallel coupling
section with a uniform separation g, and the excess loss is
extracted relative to the corresponding reference transmission
without the PCB.

IV. INFLUENCE OF DIFFERENT PCB METAL STRUCTURES
In practical PCB implementations, metallization is rarely
realized as an ideal continuous conducting plane. Instead, it
typically appears as discrete copper traces with layout-
dependent width, spacing, and orientation [45], as illustrated
in Fig. 1(b). Under these conditions, the proximity-induced
transmission behavior of the dielectric waveguide is governed
not only by the average copper coverage ratio, but also by the
anisotropic electromagnetic response of the trace array and its
relative alignment with the guided-mode evanescent field. To
examine these effects, four representative copper-trace PCBs
are fabricated, all with a fixed trace width of 1 mm and inter-
trace gaps of 1, 2, 3, and 4 mm, corresponding to copper
coverage ratios of approximately 49%, 33%, 25%, and 20%,
respectively. For each PCB, three coupling configurations are
measured:
a
perpendicular
configuration,
in
which
the
waveguide propagation direction is perpendicular to the trace
orientation, and two parallel configurations, in which the
waveguide propagates along the trace direction and is
positioned either above a copper strip (on-trace case) or above
the dielectric region between two adjacent strips (between-
traces case). The corresponding measured excess-loss spectra
are shown in Fig. 4.
A. Parallel configuration
When the waveguide propagation direction is parallel to the
copper traces, the excess loss depends strongly on whether the
waveguide is aligned with a metallic strip or with a dielectric
gap. In the on-trace case, the evanescent field overlaps directly
with a continuous strip extending along the propagation
direction. Under the boundary condition of a good conductor,
the tangential electric field is strongly suppressed at the metal
surface, while the tangential magnetic field induces surface
currents on the copper trace [46]. In the present geometry, the
copper strip is therefore more appropriately regarded as a
localized metallic loading boundary that perturbs the guided
mode, rather than as a distinct longitudinal energy-transport
channel. The corresponding excess loss can arise from
conductor-related dissipation associated with induced current,
together with stronger modal perturbation and enhanced
coupling to substrate-loaded leakage or reradiation channels in
the nearby PCB environment [46, 47]. This mechanism
explains why the on-trace condition produces relatively large
excess loss when the trace arrays is dense, especially in Fig.
4(a), where the 1 mm gap / 49% coverage case produces
strong power loss under the parallel alignment conditions.
In the between-traces case, by contrast, the waveguide is
mainly exposed to the dielectric gap and the lossy FR4
substrate beneath it. The dominant interaction is therefore no
longer metal-assisted current extraction, but substrate-assisted
leakage through the dielectric window. As the inter-trace
spacing increases, the effective dielectric opening seen by the
evanescent
field
becomes
larger,
which
enhances
field
penetration into the substrate and strengthens leakage into the
lossy PCB body. Consequently, the between-traces condition
remains high-loss, and can even become the dominant loss
channel when the metallization is sparse. This behavior is
clearly observed in Fig. 4(c) and (d), where the blue curves
exceed the on-trace response over a broad frequency range.
These two opposite tendencies explain the overall trend
under parallel alignment. As the trace spacing increases from
1 to 4 mm, the on-trace loss generally decreases because the
overall metallic loading imposed by the trace array is
weakened, whereas the between-traces loss tends to remain

B. Perpendicular configuration
A fundamentally different behavior is observed when the
waveguide propagation direction is perpendicular to the trace
orientation. In this configuration, the guided mode crosses an
alternating sequence of metallic strips and dielectric gaps along
the propagation path. The waveguide is therefore subject to a
periodically modulated metal–dielectric boundary, rather than a
continuous metallic loading path as in the parallel on-trace case.
Periodic longitudinal perturbations in dielectric waveguides are
known to generate coupling among guided and radiating waves
and hence radiation loss [48]. Combined with the evanescent-
field sensitivity of THz dielectric waveguides to nearby media
[16, 25], the excess loss in the present configuration is expected
to depend primarily on how strongly the field is exposed to the
FR4 substrate through the dielectric openings. When the trace
spacing is small, the metal strips occupy a larger fraction of the
region beneath the waveguide, thereby reducing the direct
substrate exposure seen by the evanescent field and weakening
substrate-assisted leakage. At this point, the mode loss caused by
the metal dominates. This explains why the perpendicular
configuration exhibits the smallest excess loss is the dense-trace
case shown in Fig. 4(a).
As the inter-trace gap increases and the copper coverage
decreases, however, this shielding effect is progressively
weakened. The evanescent field can penetrates more efficiently
through the dielectric gaps into the substrate, restoring substrate-
dominated leakage and increasing the measured attenuation. This
trend is directly reflected in Fig. 4(b)-(d), where the red curves
rise substantially as the trace spacing increases from 2 to 4 mm,
particularly in the 235-260 GHz region where the overall
waveguide-PCB coupling is strongest. It is worth noting that the
perpendicular case may contain grating-related periodic-loading
effects, but the observed broad excess-loss features cannot be
identified as a classical Wood’s anomaly based on the present
evidence[49, 50]. Therefore, in the perpendicular configuration,
the copper-traces arry can either protect the waveguide from the
lossy substrate or expose it to enhanced substrate loading,
depending on whether the trace array is dense enough to function
as an effective shielding boundary.
