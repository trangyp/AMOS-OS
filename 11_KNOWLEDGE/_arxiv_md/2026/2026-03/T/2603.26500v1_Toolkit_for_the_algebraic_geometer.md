Sourayan Banerjee, Oliver Lorscheid, Alejandro Martínez Méndez, and Alejandro Vargas
Abstract. In this text, we outline a theory of schemes associated with a site, which
generalizes a variety of geometries, such as manifolds, schemes, analytic spaces,
simplicial complexes, and more. We present an abstract process of gluing model spaces
via sheaf theory and recover a posteriori the underlying topological spaces that are
often present in the construction of such geometric objects. We apply this formalism to
semiring schemes and reason why the usual definition of semiring schemes has to be
considered as the good approach to the geometry of semirings.

Introduction
In this text, we exhibit the mechanism behind the construction of geometric objects
from a class of model spaces, such as manifolds, which are formed from open subsets in
Euclidean space, and schemes, which are formed from spectra of rings.
A necessary input for gluing model spaces along open subspaces is not only a notion
of open subobjects, but also a notion of coverings by these open subobjects. This
information is accurately captured by Grothendieck pretopologies. It turns out that a
Grothendieck pretopology is sufficient for the construction of geometric objects that
look locally like our model spaces.
Three perspectives on geometry. There are three prominent approaches to construct
geometric objects from model spaces, which we review in the examples of smooth
manifolds and schemes in the following; cf. Table 1 for an overview.

Definition in terms of a structure sheaf. A scheme is typically defined as a topological

neighbourhood 𝑈that is isomorphic to the spectrum of O𝑋(𝑈).
Definition as a functor of points. A well-known fact in algebraic geometry is that the
category of schemes can be embedded into the functor category from rings to sets by
identifying a scheme 𝑋with the functor of points Hom(Spec(−), 𝑋). It is possible
to characterize schemes from the perspective of functors of points; cf. [GW10, 4.1]
and [TV09].
One can pass forth and back between these definitions: a scheme can be considered as
a topological space together with an equivalence class of atlases, i.e., coverings by affine
schemes. A smooth manifold can be considered equivalently as a topological space
together with a sheaf of smooth functions, and this viewpoint is typically used for the
generalization of smooth manifolds to analytic spaces. Furthermore, a smooth manifold
𝑋can be identified with the functor of points C∞(−, 𝑋) on the category of open subsets
of Euclidean space R𝑛and smooth maps. See [EH06, Ch. 1] for an exposition of these
different viewpoints on schemes.
Top down approach to 𝔰-schemes. In the generality of a (possibly abstract) category
Aff of model spaces together with a Grothendieck pretopology T, we do not know a
priori what the underlying topological space of objects in Aff might be. The reader
might want to think of the category Aff = SRingsop of affine semiring schemes: it is not
clear from the outset what is the good definition of the prime spectrum of a semiring as
a topological space (cf. [GKMX26], which provides two different constructions that are
based on prime ideals and prime 𝑘-ideals, respectively).

This forces us to take the third route through functors of points, which are sheaves
on the site 𝔰= (Aff, T). Intuitively speaking, T provides us with a notion of open
subfunctors. We define an 𝔰-scheme as the colimit sheaf of a “monodromy free” system
of such open subfunctors.
This provides a rigorous definition of the category Sch𝔰of 𝔰-schemes and recovers
many categories of geometric objects, such as manifolds, schemes, analytic spaces,
simplicial complexes, and more.
Points for 𝔰-schemes. A posteriori, we can assign topological spaces to the objects 𝑋in
Aff as follows. The Grothendieck pretopology T provides a notion of open subschemes
of 𝑋. This collection of open subschemes forms a locale, and a result in [LM26] shows
that this locale coincides with the collection of open subsets of an associated topological
space 𝑋via Stone duality.
If Aff is anti-equivalent to a category Alg of algebraic objects, e.g. (semi)rings, then
𝑋comes equipped with a sheaf in Alg. This shows that the underlying topological space
of an object 𝑋is intrinsically determined by the Grothendieck pretopology T.
In many interesting examples, such as manifold or schemes, the underlying topological
space 𝑋turns out to coincide with the given topological space, i.e., the open subset of R𝑛
in the case of manifolds and the prime spectrum of a ring in the case of an affine scheme
Spec 𝑅. In the case of semiring schemes, 𝑋coincides with the space of all prime ideals;
see [Lor26] for a proof.
Relation to other approaches. This text is based on the forthcoming paper [LM26] and
serves as a reader friendly outlook to this theory. The ideas of this text are not entirely
new, but bear strong similarities with [TV08, section 2.2] and [Lur09]. The main novelty
of our approach is that it is stripped from all unnecessary technical conditions that are
present in the other approaches. Moreover, we continue the study of our theory beyond
the point that this is done in [TV08] and [Lur09].
Content outline. This text is organized as follows. In Section 1, we provide a summary
of the necessary concepts from sheaf theory, such as Grothendieck pretopologies, sheaves
on a site and the Yoneda embedding.
In Section 2, we introduce 𝔰-presentations and 𝔰-schemes. We provide some funda-

indicated in this introduction.
In Section 3, we review Stone duality and apply it to the locale of open subschemes,
which equips an affine 𝔰-scheme with an underlying topological space 𝑋. We show that

In Section 4, we discuss further examples of 𝔰-scchemes, such as various types of

1. Recap on sheaf theory
Let Aff be a category, which we intend to interpret as a category of affine schemes.
The reader might want to think of one of our running examples: the category AffC∞of
“smooth” open subsets of Euclidean space (equipped with smooth maps) or the opposite
category AffZ of Rings.
In order to pass from affine schemes to more general schemes, we require the notions
of covering families and sheaves. In this section, we review the necessary concepts.
1.1. Sites. A Grothendieck pretopology T on Aff is an assignment to each object 𝑋∈Aff
of a collection of families {𝜄𝑖: 𝑈𝑖→𝑋} of morphisms in Aff, that satisfies the following
properties:
(1) Isomorphisms: {𝜑: 𝑌→𝑋} is in T for every isomorphism 𝜑: 𝑌→𝑋.
(2) Local character: if {𝜄𝑖: 𝑈𝑖→𝑋}𝑖∈𝐼and {𝜄𝑖𝑗: 𝑈𝑖𝑗→𝑈𝑗} 𝑗∈𝐽𝑖(one family for
each 𝑖∈𝐼) are in T, then also {𝜄𝑖◦𝜄𝑖𝑗: 𝑈𝑖𝑗→𝑋}𝑖∈𝐼,𝑗∈𝐽𝑖is in T.
(3) Base change: if {𝜄𝑖: 𝑈𝑖→𝑋}𝑖∈𝐼is in T and 𝑓: 𝑌→𝑋is a morphism in
Aff, then Aff contains the fibre product 𝑈𝑖×𝑋𝑌for all 𝑖∈𝐼and the family of
canonical projections {𝑈𝑖×𝑋𝑌→𝑌} is in T.
We call the families in T the covering families of T. A site is a pair 𝔰:= (Aff, T) of a
category Aff together with a Grothendieck pretopology T for Aff.
In the following examples, we call a family {𝜄𝑖: 𝑈𝑖→𝑋} of maps jointly surjective if
every point of 𝑋is contained in the image of some 𝜄𝑖. This is a condition that holds for
the covering families of many prominent Grothendieck pretopologies.
Example 1.1. Grothendieck pretopologies generalize usual topologies in the following
way: Let 𝑋be a topological space, B a basis for the topology of 𝑋that is closed under
finite intersections and Open(𝑋) the category of all open subsets of 𝑋with the inclusion
maps as morphisms. Then the collection of all jointly surjective families {𝜄𝑖: 𝑈𝑖→𝑉}
with 𝑈𝑖,𝑉∈B is a Grothendieck pretopology for Open(𝑋).
Example 1.2. The Euclidean topology for the category AffC∞of “smooth” open subsets
in Euclidean space is the Grothendieck pretopology that consists of jointly surjective
families {𝜄𝑖: 𝑈𝑖→𝑋} of smooth open topological embeddings.

A sheaf on 𝔰= (Aff, T) is a presheaf F on Aff that satisfies the sheaf axiom for T:
the map ΨS is a bijection for every covering family S in T. A morphism of sheaves on 𝔰
is a morphism of presheaves, which defines the category Sh𝔰of sheaves on 𝔰as a full
subcategory of PrShAff.

presheaf Fto its sheafification F#.
1.3. The Yoneda embedding. Let 𝔰= (Aff, T) be a site. A presheaf F is representable if
it is in the essential image of the embedding ˜h : Aff →PrShAff, given by ˜h𝑌= Hom(−,𝑌).
The Yoneda functor h : Aff →Sh𝔰is the composition of ˜h with the sheafification functor
(−)# : PrShAff →Sh𝔰.
We call a family S = {𝜑: 𝑈𝑖→𝑋} subcanonical if Hom(−,𝑌) satisfies the sheaf
axiom with respect to S for every 𝑌in Aff, i.e., ΨS is a bijection for every representable
presheaf F = Hom(−,𝑌). A Grothendieck pretopology T is subcanonical if all of its
covering families are subcanonical. The canonical topology Tcan on Aff consists of all
subcanonical families S and is the finest subcanonical Grothendieck pretopology on Aff.
In other words, the following are equivalent:
(1) T is subcanonical;
(2) T is contained in Tcan;
(3) Hom(−,𝑌) is a sheaf on 𝔰for all 𝑌in Aff;
(4) the Yoneda functor h : Aff →Sh𝔰is fully faithful.
If T is subcanonical, we can thus consider objects 𝑌of Aff as sheaves h𝑌on 𝔰= (Aff, T).
Throughout the rest of the text, we assume that T is subcanonical.

2. 𝔰-Schemes
Let 𝔰= (Aff, T) be a site. An 𝔰-scheme is a certain type of colimit of representable
sheaves ℎ𝑌= Hom(−,𝑌) on Aff. For simplicity, we assume that Aff is complete and
cocomplete.
2.1. Principal opens. A principal open is a morphism in Aff that appears in a covering
family of T. We denote the class of all principal opens by P = PT. The axioms of a
Grothendieck pretopology imply the following properties for P:
(P1) all isomorphisms are in P;
(P2) P is closed under composition;
(P3) P is stable under base change: if 𝑓: 𝑈→𝑋is in P and 𝑔: 𝑌→𝑋is any
morphism in Aff, then the canonical projection 𝑈×𝑋𝑌→𝑌is in P.
Conversely, given a class of morphisms P, we define ⟨P⟩can as the finest subcanonical
Grothendieck topology for which all principal opens are in P. The relation between P
and T is as follows.
Lemma 2.1. Let P be a class of morphisms and T = ⟨P⟩can. Then PT ⊂P, with equality
if and only if P satisfies properties (P1)–(P3). Conversely, a Grothendieck pretopology
T is contained in T′ = ⟨PT⟩can and PT′ = PT.
We call a Grothendieck pretopology T subcanonically saturated if T = ⟨PT⟩can. Most
interesting Grothendieck pretopologies are subcanonically saturated. For exceptions, see
Example 2.3 and Remark 4.2.
Remark 2.2. The term “principal opens” refers to the fact that in scheme theory and its
generalizations to semirings (cf. Section 5) and other algebraic structures, it is easier to
work with principal open immersions than with arbitrary open immersions.
This is already visible in the theory of usual schemes: in contrast to principal open
immersions, which correspond to localizations of rings at a single element, a purely
algebraic characterization of open immersions of affine schemes in terms of their
coordinate algebras is a difficult challenge; also cf. Example 1.4.
It is a more profound problem for categories Aff for which it is not clear a priori what
the underlying topological space of an object would be; see Section 3 for more details.
Example 2.3. Let Aff = Ringsop be the category of (usual) affine schemes and T the
Grothendieck pretopology that consists of all covering families {𝑈𝑖→𝑋} of principal
open immersions for which 𝑈𝑖→𝑋is an isomorphism for some 𝑖. Then PT is the class
of all principal open immersions and ⟨PT⟩can consists of all Zariski coverings {𝑈𝑖→𝑋}
by principal open immersions, which do not need to contain an isomorphism in general.
Thus the inclusion T ⊂⟨PT⟩can is not an equality, i.e., T is not subcanonically saturated.

smooth open topological embeddings. In this case, P = PT is the class of smooth open
topological embeddings, and T is subcanonically generated, as can be seen as follows.

The Euclidean topology T is subcanonical: the functor Hom(−,𝑌) satisfies the
sheaf axiom with respect to covering families {𝑈𝑖→𝑋} in T because a smooth map
𝜑: 𝑋→𝑌corresponds a family of smooth maps 𝜑𝑖: 𝑈𝑖→𝑌that coincide pairwise on

On the other hand, let S = {𝜄𝑖: 𝑈𝑖→𝑋} be a subcanonical family of smooth open
topological embeddings. We claim that S is in T, i.e., S is jointly surjective. Indded,
if this were not the case, i.e., if there was a point 𝑥∈𝑋−Ð im𝜄𝑖, then also the base
change {𝑈𝑖×𝑋{𝑥} →{𝑥}} would be subcanonical (using the base change property of

on the (empty) intersections, but there is in general more than one smooth map {𝑥} →𝑌.
This is the desired contradiction and shows that S is in T.
2.1.2. Principal opens for the Zariski topology. Let Aff = AffZ be the category of affine
schemes, equipped with the Zariski topology T from Example 1.3, which consists of
jointly surjective families {𝑈𝑖→𝑋} of open immersions. In this case, P = PT is the
class of open immersions, and T is subcanonically generated, which can be justified in a
similar way as for the Euclidean topology; see Section 2.1.1. In particular, the proof that
the Euclidean topology is subcanonical carries over verbatim to the Zariski topology.
The proof that every subcanonical family S = {𝑈𝑖→𝑋} of open immersions is in T
requires a slight adaptation. Assume that this was not the case, i.e., there was a point
𝑥∈𝑋−Ð im𝜄𝑖. Then let 𝑍= Spec 𝑘(𝑥) be the spectrum of the residue field of 𝑥and
𝑍→𝑋the canonical inclusion, which has image 𝑥. Pulling back the family S to 𝑍

all 𝑖. This leads to the desired contradiction of the sheaf axiom for a suitably chosen
affine scheme 𝑌(e.g. for 𝑌= 𝑍⊔𝑍).
2.2. 𝔰-Presentations. Let 𝔰= (Aff, T) be a site, P the class of its principal opens and
Sh𝔰the category of sheaves on 𝔰. An 𝔰-presentation is a certain type of diagram U in
Aff that characterizes an 𝔰-scheme 𝑋as its colimit in Sh𝔰. We introduce all required
notions in the following.
A diagram in Aff is a functor U : I →Aff from an “index” category I to Aff. We
typically write 𝑈𝑖= U(𝑖) for the image of objects 𝑖of I. We say that U is a diagram of
principal opens if all morphisms U(𝑖→𝑗) are in P. A diagram U is monodromy free if
the canonical inclusion 𝑈𝑖→colim Sh𝔰U is a monomorphism of sheaves on 𝔰for every
𝑖in I.
Definition 2.4. An 𝔰-presentation is a monodromy free diagram U of principal opens in
Aff.

Definition 2.7. An affine 𝔰-scheme is a representable sheaf on 𝔰, i.e., it is isomorphic to
ℎ𝑋= Hom(−, 𝑋) for some 𝑋in Aff. An 𝔰-scheme is a sheaf 𝑋on 𝔰that is isomorphic
to the colimit colim Sh𝔰U of a monodromy free 𝔰-presentation. We define Sch𝔰as the
full subcategory of Sh𝔰that consists of all 𝔰-schemes.
Remark 2.8. This definition of 𝔰-presentation deviates from that in [LM26], where
𝔰-presentations are required to be closed under limits of finite connected subdiagrams
but are in general not required to be monodromy free. This is relevant if one aims to
cover a generalization of algebraic spaces and similar objects. Since we do not touch

Remark 4.2. The Grothendieck pretopology T from this section is not subcanonically
saturated, i.e., T is properly contained in T′ = ⟨P⟩can, which consists of all jointly
