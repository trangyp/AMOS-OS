ABSTRACT
Cygnus X-3 is a Galactic X-ray binary with a 4.8-hr orbital period operating in the ultraluminous
regime. Although the system is viewed at relatively low inclination (i ≈28◦), it exhibits a deep orbital
modulation. Recent IXPE observations show strong linear polarization orthogonal to the radio jet,
indicating that the X-ray emission is dominated by reflection from the inner walls of a supercritical
outflow funnel.
We propose a “Hybrid” Roche-lobe overflow (RLOF) scenario in which a massive Wolf–Rayet donor
effectively fills its Roche lobe with a focused wind driving a super-Eddington accretion stream. Using
a numerical synthesis of the folded light curve, we show that the modulation is reproduced when the
central funnel is periodically occulted by a vertically extended, shock-heated “Turbulent Wall” formed
by stream impact on the outer disk rim.
This produces a phase lag (∆ϕ ≈0.11) between X-ray
minimum and binary conjunction, with extended attenuation by the WR wind defining a broader
“Suppression Region”.
This geometry explains the enhanced iron-line equivalent width during X-ray minimum via a coro-
nagraphic effect.
The large radial-velocity amplitude of Fe xxvi measured by XRISM (Kobs ≈
430 km s−1) and its zero-crossing at ϕX = 0.0 arise naturally in the stream-impact region rather
than from orbital motion of the compact object.
Finally, we show that the observed secular orbital expansion ( ˙P > 0) follows directly from highly non-
conservative mass transfer with inner-disk mass loss, indicating that Cygnus X-3 is a stable, long-lived
system in a supercritical accretion regime.
Keywords: X-rays: binaries — stars: Wolf–Rayet — accretion, accretion disks — polarization
1. INTRODUCTION
Since its discovery in 1966 (Giacconi et al. 1967),
Cygnus X-3 has remained one of the most enigmatic
objects in the Galaxy.
It is the only known Galactic
High-Mass X-ray Binary (HMXB) comprising a Wolf–
Rayet (WR) star and a compact object, serving as the
definitive local analog for Ultraluminous X-ray Sources
(ULXs).
Defined by its smooth, quasi-sinusoidal 4.8-
hour orbital modulation (Parsignault et al. 1972), the
system alternates between radio-quiet and massive flar-
ing states (Waltman et al. 1994). Spectroscopically, it is
dominated by the “Iron Forest,” the strongest Fe K emis-

sion line complex of any X-ray binary (Serlemitsos et
al. 1975). While early observations could only resolve a
single broad feature, modern instruments (ASCA, Chan-
dra, XRISM ) have revealed this complex structure in de-
tail (Kitamoto et al. 1994; Kallman et al. 2019; XRISM
Collaboration 2024).
The current consensus for the geometry and accretion
dynamics of Cygnus X-3 is that a compact object (black
hole or neutron star) orbits inside the dense stellar wind
of its WR companion (van Kerkwijk et al. 1996) and
is powered by classical Bondi–Hoyle–Lyttleton (BHL)
wind accretion (Hoyle & Lyttleton 1939; Bondi & Hoyle
1944; Pringle 1974; Szostek & Zdziarski 2008; Antokhin
et al. 2022). The 4.8-hour modulation is caused by vary-
ing path lengths through the wind to the X-ray source
(Pringle 1974; Szostek & Zdziarski 2008). Detailed mod-

in the hard state, oriented orthogonal to the radio jet
(Veledina et al. 2024a). This signal implies the emis-

super-critical outflow funnel viewed at low inclination

fluorescence (Mikušincová et al. 2025). Veledina et al.

from view. This ULX interpretation means the Cygnus
X-3 is a local example of “slim disks” or “puffed-up” ac-
cretion flows (Abramowicz et al. 1988), where radiation
pressure inflates the disk into a geometrically thick torus
(H/R ∼1) with a central funnel (Ohsuga & Mineshige
2011; Jiang et al. 2014). As we will show here, this new
discovery presents further challenges to the wind accre-
tion model and instead favors an alternate “Occultation
Model” proposed by White & Holt (1982), which ar-
gued that the orbital modulation is geometric, primarily
caused by a thickened bulge at the edge of the accretion
disk.
2. ACCRETION GEOMETRY
The fundamental conflict facing current models is the
requirement for a low inclination angle. Under the his-
toric assumption of i ≈60◦, the binary mass function
implied a low-mass companion (MW R ≲5M⊙) unable
to fill its Roche-lobe. The IXPE results fundamentally
alter this calculus by showing this is a low inclination
system. The inclination, i, was first estimated at ≈25◦
(Veledina et al. 2024a) and subsequently refined to the
range 26◦–28◦by Veledina et al. (2024b). Historically,
the mass function derived from infrared lines of the WR
wind was small (f(M) ≈0.027M⊙; Hanson et al. 2000).
At high inclinations, this allowed for a low-mass donor.
However, combining the low IXPE inclination with the
standard assumption that the compact object is a black
hole (MX ≳5–10M⊙) forces a radical re-evaluation. To
satisfy the mass function at i ≈27◦, the companion
must be a massive Wolf–Rayet star (MW R ≳15–20M⊙).
This high-mass scenario is independently suggested by
recent high-resolution spectroscopy from XRISM, which
detects a large velocity amplitude of KX ∼430 km s−1
(Miura et al. 2025). While we argue in Section 4 that
this feature traces the accretion stream dynamics rather
than pure orbital motion, its extreme magnitude is phys-
ically difficult to reconcile with the low-velocity flows
expected in a low-mass, wind-fed system.
Unlike the
low-mass scenario, such a massive WN7-type progenitor
has an extended photospheric radius fully capable of fill-
ing its Roche-lobe (e.g., Hamann et al. 2006; Gräfener
et al. 2012).
This geometry resurrects the model of White & Holt
(1982), who argued that the modulation is caused by
a thickened bulge or wall at the edge of the accretion
disk which occults the Accretion Disk Corona (ADC).
This obscuration geometry identifies Cygnus X-3 as a
super-critical analog to the ADC and dipping Low-Mass
X-ray Binaries (LMXBs) (White & Holt 1982; White et
al. 1995; Díaz Trigo et al. 2006). While White & Holt
(1982) envisioned a hydrostatic ADC typical of LMXBs,
the super-Eddington nature of Cygnus X-3 implies the
scattering surface is the inner skin of a radiation-driven
funnel (Poutanen et al. 2007; Veledina et al. 2024a).
However, the geometric principle—occultation of an ex-
tended scattering source by a disk bulge—remains iden-
tical.
We refer to this as a “Hybrid” Roche-lobe overflow
(RLOF) scenario, as it combines the intense stellar wind
characteristic of a WR star with the focused accretion
stream of RLOF.

4. KINEMATICS AND THE BINARY MASS
X-ray spectroscopy provides constraints on the loca-
tion of the absorbing and emitting plasma, which in turn
provides a critical test of the “Hybrid RLOF" geometry.
High-resolution mapping confirms that the majority of
the lower-energy X-ray lines (e.g., from Si, S, Ar, and
Ca) originate deep within the dense Wolf–Rayet wind, in
close proximity to the donor star (Kallman et al. 2019;
Vilhu & Koljonen 2025). In contrast, the highly ionized
Iron K-shell emission (Fe xxv and Fe xxvi) exhibits
distinct orbital variability associated with the compact
object (Miura et al. 2025), proving it arises from a phys-
ically separate, extreme-ionization region.
The observed width of the Fe xxvi emission line
(FWHM ≈800 km s−1; Miura et al. 2025) is significantly

The fact that the line velocity nulls at the

The velocity width directly measures the violent turbu-

emission at ϕX = 0.0, when the inner wall is hidden,
requires that the line-emitting atmosphere rises above
the occulting Wall in a wind/corona/photosphere. This
framework naturally resolves the apparent tension be-
tween the extreme densities required for continuum oc-
cultation and the moderate densities inferred from X-

of the emission (the dense Wall) from the extended,
lower-density atmosphere that dominates the observed
Fe xxvi Lyα flux.
As the system moves past ϕX ≈0.125, the Wall ro-
tates to the far side of the orbit, such that line emission
from the Bulge must traverse the dense central regions of
the Wolf–Rayet wind before reaching the observer. This
prolonged “wind eclipse of the Bulge” reproduces the
wide suppression of the Fe xxvi Lyα line, the “Iron Dip”
(Kitamoto et al. 1994), and the high-velocity absorption
observed by XRISM (XRISM Collaboration 2024).
With the emission securely tied to the stream-impact
site, we can estimate the black hole mass. For an in-
clination of i ≈28◦(Veledina et al. 2024a), the radial
velocity amplitude of the Fe xxvi lines in Cygnus X-3

projected orbital semi-amplitude (KBH = Vorb sin i) and
the projected rotational velocity of the Wall (Vwall sin i):

For a canonical low-mass black hole (MX ≈5M⊙)
paired with a 20M⊙donor (q ≈4), the orbital speed is so
high that KBH alone would exceed 500 km s−1, violating
the observed nominal kinematics. In fact, matching the
observation in this scenario would require the unphysi-
cal condition of retrograde disk rotation. In contrast, a
massive black hole of MX ≈15M⊙(q ≈1.3) balances
the budget. This mass yields a projected orbital am-
plitude of KBH ≈240 km s−1. The difference between
this and the observed nominal 430 km s−1 is supplied
by the Wall’s rotational velocity (Vwall sin i ≈190 km
s−1), which corresponds to a physical flow speed in the
disk plane of Vwall ≈400 km s−1. For a 15M⊙black
hole, the local Keplerian velocity at the tidal truncation
radius (Rdisk ≈1.4R⊙) is VK ≈1430 km s−1. The de-
rived Splash Wall velocity (≈400 km s−1) is therefore
highly sub-Keplerian (≈28% of VK). This provides de-
cisive kinematic evidence that the line-emitting material
is not in a stable free-fall orbit. Instead, it represents
the decelerated, post-shock stream at the circularization
radius of a wind-fed disk, where specific angular momen-
tum is naturally low.
Crucially, the inferred mass of the Wolf–Rayet star
is consistent with historical interpretations of the in-
frared radial velocity curves. Hanson et al. (2000) noted
that the radial velocity zero-crossing of the He i lines
lags the X-ray minimum, and that the velocity ampli-
tude measured from these infrared features is systemat-
ically underestimated. We caution that the mass func-
tion derived by Hanson et al. (2000) is likely unreliable.
Their analysis relied on the He i 2.058 µm line during an
outburst, where complex P-Cygni variability and wind
ionization changes can severely distort the apparent ra-
dial velocity of the absorption component. As discussed
by Zdziarski et al. (2013) and further supported by de-
tailed spectral modeling of the wind structure (Koljonen
& Maccarone 2017), irradiation and wind-shielding ef-
fects shift the effective line-formation region toward the
L1 point, significantly reducing the observed orbital ve-
locity swing. Accounting for this infrared “RV offset,”
together with the Splash Wall geometry identified here,
yields system parameters that are fully consistent with a
∼20 M⊙Wolf–Rayet donor and a ∼15 M⊙black hole.

The “Geometric Crisis” effectively offers a choice be-
tween a wind-formed Accretion Wake or an RLOF-
formed Stream Bulge. The “Hybrid” RLOF plus disk-
bulge geometry reproduces the depth, asymmetry, and
non-zero flux floor of the orbital X-ray modulation with-
out invoking the extreme wind opacities, accretion wake
and shocks required by the wind accretion model. The
key insight is that in this “Hybrid” RLOF model, the ob-
served modulation is produced by two physically distinct
processes operating at different orbital phases (Figure
1). A vertically extended, azimuthally localized “Tur-
bulent Wall” at the outer disk rim provides the geo-
metric shutter that controls the depth of the minimum
(ϕX ≈0.0). Meanwhile, phase-dependent attenuation
in the dense Wolf–Rayet wind governs the slow recov-
ery, producing a “suppression zone” where the bulge has
rotated out of the line of sight, but the wind takes over
as a scattering medium with moderate optical depth
(τ ∼0.4).
In this scenario, the opaque shutter (the “Turbulent
Wall”) selectively occults the unpolarized central emis-
sion, increasing the fractional contribution of the polar-
ized reflection from the surrounding funnel walls. The
IXPE detection of a polarization dip at the 6.4 keV Iron
line provides the definitive signature of this reflection ge-
ometry (Veledina et al. 2024a).
This model also naturally explains the behavior dur-

5.2. Physical Origin and Vertical Extent of the
Turbulent Wall
The “Turbulent Wall” (H/R)peak ∼2 may seem ex-
treme compared to standard, radiation-supported ‘slim
disk’ models. Indeed, puffing up the outer disk via inter-
nal radiation pressure alone would require implausibly
large accretion rates ( ˙M ≈105 ˙MEdd) (Liu et al. 2025).
However, the occulting wall geometry is not a product
of internal radiation pressure. Rather, it is a dynamic
feature formed by the direct kinetic impact of the RLOF
stream colliding with the outer rim of the accretion disk
(Armitage & Livio 1998). This stream-disk impact cre-
ates a localized, shock-heated bulge—a well-documented
phenomenon responsible for the orbital X-ray dips ob-
served in LMXBs such as EXO 0748-676 (Parmar et al.
1986).
In standard LMXB “Dippers” e.g., Díaz Trigo et al.
(2006), the orbital modulation arises when the line of
sight (i ∼60◦−80◦) intersects the raised rim of the
accretion disk. In Cygnus X-3, the interaction is more
extreme: the stream impact is so violent that the re-
sulting bulge extends to high latitudes.
We refer to
this as a “Polar Dipper”, where we observe dipping be-
havior—periodic occultation of the central engine—even
when viewing the system at i ≈28◦.
The high density required for the Wall (ne
∼
1015 cm−3) is physically consistent with these mass flow
rates. Our model fit indicates a “Turbulent Wall” height
(disk plus bulge) of H ∼2Rdisk and an azimuthal shock
width of ≈0.14 in phase. Assuming the stream pene-
trates with a radial thickness of ∆R ∼0.1Rdisk (a con-
servative upper limit based on the typical supersonic
stream width of ≲0.05Rdisk), implies an impact area
A ≈H × ∆R ∼2 × 1021 cm2 (for Rdisk ≈1011 cm). For

The observed increase in iron line equivalent width
during X-ray minimum (Serlemitsos et al. 1975; XRISM

agraph, occulting the compact continuum core (Rcore ∼
108 cm) at ϕX = 0.0 while leaving the large-scale, line-

“eclipse behavior” observed in classical HMXBs (e.g.,
Cen X-3, Vela X-1), where the equivalent width of the
fluorescent iron line rises sharply as the neutron star
passes behind the donor, suppressing the direct contin-
uum while the extended wind nebula remains illumi-
nated (Aftab et al. 2019).
This geometric separation is supported by the phase-
resolved behavior of the Fe xxvi Lyα line observed with
XRISM (XRISM Collaboration 2024). The data reveal
a broad suppression of the line flux from ϕX ≈0.125
to 0.50.
In our “Hybrid” model, this interval corre-
sponds to orbital phases where the “Turbulent Wall”
(ϕphys ≈0.84) lies on the far side of the binary rela-
tive to the observer, such that the line of sight to the
emitting Bulge must traverse the densest regions of the
Wolf–Rayet wind (centered at ϕphys ≈0.0). Conversely,
the line flux reaches its observed maximum at the oppo-
site phase (ϕX ≈0.65–0.85), when the Bulge rotates to
the near side of the system, providing a direct and unob-
scured view of the emission region. In this framework,
the long-recognized “iron dip” (Kitamoto et al. 1994) is
physically identified as a prolonged wind eclipse of the
accretion-stream bulge.
The analogy with other RLOF systems can be ex-
tended to CVs, where a similar disk/stream interaction
occurs. The “Hot Spot” in a CV produces emission lines
with velocity vectors deviating from the white dwarf’s
motion (the classic “S-wave”; Warner 1995, Marsh1988),
the “Turbulent Wall” in Cygnus X-3 introduces a non-
Keplerian velocity component to the Iron line profiles.
The XRISM detection of a large velocity amplitude
(Kobs ≈430 km s−1) is therefore not a direct measure-
ment of the black hole’s orbital speed, but a tracer of
the turbulent, high-velocity impact region.
The detailed microstructure of the “Turbulent Wall”
warrants further investigation. The interaction between
the supersonic accretion stream and the super-critical
disk rim involves complex non-linear fluid dynamics, in-
cluding radiative shocks and Kelvin-Helmholtz instabil-
ities, which are beyond the scope of this work. We iden-
tify full 3D radiation-hydrodynamic simulations as the
critical next step to confirm the vertical stability of the
impact bulge and the dynamics of the matter ejected
from the system.

5.3. Does the Wolf–Rayet Donor Fill its Roche Lobe?
Whether the Wolf–Rayet (WR) donor in Cygnus X-
3 fills its Roche-lobe depends critically on its spectral
classification and the resulting definition of its stellar
radius. Historically, the low mass function derived from
infrared wind lines (f(M) ≈0.027M⊙; Hanson et al.
2000) implied a low-mass companion. However, the low
inclination constrained by IXPE (i ≈28◦) implies a
much higher de-projected orbital velocity for the WR
star. Assuming the compact object is a black hole, this
geometry requires the companion to be a massive Wolf–
Rayet star (MW R ≳15–20M⊙).
This mass requirement strongly favors the WN 5–7
spectral classification (van Kerkwijk et al. 1996) over
the lighter WN 4–6 type (Koljonen & Maccarone 2017).
While WN 4 stars are typically low-mass (∼5M⊙),
Galactic WN 7 stars are intrinsically massive, with
typical masses of MW R ≈20–50M⊙(Crowther 2007).
We attribute the observed high-ionization lines (e.g.,
He II and N V), which formally suggest an earlier
WN subtype, to X-ray photoionization of the stellar
wind by the compact object. Phase-resolved IR spec-
troscopy demonstrates that these lines originate in the
X-ray–modified wind rather than in the stellar photo-
sphere, and thus their ionization balance reflects exter-
nal irradiation rather than the intrinsic effective temper-
ature of the donor (van Kerkwijk et al. 1996; Koljonen
& Maccarone 2017).
For a WN 7 donor, the classical hydrostatic core ra-
dius (Rcore ≲1 R⊙; Langer 1989) is not the relevant
scale for mass transfer.
Instead, the effective photo-
sphere forms in the accelerating optically thick wind.
Modern non-LTE atmosphere models place this photo-
spheric radius substantially further out, at Rphot ∼1.5–
3 R⊙(e.g., Hamann et al. 2006; Gräfener et al. 2012).
For an orbital period of 4.8 hr and a total system mass
of ∼20–25 M⊙(assuming MX ∼5M⊙), the binary sepa-
ration is a ≃3–3.5 R⊙. Using Eggleton’s approximation
with a mass ratio q = MWR/MX ≃3–4, the Roche-lobe
radius of the WR donor is RRL ≃1.4–1.7 R⊙. Thus,
while the hydrostatic core underfills the Roche-lobe, the
effective wind photosphere of a WN 7 donor grazes or
overfills it. In this full RLOF regime, we expect the com-
pact object to capture approximately 50% of the mass
loss (the hemisphere facing L1), yielding a transfer rate
of ≈1.5 × 10−5M⊙yr−1.
This conclusion is robust even for the higher black
hole mass (MX ∼15M⊙) suggested by our kinematic
analysis of the velocity budget (Section 4). Increasing
the black hole mass reduces the mass ratio q, which de-
creases the fractional Roche-lobe size (RRL/a), but si-
multaneously increases the binary separation a. These
effects largely offset each other. For MX = 15 M⊙, the
Roche-lobe radius remains comparable to the WN 7 pho-
tospheric radius.
With a mass ratio of q ≈1.3, the
system remains comfortably below the critical threshold
for dynamical instability (qcrit ∼3.5) for donors with
radiative envelopes, ensuring that the Roche-lobe over-
flow proceeds stably on the nuclear timescale rather than
running away (van den Heuvel et al. 2017).

compact object, naturally generating a supercritical disk

the observed properties of Cygnus X-3.
5.5. Multi-Wavelength Consistency
The “Hybrid” geometry predicts the energy depen-
dence of the orbital modulation observed above 30 keV.
At soft X-ray energies (∼1–10 keV), the modulation
arises from the combination of two physically distinct
components: geometric occultation by the “Turbulent
Wall” (sharp ingress) and Thompson scattering and iron
K photoelectric absorption in the dense Wolf–Rayet
wind (broad egress).
As photon energy increases, the Iron K absorption
becomes irrelevant above ∼30 keV. In this hard X-
ray regime, the wind modulation is driven solely by
Thomson scattering. However, at even higher energies,
Klein–Nishina corrections reduce the effective scatter-
ing cross-section. Consequently, the wind’s contribution
to the modulation amplitude diminishes, leaving a pro-
file governed primarily by the energy-independent geo-
metric occultation of the central engine by the “Turbu-
lent Wall”. This explains the transition to a narrower
minimum light curve centered on ϕX ∼0 observed in
hard X-rays by INTEGRAL and Swift/BAT (Zdziarski
et al. 2012), which traces the bulge eclipse rather than
the “Suppression zone”. In contrast, the GeV gamma-
ray modulation observed by the Fermi LAT lags signif-
icantly (ϕX ≈0.3 −0.4; Abdo et al. 2009). As proposed
by Dubus et al. (2010), this likely arises from the eclipse
of anisotropic inverse Compton scattering in a jet in-
clined relative to the orbital plane normal, confirming
that the jet orientation is decoupled from the disk/wall
geometry.
The orbital behavior of the infrared (IR) emission pro-
vides an independent test of this geometry. Historically,
the IR minimum is observed to be largely synchronous
with the X-ray minimum (ϕX ≈0.0) (Mason et al.
1976). In the standard wind model, this synchroniza-
tion is assumed to be coincidental. If the IR modula-
tion were driven solely by opacity variations in the global
Wolf–Rayet wind, the IR minimum should coincide with
the physical conjunction at ϕX ≈0.11. The fact that
the IR minimum remains aligned with the “Turbulent
Wall” (ϕX ≈0.0) strongly suggests that the Wall acts
as a “common shutter” for both bands.
This implies
that the variable IR component is not diffuse, but is co-
spatial with the central accretion structure—consistent
with emission from the shock-heated “turbulent” region.
