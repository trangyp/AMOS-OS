The Carnot Bound: Limits and Possibilities for
Bandwidth-Efficient Consensus (WIP)
ANDREW LEWIS-PYE1,2 and PATRICK O’GRADY2, 1 London School of Economics, UK and 2 Com-
monware, USA
In leader-based protocols for State Machine Replication (SMR), the leader’s outgoing bandwidth is a natural
throughput bottleneck. Erasure coding can alleviate this by allowing the leader to send each processor a single
fragment of each block, rather than a full copy. The data expansion rate, the ratio of total data sent to payload
size, determines how close throughput can get to the underlying network bandwidth.
We investigate the fundamental limits and possibilities for bandwidth-efficient leader-based consensus.
On the negative side, we prove that protocols with 2-round finality (one round of voting) cannot achieve a
data expansion rate below approximately 2.5, a bound that is matched by existing protocols. On the positive
side, we show that protocols with 3-round finality (two rounds of voting) can push the data expansion rate
arbitrarily close to 1. The key insight is that the second voting round provides a recovery mechanism: leaders
can attempt aggressive erasure codes and safely fall back to more conservative ones when reconstruction fails,
without compromising consistency.
We present two protocols with 3-round finality realising this approach. Carnot 1 assumes 𝑛≥4𝑓+ 1
processors (of which at most 𝑓may be Byzantine) and achieves a clean design requiring no additional fragment
dissemination beyond the initial protocol messages. Carnot 2 operates under the optimal resilience assumption
𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1, at the cost of additional fragment dissemination when Byzantine processors interfere. Both
protocols can incorporate stable leaders and optimistic proposals to maximise throughput and minimise
latency. Under favourable conditions, with correct leaders and few actual faults, both protocols allow leaders
to use data expansion rates approaching 1; under adversarial conditions, leaders can revert to safe expansion
rates of approximately 1.33 and 1.5, respectively – both still well below the 2.5 lower bound for protocols with
2-round finality.

Introduction
State Machine Replication (SMR) [28] is a fundamental primitive for distributed computing that
allows a collection of processors to maintain a consistent, shared log of transactions despite the
failure, or even malicious behaviour, of some participants. Originally developed for fault-tolerant
systems, SMR has become the algorithmic backbone of modern blockchains and decentralised
applications. The core challenge is to ensure that all correct processors agree on the same sequence of
transactions (Consistency), while also guaranteeing that new transactions are eventually included
(Liveness), even in the presence of Byzantine faults, where corrupted processors may behave
arbitrarily.
Protocols for SMR typically operate in the partially synchronous model [14], in which messages
may be delayed arbitrarily before an unknown time GST (the Global Stabilisation Time), but are
delivered within a known bound Δ thereafter. This model captures realistic network conditions,
including temporary partitions, and underpins many widely deployed protocols, from PBFT [9] to
the consensus layers of modern blockchains such as Ethereum [7].
The throughput challenge. A central goal in recent protocol design is to maximise throughput:
the rate at which transactions can be finalised. Transactions are typically grouped into blocks,
and protocols proceed by having processors agree on a sequence of blocks. Two broad families
of protocols have emerged. In DAG-based protocols [19, 20, 33, 36], multiple processors propose
blocks concurrently, organising them into a directed acyclic graph from which a linear ordering is
then extracted. In leader-based protocols [9, 11, 30, 38], a single designated leader proposes each
block, and the remaining processors vote to accept or reject it.

DAG-based protocols can achieve high throughput through their concurrency, but at the cost of
higher latency, in the sense that finalisation requires more rounds of communication. Leader-based
protocols offer lower latency, but the leader’s outgoing bandwidth is a natural bottleneck, which
can be alleviated using erasure coding. Rather than sending a full copy of each block to every
processor, the leader encodes the block’s payload and sends each processor a single fragment, from
which the full payload can be reconstructed once sufficiently many fragments are collected. The
efficiency of this encoding is captured by the data expansion rate: if the payload has size 𝛽, then
the total data the leader must send is approximately 𝑑· 𝛽, where 𝑑is the data expansion rate. As
examined by Lewis-Pye, Nayak and Shrestha [25], this factor 𝑑directly governs the throughput
bottleneck. For a network in which each processor has bandwidth 𝐵, i.e., processors can send 𝐵bits
per unit time, payloads of size 𝛽require approximately 𝑑𝛽/𝐵time to disseminate. The maximum
throughput—measured in payload bits finalised per unit time—is therefore approximately 𝐵/𝑑.
To approach throughputs matching the underlying network bandwidth, one must push the data
expansion rate as close to 1 as possible.
Finality and voting rounds. Before stating our results, we establish some basic terminology. We
consider a set of 𝑛processors, of which at most 𝑓may display Byzantine (arbitrary) behaviour. In
leader-based protocols, which are the focus of this paper, the protocol proceeds in sequential views,
each with a designated leader who assembles transactions into a block and proposes it to the other
processors, who then vote on whether to accept it. We use the term 𝑟-round finality to describe a
protocol in which finality requires 𝑟rounds of communication: one round for the leader’s proposal,
followed by 𝑟−1 rounds of voting. Protocols with 2-round finality, consisting of one proposal
round and one voting round, include Minimmit [11], Hydrangea [32], and Kudzu [30], and typically
assume 𝑛≥5𝑓+ 1 (𝑛≥5𝑓−1 is necessary and sufficient [23]). Protocols with 3-round finality,
consisting of one proposal round and two voting rounds, include Simplex [10] and the protocols
we present in this paper, and typically operate under the (optimal) assumption that 𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1.
Our contributions. This paper investigates the fundamental limits and possibilities for bandwidth-
efficient leader-based consensus using erasure coding. We establish a lower bound on the data
expansion rate for protocols with 2-round finality, and show that protocols with 3-round finality
can circumvent this bound, achieving data expansion rates approaching 1.
An impossibility result for 2-round finality (Section 8). We prove that protocols with 2-round finality
cannot achieve a data expansion rate below 2.5. This bound is tight: E-Minimmit [11] and Kudzu [30]
achieve data expansion rates of approximately 2.5.
Circumventing the bound with 3-round finality. The additional voting round in 3-round finality
protocols provides a crucial recovery mechanism. When the leader uses an aggressive erasure code
(with a high reconstruction threshold 𝑘), it is possible that some correct processors reconstruct
the payload, perhaps with the aid of fragments from Byzantine processors, while others cannot.
In a 2-round finality protocol, this possibility leads to unrecoverable configurations: consistency
and liveness cannot both be maintained. With two rounds of voting, however, the second round
can detect whether data availability has been achieved and, if not, allow the protocol to nullify the
view and retry. This means leaders can attempt low data expansion rates and safely fall back to
higher rates when reconstruction fails.
Specifically, a correct leader can use an (𝑛,𝑘)-erasure code for any 𝑘up to 𝑛−1, meaning that
𝑘of 𝑛fragments suffice for reconstruction. This produces a data expansion rate of 𝑛/𝑘. In the
optimistic case, when the leader is correct, the network is synchronous, and at most 𝑓𝑎processors
are actually faulty, the leader can set 𝑘as high as 𝑛−𝑓𝑎−1 (the leader does not need to disseminate
its own fragment), giving a data expansion rate that approaches 1 as 𝑓𝑎becomes small relative to 𝑛.

When conditions deteriorate, the protocol automatically reverts to a safe expansion rate without
interrupting progress. We present two protocols realising this approach: Carnot 1, a simple protocol
assuming 𝑛≥4𝑓+ 1, and Carnot 2, which operates under the weaker (and optimal) assumption
𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1. The safe rate depends on the resilience assumption: approximately 1.5 for 𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1,
or approximately 1.33 for 𝑛≥4𝑓+ 1.
Carnot1 1: a clean design for 𝑛≥4𝑓+ 1 (Sections 4–6). Our first protocol assumes 𝑛≥4𝑓+ 1
and achieves a particularly clean design. A key concern when using aggressive erasure codes is
ensuring data availability: if a correct processor advances to the next view on the basis of a block
whose payload it cannot fully reconstruct, it must be guaranteed that the payload can eventually
be recovered from the messages received by correct processors collectively. In many protocols, this
guarantee requires correct processors to perform additional rounds of fragment dissemination when
the leader is Byzantine, adding communication overhead precisely in the cases where resources
are most constrained. Carnot 1 avoids this entirely: processors echo their certified fragment once
upon receiving it from the leader and voting, but no further echoing is ever required. The protocol
achieves this through a careful choice of quorum sizes that guarantees every view produces either
a compact data-availability certificate or a mechanism for skipping the view, without additional
communication.
Carnot 1 also incorporates stable leaders, where a single leader proposes blocks across multiple
consecutive views (a superview), to eliminate the inter-proposal gap that arises with rotating leaders.
Additionally, we describe how optimistic proposals, inspired by Moonshot [13], can be used to allow
the next leader to begin proposing before the current superview has concluded, further reducing
latency.
Carnot 2: a protocol for 𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1 (Appendices B–D). Our second protocol operates under the
standard optimal resilience assumption 𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1, at the cost of requiring additional fragment
dissemination when Byzantine processors interfere. When a processor reconstructs a payload but
does not receive proof of data availability, it re-encodes the payload using a more conservative
erasure code and disseminates the resulting fragments, ensuring that all correct processors can
eventually reconstruct the payload as well. Under standard operation with correct leaders, no extra
dissemination occurs.
Both protocols build on the Simplex consensus protocol [10], a simple and efficient protocol for
partial synchrony with rotating leaders and two rounds of voting per view. Our protocols extend
Simplex with erasure coding, stable leaders, and the mechanisms described above. To summarise the
trade-off between the two: Carnot 1 is simpler and more communication-efficient under adversarial
conditions, but requires a stronger resilience assumption; Carnot 2 tolerates the optimal number
of faults, but may require additional communication when Byzantine processors interfere. Under
standard operation with correct leaders, the two protocols behave almost identically.

Transactions. Transactions are messages of a distinguished form, signed by the environment. Each

Extractable SMR: informal discussion. Many protocols for State Machine Replication (SMR) [28]
do not explicitly specify the mechanism by which correct processors retrieve the full finalised log of
transactions. Instead, they ensure data availability for the log. In Narwhal [12], for example, blocks
of transactions are sent to all processors, who then send acknowledgement messages confirming
receipt. A set of 𝑓+ 1 such acknowledgements acts as a data availability certificate for the block: it
proves that at least one correct processor has received the block, and can therefore supply it to
others upon request. Consensus can then be run on certificates rather than full blocks. The notion
of Extractable SMR was introduced in [26] to formalise the task being solved by such protocols.
Roughly speaking, the requirement is that, while an individual processor may not be able to
determine the full finalised log (without extra communication), the correct processors collectively
receive sufficient messages to determine it.

Carnot: the intuition
In this section, we informally describe the intuition behind our positive results. In Section 8, we
show that achieving a data expansion rate lower than 2.5 is not possible for protocols with 2-round
finality. In this section, we therefore consider protocols with two rounds of voting (3-round finality).
We start by supposing 𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1. First, in Section 3.1, we review the Simplex protocol [10],
upon which our protocols will be based. Then, in Section 3.2, we briefly consider the throughput
advantages of stable leaders. In Sections 3.3-3.5, we consider increasingly advanced approaches to
reducing data expansion.
3.1
Recalling Simplex
Simplex [10] is a protocol for partial synchrony assuming 𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1, with rotating leaders. It
proceeds in sequential views 𝑣= 1, 2, . . ., each with leader lead(𝑣).
Two rounds of voting. The protocol uses two rounds of voting per view. When a leader enters
view 𝑣, it proposes a block extending a notarised chain. Upon receiving a valid proposal 𝑏, each
processor disseminates (sends to all) a stage-1 vote for 𝑏. A set of 𝑛−𝑓stage-1 votes for 𝑏, each by
a different processor, is called a stage-1 notarisation for 𝑏. Upon receiving a stage-1 notarisation for
𝑏, a processor moves to view 𝑣+ 1. If, upon entering 𝑣+ 1, the processor’s timer for view 𝑣has
not yet fired, it disseminates a stage-2 vote for 𝑏. A set of 𝑛−𝑓stage-2 votes is called a stage-2
notarisation for 𝑏, and 𝑏is finalised when it receives such a notarisation.
Nullifications. If the leader is faulty or slow, processors time out after 3Δ and disseminate a
nullify(𝑣) message. A set of 𝑛−𝑓nullify(𝑣) messages is called a nullification for view 𝑣; processors
also move to view 𝑣+ 1 upon receiving a nullification. The critical design constraint is that each
correct processor either sends a stage-2 vote or a nullify(𝑣) message, but never both. It follows by
the standard quorum intersection argument ((𝑛−𝑓) + (𝑛−𝑓) −𝑛≥𝑓+ 1 when 𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1) that a
stage-2 notarisation and a nullification for the same view cannot coexist. Similarly, two distinct
blocks cannot both receive a stage-1 notarisation in the same view.
Progression through views. Correct processors disseminate stage-1 notarisations and nullifica-
tions upon first receipt. If any correct processor leaves view 𝑣upon receiving a stage-1 notarisation,
then all correct processors receive it and also leave the view. Otherwise, all correct processors
eventually time out and disseminate nullify(𝑣) messages, producing a nullification.
Consistency. No correct processor votes for a view 𝑣block with parent 𝑏′ for view 𝑣′ unless it has
received a stage-1 notarisation for 𝑏′ and nullifications for all views in the open interval (𝑣′, 𝑣). If a
view 𝑣block 𝑏is finalised, then view 𝑣does not receive a nullification, so correct processors cannot
vote for any block inconsistent with 𝑏in a subsequent view.

3.2
The advantage of stable leaders
The cost of rotating leaders. In Simplex, each view has a different leader. Before proposing,
the new leader must receive a stage-1 notarisation or nullification from the previous view, and
this creates a significant gap between consecutive proposals during which no block-data is being
disseminated, limiting throughput and increasing latency. One can partially address this with
optimistic proposals, whereby the leader of view 𝑣+ 1 proposes a block upon receiving the proposal
for view 𝑣, without waiting for a stage-1 notarisation. This reduces the gap, but does not eliminate
it: the new leader must still receive the previous leader’s proposal before it can propose its own
block.
Stable leaders and pipelining. A more direct solution is to give the same leader multiple successive
views. If a correct leader has 𝑥consecutive views, it can pipeline proposals: as soon as it sends the
block for view 𝑣, it can immediately begin sending the block for view 𝑣+ 1, without waiting for any
messages. This eliminates the inter-proposal gap entirely for all but the first view of each leader’s
tenure, allowing throughput to approach the underlying network bandwidth as 𝑥grows. In our
protocol, we formalise this using superviews: each superview is a sequence of 𝑥views with a single
leader, and leaders rotate between superviews.
3.3
Basic erasure coding
We now turn to the question of how each individual block is disseminated. In Simplex, the leader
sends a copy of the full block to every other processor, so that the total data sent is approximately
𝑛times the block size — a data expansion rate of 𝑛. A natural approach to reducing this is to use
erasure coding. In this section, we describe a straightforward approach producing a data expansion
rate of 3, using methods first described in [8]. In Sections 3.4 and 3.5, we consider how to achieve
lower data expansion rates.
Erasure-coded proposals. Rather than sending the entire block to each processor, the leader
separates out the block’s payload 𝐶(its sequence of transactions) and encodes it using an (𝑛,𝑛−2𝑓)-
erasure code, producing 𝑛fragments of size approximately |𝐶|/(𝑛−2𝑓) each, together with a short

3.4
Achieving a data expansion rate of 1.5
The data expansion rate of 3 arose because only 𝑛−2𝑓fragments were guaranteed to reach every
correct processor, forcing the use of an (𝑛,𝑛−2𝑓)-erasure code. To reduce the data expansion
rate, we need to increase the number of fragments required for reconstruction. In this section, we
describe how to achieve a data expansion rate of 𝑛/(𝑛−𝑓), which equals (3𝑓+ 1)/(2𝑓+ 1) when
𝑛= 3𝑓+ 1, approaching 1.5 as 𝑓grows.
Using an (𝑛,𝑛−𝑓)-erasure code. Blocks are formed using erasure coding as before, but now with
an (𝑛,𝑛−𝑓)-erasure code: 𝑛−𝑓fragments are required for reconstruction. A processor 𝑝𝑖proceeds
to view 𝑣+ 1 upon receiving both a stage-1 notarisation for a view 𝑣block 𝑏and fragments of 𝑏
from 𝑛−𝑓distinct processors. Under standard operation, i.e., correct leaders during synchrony,
these 𝑛−𝑓fragments arrive naturally as part of stage-1 voting, and the protocol behaves just as in
Section 3.3.
The problem: ensuring data availability. The difficulty arises when the leader is faulty. In such
cases, a correct processor 𝑝𝑖might collect 𝑛−𝑓fragments and form a stage-1 notarisation, but
some other correct processor 𝑝𝑗might not receive enough fragments to reconstruct the payload.
Since we now require 𝑛−𝑓fragments rather than 𝑛−2𝑓, the guarantee from Section 3.3 — that
the correct voters alone provide enough fragments — no longer suffices. We therefore need an
additional mechanism to ensure that all correct processors eventually receive the fragments they
need.
Fragment echoing. The solution is as follows. Upon proceeding to view 𝑣+ 1, processor 𝑝𝑖
continues with the instructions for the new view but also, in the background (without slowing the
critical path), sets a timer to expire after Δ. When the timer expires, 𝑝𝑖checks which processors

have not yet sent their fragment of 𝑏, and sends each such processor 𝑝𝑗their fragment, which 𝑝𝑖
can reconstruct from the decoded payload, along with the stage-1 notarisation for 𝑏. Additionally,
any processor 𝑝𝑗that receives its own fragment of 𝑏together with a stage-1 notarisation (from any
processor, not just the leader) is required to echo that fragment by disseminating it to all processors,
even if 𝑝𝑗has since moved to a later view or has previously voted for a different fragment. The
stage-1 notarisation requirement here is important: it limits the amount of echoing that Byzantine
processors can provoke, since extra echoing is triggered only for a block that has actually been
notarised.
Two types of extra communication. Under standard operation, no extra fragment echoing
occurs. When it does occur, there are two types: (a) 𝑝𝑖may have to send 𝑝𝑗their fragment (if 𝑝𝑖has
not received that fragment from 𝑝𝑗); (b) 𝑝𝑗may subsequently have to echo their fragment to all
processors upon receiving it together with the stage-1 notarisation.
M-certificates as a data availability optimisation. An issue with the mechanism above is
that even crashed processors trigger extra fragment echoing. To mitigate this, we introduce an
optimisation. If 𝑓+ 1 stage-2 votes for 𝑏are collected, at least one must be from a correct processor,
and a correct processor only sends a stage-2 vote after successfully reconstructing the payload.
The 𝑓+ 1 votes can therefore be combined into a compact threshold certificate — which we call a
stage-2 M-certificate (where ‘M’ stands for ‘mini’) — that serves as a certificate of data availability.
Since any block receiving an M-certificate must also have received a stage-1 notarisation (because
each correct stage-2 voter must first have seen one), an M-certificate on its own suffices for view
progression: a processor that receives an M-certificate for a view 𝑣block can proceed to view 𝑣+ 1
without needing to collect 𝑛−𝑓fragments directly. This eliminates the need for further fragment
echoing upon receipt of an M-certificate.
3.5
Pushing the data expansion rate lower than 1.5
While a data expansion rate of 1.5 may be necessary when 𝑓processors are actually faulty, when
fewer processors are faulty it should be possible for leaders to use lower expansion rates. The idea
is to allow leaders to attempt lower expansion rates and revert to rate 1.5 if reconstruction fails. We
show in Section 8 that this is not possible for protocols with 2-round finality: even a single attempt
at a lower data expansion rate can lead to a situation from which Consistency and Liveness cannot
both be maintained. With 3-round finality (two rounds of voting), however, recovery is possible.
The difficulty. When the leader uses an (𝑛,𝑘)-erasure code with 𝑘> 𝑛−𝑓, the 𝑛−𝑓fragments
echoed by correct voters may not suffice for reconstruction. If no correct processor can reconstruct
the payload, this is unproblematic: all correct processors eventually time out and nullify the view.
The difficulty arises when some correct processors reconstruct the payload (perhaps with the aid
of fragments from Byzantine processors) while others cannot. In this case, unlike in Section 3.4,
it does not suffice to have correct processors echo their fragments: even if all correct processors
receive and echo their fragments, the 𝑛−𝑓fragments from correct processors may not be enough
for reconstruction when 𝑘> 𝑛−𝑓.
Carnot 2: an approach for 𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1. The most natural solution, which we present as Carnot 2
in Appendix C, is as follows. When a processor decodes the payload and moves to the next view,
but does not receive an M-certificate (i.e., proof of data availability), it reverts to encoding the
payload using an (𝑛,𝑛−𝑓)-erasure code and sends out the relevant fragments; upon receiving their
fragment together with the corresponding stage-1 certificate, other processors echo their fragment
to all. In this way, if any correct processor leaves the view upon decoding the block, it can be sure

that Byzantine action can trigger a significant amount of extra fragment echoing.
Carnot 1: avoiding extra echoing for 𝑛≥4𝑓+1. In the main body, we present a cleaner approach
that assumes 𝑛≥4𝑓+ 12 but avoids any extra fragment echoing: processors echo their certified
fragment once upon receiving it from the leader and voting, but no further echoing is ever required.
The key is to use stage-2 notarisations of size 𝑛−𝑓and nullifications of size 2𝑓+ 1. Two properties
follow.
(a) A stage-2 notarisation and a nullification for the same view cannot coexist. This follows
from the standard quorum intersection argument: (𝑛−𝑓) + (2𝑓+ 1) −𝑛= 𝑓+ 1, so any stage-2
notarisation and any nullification for the same view must share at least one correct processor. Since
no correct processor sends both a stage-2 vote and a nullify message for the same view, such a pair
cannot form.
(b) Each view produces an M-notarisation or a nullification (or both). Recall that an M-
notarisation requires only 𝑓+ 1 stage-2 votes. If more than 𝑓correct processors send stage-2 votes
for view 𝑣, then — since all stage-2 votes by correct processors are for the same block — at least
𝑓+ 1 stage-2 votes are collected, producing an M-notarisation. If at most 𝑓correct processors send
stage-2 votes, then at least 𝑛−2𝑓≥2𝑓+ 1 correct processors send nullify(𝑣) messages, producing
a nullification.
Consequences for view progression. Property (a) guarantees Consistency will still be satisfied.
Property (b) means we can require either an M-certificate (the threshold certificate formed from
an M-notarisation) or an N-certificate (the threshold certificate formed from a nullification) for
view progression. Since an M-certificate guarantees that at least one correct processor has decoded
the payload, data availability is ensured without any additional fragment echoing. In Section 4, we
will implement this using the following terminology: each processor maintains a local variable
blocks, which stores all blocks for which it has received an M-certificate. A processor advances to
view 𝑣+ 1 only upon adding a view 𝑣block to its local value blocks, or else upon receiving an
N-certificate for view 𝑣.
Does requiring M-certificates for view progression limit throughput? One might worry that
waiting for an M-certificate rather than a stage-1 notarisation delays view progression and so limits
throughput (for Carnot 2, this is not an issue). For Carnot 1, this is not an issue within a superview,
because the leader pipelines block dissemination regardless of view progression. For transitions
between superviews, the delay can be handled by optimistic proposals (see Section 6), which allow
the next leader to propose immediately upon receiving the previous leader’s block. One might still
worry about latency; we discuss this further in Section 7 (to be added) using the Pipes model. The
summary is that when throughput is sufficiently high, each processor receives an M-certificate for
a view 𝑣block before receiving its full fragment for view 𝑣+ 1, so waiting for the M-certificate
does not delay processing.
Carnot 1 vs Carnot 2. To summarise, Carnot 2 works under the weaker assumption 𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1 but
requires extra fragment echoing when Byzantine processors interfere. Carnot 1 assumes 𝑛≥4𝑓+ 1
but is simpler and requires no extra fragment echoing beyond the initial echo upon voting.

view 𝑣of superview 𝑤before proposing its first block. Since 𝑝𝑖enters view 𝑣only after adding a
view 𝑣−1 block to blocks or receiving an N-certificate for view 𝑣−1, this creates a gap during
which no leader is actively sending proposals. This gap increases transaction latency and reduces
throughput, particularly when the number of views per superview is small.
In this section, we describe how Carnot 1 can integrate optimistic proposals, using an approach
inspired by Moonshot [13], to allow 𝑝𝑖to propose a view 𝑣block before entering view 𝑣, while
the previous superview is still in progress. Under standard operation—during synchrony and
when leaders are correct—the optimistic proposal will be finalised, reducing the latency gap. If the
optimistic proposal cannot be finalised, the protocol falls back to the standard mechanism: 𝑝𝑖makes
a fresh proposal after entering view 𝑣. After GST and when 𝑝𝑖is correct, one of these proposals
will be finalised.
Note that optimistic proposals are relevant only for the first block of each superview (the
initial view block). For non-initial views within a superview, the same leader is already pipelining
proposals, so no gap arises: with a stable leader there is not even the 𝛿time gap that arises during
optimistic proposals due to message delay between successive leaders.
When should 𝑝𝑖propose optimistically? Let 𝑝𝑗= lead(𝑣−1) be the leader of the previous
superview, and suppose 𝑝𝑗proposes a view 𝑣−1 block 𝑏′. There are at least two options for when
𝑝𝑖should make its optimistic proposal:
(a) Propose upon receiving a votable fragment for view 𝑣−1. Upon receiving a certified fragment
of 𝑏′ that is votable for view 𝑣−1, processor 𝑝𝑖immediately proposes a view 𝑣block with
parent 𝑏′.

a further 𝛽bits for the full block to 𝑝𝑖. This increases latency and significantly impacts throughput

We treat Option (a) below and in Appendix A; modifying the approach for Option (b) is straight-
forward.
Coordinating proposals: the problem. To ensure the proof of Consistency is unaffected, we still
require correct processors to send at most one stage-1 vote and at most one stage-2 vote in each
view. In a context where the leader 𝑝𝑖may make more than one proposal for an initial view 𝑣, this
raises a question: how can we ensure that one of the proposals receives enough votes to become
finalised?
If no correct processor is able to vote for the optimistic proposal, then making a second proposal
is unproblematic: no correct processor regards the optimistic proposal as votable for view 𝑣, so
when they receive a second (properly formed) proposal, they will all vote for it. The difficulty arises
when some correct processors are able to vote for the optimistic proposal 𝑏(because its parent 𝑏′ is
in their local value blocks), while others are not.
Coordinating proposals: the solution. To handle this, we implement a locking mechanism for
initial views. When evaluating votability condition (i) from Section 4 for an initial view 𝑣, correct
processors use their local value blocks as defined at the timeslot at which they entered view 𝑣, rather
than their current value. This restriction is lifted for a specific block when the leader 𝑝𝑖sends a
(release,𝑏) message for that block: upon receiving (release,𝑏), processors use their current value
of blocks when evaluating whether they have received a votable fragment of 𝑏.
When a second proposal is not needed. Upon entering view 𝑣at timeslot 𝑡, the leader 𝑝𝑖waits 2Δ
before deciding whether it needs to make a second proposal (note that, during synchrony and
when leaders are correct, this wait does not impact the ‘critical path’). Suppose view 𝑣begins after
GST, i.e., the first correct processor to enter view 𝑣does so after GST. By Lemma 5.7, all correct
processors enter view 𝑣by 𝑡+ 𝛿. If any correct processor had 𝑏′ in its local value blocks upon
entering view 𝑣, then that processor had an M-certificate for 𝑏′ at that time, and disseminates it
(line 4). The leader 𝑝𝑖therefore receives this M-certificate by 𝑡+ 2𝛿≤𝑡+ 2Δ, and adds 𝑏′ to its own
blocks by this time. In this case, 𝑝𝑖disseminates (release,𝑏) without making a second proposal.
Upon receiving this message, all correct processors evaluate votability for 𝑏using their current
value of blocks, which now contains 𝑏′ (since the M-certificate has been disseminated). All correct
processors can therefore vote for 𝑏.
When a second proposal is needed. If 𝑝𝑖has not added 𝑏′ to blocks by 𝑡+ 2Δ, then—assuming the
view begins after GST—no correct processor had 𝑏′ in blocks upon entering view 𝑣, and hence
no correct processor has voted for 𝑏. In this case, 𝑝𝑖proposes a new block 𝑏′′ for view 𝑣(whose
parent is determined by 𝑝𝑖’s current local variable b) and disseminates (release,𝑏′′). All correct
processors can then vote for 𝑏′′.
The formal specification and verification appear in Appendix A.

The data expansion bound for 2-round finality
In this section, we establish bounds on coding efficiency for protocols with 2-round finality. We
first explain the connection between SMR protocols with 2-round finality and Byzantine Broadcast.
We then define 2-round Byzantine Broadcast formally, state our theorem, and prove it using an
indistinguishability argument.
From 2-round finality to Byzantine Broadcast. An SMR protocol has 2-round finality if, when
the leader is correct, the network is synchronous, and sufficiently many processors are correct, a
block proposed by the leader is finalised after two rounds of message exchange. Protocols with
2-round finality, such as E-Minimmit [11], Hydrangea [32], and Kudzu [30], assume 𝑛≥5𝑓+ 1
(𝑛≥5𝑓−1 is necessary and sufficient [23]).
We formalise the relevant notion of Byzantine Broadcast below, and then explain how any SMR
protocol with 2-round finality yields a solution.
8.1
2-round Byzantine Broadcast
We define 2-round Byzantine Broadcast (2-round BB) in the partially synchronous model with
Δ = 1. Let 𝑓∗≤𝑓be a parameter.
• We consider a set Π of 𝑛processors, of which at most 𝑓display Byzantine faults.
• One processor is designated the broadcaster. All processors are told the identity of the
broadcaster.
• The broadcaster is given an input in some set 𝑉. The set 𝑉is known to all processors.
• The protocol must satisfy the following conditions:
– Termination. All correct processors must eventually output a value in 𝑉.
– Agreement. No two correct processors output different values.
– Validity. If GST = 0, the broadcaster is correct with input 𝑣, and at most 𝑓∗processors are
faulty, then all correct processors output 𝑣by timeslot 2.
From SMR with 2-round finality to 2-round BB. Any SMR protocol with 2-round finality yields
a protocol for 2-round BB as follows. Given an input 𝑣, the broadcaster acts as the first leader
and proposes a block containing 𝑣. Processors then execute the full SMR protocol and output the
first finalised payload. Validity follows from the protocol’s liveness guarantee: if GST = 0, the
broadcaster is correct, and at most 𝑓∗processors are faulty (for these protocols, 𝑓∗= 𝑓), the block
is finalised within two rounds. Agreement follows from Consistency. Termination follows from
the protocol’s general liveness mechanism—processors eventually finalise some block, even if this
requires additional rounds. Our lower bound therefore applies to any SMR protocol with 2-round
finality.

Related work
Classical Byzantine Consensus. The study of protocols for reaching consensus in the presence
of Byzantine faults was introduced by Lamport, Shostak, and Pease [24], with a treatment of SMR
given later by Schneider [29]. Dwork, Lynch and Stockmeyer [14] showed that 𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1 is
optimal for partial synchrony. Standard protocols using this assumption, such as PBFT [9] and
Tendermint [5, 6], satisfy 3-round finality. As shown by [1], this is optimal. More recently, Chan
and Pass [10] introduced Simplex, a particularly simple protocol for partial synchrony with rotating
leaders and two rounds of voting per view. Simplex achieves 3-round finality with 𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1 and
has a clean structure that lends itself well to extension. Our protocols build directly on Simplex,
extending it with erasure coding, stable leaders, and the mechanisms for pushing the data expansion
rate towards 1 described in this paper.
Erasure coding in distributed protocols. The use of erasure codes to reduce communication costs
in distributed protocols has a long history. Cachin and Tessaro [8] introduced asynchronous verifiable
information dispersal (AVID), combining erasure codes with Merkle trees to allow a broadcaster to
distribute data among 𝑛servers so that the data can be recovered from any sufficiently large subset
of fragments, with each fragment verifiable against a commitment in the block header. Our use
of certified fragments follows this approach directly. As detailed below, a number of other recent
SMR protocols also make use of this approach. The Pipes framework of Lewis-Pye, Nayak and
Shrestha [25] analyses the throughput implications of erasure coding in leader-based protocols,
showing that the data expansion rate directly governs the throughput bottleneck. Our work builds
on this line of research, establishing that the 2.5 rate is optimal for 2-round finality and showing
how to circumvent this bound with 3-round finality.
Protocols with 2-round finality. A long line of work [4, 16, 18, 22, 27, 34] considers protocols
with a ‘fast path’, allowing quick finalisation under favourable conditions. FaB [27] introduced
a parameterised model with 𝑛≥3𝑓+ 2𝑝+ 1 processors, achieving 2-round finality when at
most 𝑝processors are Byzantine. Kuznetsov et al. [23] later showed that the optimal bound is
𝑛≥3𝑓+2𝑝−1 (see also [1]). Recent protocols in this family include Minimmit and E-Minimmit [11],
Alpenglow [21], Kudzu [30], Hydrangea [32], Banyan [37], and ChonkyBFT [15]. Among these,
E-Minimmit, Kudzu and Alpenglow incorporate erasure coding, achieving data expansion rates of
approximately 2.5. Our impossibility result (Section 8) shows that this rate cannot be improved for
protocols with 2-round finality.
Optimistic proposals and stable leaders. In rotating-leader protocols, a standard bottleneck is
that the leader of view 𝑣+1 must wait for a certificate from view 𝑣before proposing. Moonshot [13]
introduced the concept of optimistic proposals, in which the leader of view 𝑣+ 1 proposes a block
as soon as it receives the proposal for view 𝑣, without waiting for the corresponding certificate.
Hydrangea++ [31] applies the same technique to Hydrangea. In this paper, we combine optimistic
proposals with stable leaders (where a single leader proposes across multiple consecutive views) to
allow the next leader to begin proposing before the current superview has concluded, eliminating
the inter-superview gap.
DAG-based protocols. It has often been observed that low communication complexity does not
necessarily translate into high throughput in practice. Narwhal and Tusk [12] demonstrated that
building on a DAG-based dissemination layer, in which all processors share responsibility for
transmitting transactions, can yield significantly higher throughput than leader-based approaches.
Narwhal further improves throughput at the systems level by allowing each processor to employ
multiple worker nodes for data transmission, effectively trading additional hardware resources

(CPUs and network bandwidth) for performance. While increased bandwidth can be incorporated
into our model, the use of multiple CPUs per processor is not. Because data and protocol messages
are transmitted by many processors concurrently, DAG-based protocols typically incur higher
communication complexity than their leader-based counterparts. Beyond serving as a dissemination
layer, a number of works [2, 17, 19, 20, 33, 35] use the DAG structure itself to reach consensus. Early
DAG-based protocols suffered from high round-latency, and reducing this to match leader-based
protocols has been an active area of research.

Discussion
We have shown that protocols with 2-round finality cannot achieve a data expansion rate below
2.5, and that protocols with 3-round finality can push the data expansion rate arbitrarily close to
1. Two protocols realising this were presented: Carnot 1, which assumes 𝑛≥4𝑓+ 1 and avoids
any extra fragment dissemination, and Carnot 2, which operates under the optimal assumption
𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1 at the cost of additional fragment dissemination when Byzantine processors interfere.
Several questions remain open. First, our impossibility result applies to protocols with 2-round
finality (one round of voting). It would be interesting to establish tight bounds on the achievable data
expansion rate for protocols with 3-round finality in the worst case. Carnot 1 achieves approximately
1.33 and Carnot 2 achieves approximately 1.5, but we do not know whether these rates are optimal
for their respective resilience assumptions.
Second, while the Pipes model analysis of Section 7 (to be added) provides formulas relating
superview length, erasure coding parameters, and throughput, an interesting practical question is
how to set these parameters dynamically in realistic settings where network conditions and fault
rates are in flux. More broadly, one might wish to move between protocols with 2-round finality
(which require fewer rounds of communication but a higher data expansion rate) and 3-round
finality (which can achieve lower data expansion rates and thus higher throughput) depending on
current demand. Understanding how to smoothly transition between these regimes is an appealing
direction for future work.

Carnot 2: The intuition
In this appendix, we present Carnot 2, which operates under the optimal resilience assumption
𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1. Much of the protocol is identical to Carnot 1. The key differences concern view
progression, the use of threshold signatures for stage-1 votes, and the mechanism for ensuring data
availability when the leader uses an aggressive erasure code.
Stage-1 certificates and view progression. In Carnot 1, stage-1 votes do not use threshold
signatures, and processors progress to the next view only upon receiving an M-certificate or an
N-certificate. In Carnot 2, we use threshold signatures for stage-1 votes (formed from 𝑛−𝑓signature
shares, since 𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1), allowing stage-1 notarisations to be compactly represented as stage-1
certificates. These certificates are forwarded by processors upon receipt, just as M-certificates and
N-certificates are.
View progression is then modified as follows. A processor 𝑝𝑖progresses to view 𝑣+ 1 upon the
earliest of the following events:
(a) 𝑝𝑖receives a stage-2 M-certificate for a view 𝑣block (as in Carnot 1), or;

Lewis-Pye and O’Grady
31
(b) 𝑝𝑖receives a stage-1 certificate for a view 𝑣block 𝑏and successfully reconstructs the payload
of 𝑏from certified fragments (or recovery fragments, described below), or;
(c) 𝑝𝑖receives an N-certificate for view 𝑣(as in Carnot 1).
In case (a), the M-certificate serves as proof of data availability, and no further action is needed. In
case (c), the view is nullified and no data availability is required. Case (b) is the new case: 𝑝𝑖has
reconstructed the payload but does not yet have proof that other correct processors can do so as
well. In this case, 𝑝𝑖sets a timer and continues with other instructions (proposing and voting in
subsequent views as normal). The timer does not impact the critical path during synchrony and
when processors act correctly, and is only used to determine whether and when 𝑝𝑖should send
recovery fragments. The choice of timing parameter is described in Appendix C.
If the timer expires and 𝑝𝑖has not yet received an M-certificate for 𝑏, then 𝑝𝑖sends certified
recovery fragments of 𝑏to other processors. Upon receiving a recovery fragment together with the
corresponding stage-1 certificate, a processor echoes its recovery fragment to all. This is analogous
to the two types of extra fragment echoing described in Section 3.4: (a) 𝑝𝑖may need to send a
processor 𝑝𝑗their recovery fragment, and (b) 𝑝𝑗may subsequently echo their recovery fragment to
all processors.
Recovery fragments when 𝑘= 𝑛−𝑓−1. An important optimisation applies when the recon-
struction parameter for the block is 𝑘= 𝑛−𝑓−1. In this case, the primary (𝑛,𝑛−𝑓−1)-encoding is
already conservative enough for recovery when correct processors other than the leader echo their
fragments. Certified recovery fragments are therefore identical to certified fragments. This means
that 𝑝𝑖need not send a recovery fragment to any processor from whom it has already received
a certified fragment, nor to any processor to whom it has already sent one, which may be every
processor. In many cases, this results in no extra communication at all.
Recovery fragments when 𝑘> 𝑛−𝑓−1: the verification problem. When 𝑘> 𝑛−𝑓−1, the
recovery mechanism requires re-encoding the payload using the more conservative (𝑛,𝑛−𝑓−1)-
code. If done naively, this introduces a subtle issue concerning verifiability.
In the primary encoding, each fragment is accompanied by a Merkle proof against the Merkle
root committed to in the block header, which is signed by the leader. This allows any processor
to verify that a fragment is consistent with the block the leader proposed. Recovery fragments,
however, belong to a different erasure coding of the same payload, with a different Merkle tree and
a different root. A processor receiving a recovery fragment has no way to verify it against the block
header, because the block header only contains the Merkle root for the primary (𝑛,𝑘)-encoding.
A naive approach therefore creates an opportunity for attack. A Byzantine processor could
claim to have reconstructed the payload, produce recovery fragments for an arbitrary payload, and
disseminate them. Correct processors, unable to verify these fragments, would echo them. While
this does not violate consistency (a processor that reconstructs the payload can check it against the
primary Merkle root, and no processor votes on the basis of recovery fragments when 𝑘> 𝑛−𝑓−1),
it allows Byzantine processors to cause correct processors to disseminate an arbitrary amount of
data, which is undesirable.
Dual Merkle roots. The solution is to have the leader commit to the recovery encoding at the
time of proposal. Specifically, when proposing a block with reconstruction parameter 𝑘> 𝑛−𝑓−1,
the leader computes two erasure encodings of the payload: the primary (𝑛,𝑘)-encoding and a
recovery (𝑛,𝑛−𝑓−1)-encoding. The block header includes the Merkle roots of both encodings.
Since the leader already has the full payload, this requires only additional computation (two erasure
encodings, potentially computed in parallel, instead of one) and adds only a single hash to the block
header, with no extra communication.

Recovery fragments can now be verified: a processor receiving a recovery fragment checks it
against the recovery Merkle root in the block header, just as it would check a primary fragment
against the primary Merkle root. Byzantine processors can no longer produce fake recovery
fragments that will be accepted by correct processors.
Handling a Byzantine leader. A Byzantine leader could commit to a bogus recovery Merkle
root, one that does not correspond to the same payload as the primary encoding. To guard against
this, a correct processor that reconstructs the payload from the primary encoding must verify the
recovery Merkle root before adding the block to blocks and progressing to the next view. It does so
by re-encoding the payload using the (𝑛,𝑛−𝑓−1)-code, computing the resulting Merkle root, and
checking that it matches the one in the block header. If it does not match, the processor knows the
leader is Byzantine: it does not add the block to blocks, does not send a stage-2 vote, and instead
nullifies the view.
Summary. Carnot 2 extends Carnot 1 with stage-1 certificates and a recovery mechanism for
ensuring data availability under the weaker assumption 𝑛≥3𝑓+ 1. When a correct processor
reconstructs the payload and progresses to the next view without an M-certificate, it sets a timer.
If the timer expires without an M-certificate arriving, the processor produces certified recovery
fragments and sends them to other processors, who echo them upon receipt. When 𝑘= 𝑛−𝑓−1,
recovery fragments coincide with primary fragments, limiting unnecessary communication. When
𝑘> 𝑛−𝑓−1, a dual Merkle root mechanism ensures that recovery fragments are verifiable. Under
standard operation with correct leaders, the M-certificate arrives before the timer expires, and no
recovery fragments are produced.

exists 𝑡′ < 𝑡such that ProposeReady = true as locally defined for 𝑝𝑖at 𝑡′, and 𝑝𝑖proposes a block
𝑏for view 𝑣at 𝑡′. It follows that all correct processors receive a votable fragment for view 𝑣within

Let 𝑘be the reconstruction parameter for 𝑏. Suppose first that no correct processor adds 𝑏to
blocks by 𝑡+ 2𝛿. Then, since fragments are well disseminated in view 𝑣, at least 𝑘processors
enter view 𝑣and disseminate stage-1 votes and their certified fragments of 𝑏by 𝑡+ 2𝛿. All correct

If any correct processor adds 𝑏to blocks by 𝑡+ 2𝛿upon receiving a stage-2 M-certificate for 𝑏,
then all correct processors receive that certificate by 𝑡+ 3𝛿, and so add 𝑏to blocks by that time. If

□
Lemma D.6 (Liveness). Carnot 2 satisfies Liveness.
Proof. Given Lemmas D.1-D.5, the proof is almost identical to that for Lemma 5.11. The only
part of the proof that differs is in establishing that, if any correct processor 𝑝𝑖adds a block 𝑏′
to blocks, then some correct processor recovers the payload of 𝑏′. If 𝑝𝑖adds 𝑏′ to blocks upon
receiving a stage-2 M-certificate for 𝑏′ then the proof is the same as in Lemma 5.11. Otherwise, 𝑝𝑖
already recovers the payload of 𝑏′ upon adding it to blocks, so the claim is immediate.
