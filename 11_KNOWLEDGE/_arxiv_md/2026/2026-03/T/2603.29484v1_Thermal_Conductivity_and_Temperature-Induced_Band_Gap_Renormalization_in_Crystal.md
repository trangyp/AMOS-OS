Thermal Conductivity and Temperature-Induced Band Gap
Renormalization in Crystalline and Amorphous Ga2O3
Rustam Arabov,1 Jiaxuan Li,2 Xiaotong Chen,2 Nikita Rybin,1, 3 and Alexander Shapeev1, 3
1)Skolkovo Institute of Science and Technology, Bolshoi bulvar 30, build.1, 121205, Moscow,

3)Digital Materials LLC, Kutuzovskaya str., 4A, 143001, Odintsovo, Russia
(Dated: 1 April 2026)
In the present work, we performed calculations of the lattice thermal conductivity (LTC) and electron–phonon inter-
actions in crystalline and amorphous gallium oxide. The calculations were performed by coupling a machine-learned
interatomic potential – the moment tensor potential (MTP) model – to first-principles calculations. Crystalline β-
Ga2O3 exhibits a pronounced zero-point band gap renormalization (BGR) of ∼0.2 eV and a BGR of ∼0.45 eV at
700 K. The computed temperature dependence of BGR induced by classical nuclear motion in β-Ga2O3 is stronger
than that in amorphous Ga2O3. Thermal transport calculations reveal that the LTC of amorphous Ga2O3 remains near
0.9 W m−1 K−1 for temperatures between 300 K and 700 K, which is approximately an order of magnitude lower than
that of β-Ga2O3. Overall, the presented MTP-based framework provides a computationally tractable and reliable route
for predicting properties of semiconductors (both crystalline and amorphous) under operating conditions relevant to
microelectronics and optoelectronics.
Gallium oxide is among the most promising materials
for applications in novel electronic and optoelectronic de-
vices, which are rapidly developed nowadays. β-Ga2O3 is
a wide band gap semiconductor applied in high-power elec-
tronic devices1, light detectors2, and LEDs3. Amorphous gal-
lium oxide can be used as a material for gas sensors, mem-
ory devices, and photodetectors4. The application potential
of Ga2O3 is determined by its thermal transport and elec-
tronic properties, which are influenced by lattice vibrations,
or phonons. Phonons transfer heat, defining the lattice thermal
conductivity (LTC), which is the main part of thermal conduc-
tivity in semiconductors5. In addition, lattice vibrations influ-
ence the electronic band structure via electron-phonon cou-
pling, leading to band gap renormalization (BGR)6. These
effects of lattice vibrations on thermal transport and elec-
tronic properties can be investigated by several ab initio DFT-
based computational methods.
For example, the approach
based on the solution of the Boltzmann transport equation for
phonons7–9 and the Green-Kubo method10,11 are applicable
for calculating LTC of solids. Phonon-induced BGR can be
calculated using the Allen-Heine-Cardona theory12.
However, these approaches require calculating interatomic
energies and forces for large supercells and long molecular
dynamics trajectories. Therefore, these DFT-based methods
are prohibitively computationally expensive for complex ma-
terials. Nevertheless, there is a way of overcoming this limi-
tation. Machine-learning interatomic potentials, such as mo-
ment tensor potentials (MTPs)13, allow one to calculate inter-
atomic energies and forces with accuracy close to DFT, but
much more efficiently. Thus, computation of interatomic en-
ergies and forces within the aforementioned approaches for
obtaining LTC and BGR can be done by means of machine-
learning interatomic potentials instead of DFT, which will
make the calculations much less expensive14,15.
In the present work, we investigate heat transport and
electron-phonon coupling in crystalline (β-Ga2O3) and amor-
phous Ga2O3 (a-Ga2O3). Using MTPs to compute interatomic
energies and forces, we investigate BGR induced by lattice
vibrations in these materials and calculate LTC for a-Ga2O3
with the Green-Kubo approach. The gaps between absorption
edges are considered as band gaps in our work. The results
obtained in this work (BGR in β-Ga2O3, LTC of amorphous
gallium oxide) show a satisfying agreement with the experi-
mental data16–19.
The MTPs used for computing interatomic energies and
forces were trained on the DFT calculations data. All DFT
computations in the present work were performed by means of
VASP software20 with the projector augmented wave (PAW)
method21. To describe the exchange-correlation functional,
the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE)22 parametrization of the
generalized gradient approximation (GGA)23 was applied.
The 3d electrons of Ga were explicitly included in the cor-
responding pseudopotential. The plane-wave basis cutoff en-
ergy and the electronic self-consistency threshold for all DFT
calculations were set to 500 eV and 10−6 eV, respectively.
The procedure of MTP training and validation for β-Ga2O3
was the following. At the beginning, the unit cell geome-
try optimization was done for this material in the scope of
DFT. The unit cell was relaxed in five stages, varying the al-
gorithm, stopping criteria for ionic relaxation, and degrees of
freedom for each stage. Details on the geometry optimiza-
tion process are given in supplementary material (see the sec-
tion “β-Ga2O3 unit cell geometry optimization”). After opti-
mizing the β-Ga2O3 unit cell geometry, the MTP of level 18
(325 parameters) was trained for predicting interatomic ener-
gies and forces in this material. The training set for the MTP
consisted of the configurations from the work of Rybin and
Shapeev14 and the snapshots taken from an additional NpT
molecular dynamics (MD) trajectory for β-Ga2O3. The pro-
cedure of this MTP training is described in the section “De-
tails on MTP training and validation for β-Ga2O3” in supple-
mentary material. The accuracy of the MTP was validated

After that, we analyzed the influence of lattice dynamics an-
harmonicity on electron-phonon coupling in this material. We
computed the anharmonicity measure34 σA(T) and calculated
BGR taking into account the anharmonicity of nuclear mo-
tion and lattice thermal expansion. The anharmonicity mea-
sure quantifies the ratio between the forces arising from anhar-
monic interactions and the total interatomic forces in a mate-
rial. Thus, σA(T) can be used for distinguishing between har-
monic and anharmonic materials. For calculating BGRMD(T)
considering the nuclear motion anharmonicity, used the sam-
ples taken from the NVT MD trajectories. Classical NVT
MD simulations lasting for 100 ps with a 1 fs timestep were
performed with the supercell consisting of 120 atoms at each
temperature of interest. 35 samples separated by 1 ps inter-
vals were taken from each MD trajectory for further BGR cal-
culations. All MD simulations with the MTP in the present
work were carried out using LAMMPS software35 with the
LAMMPS-MLIP interface36.

In addition, according to the data given in Fig. 8, both crys-
talline and amorphous Ga2O3 possessed significant renormal-
ization of the band gap with temperature (BGRMD(900 K) =
0.66±0.03 eV and BGRMD(900 K) = 0.48±0.03 eV for β-

gap for calculating the electronic properties of Ga2O3.
After analyzing BGR in a-Ga2O3, we moved on to the char-
acterization of heat transport in this material. The method
based on the Green-Kubo formula10,11,41 was applied for cal-
culating the LTC of a-Ga2O3 by integrating the heat current
autocorrelation function (HCACF) extracted from the MD tra-
jectories. The LTC values for amorphous Ga2O3 were ob-
tained at the temperatures of 300 K, 400 K, 500 K, and 700 K.
At each temperature, 20 independent MD simulations were
carried out with the MTP. The final dependence of LTC on
temperature for amorphous Ga2O3 was obtained by averaging
the LTC values computed based on the 20 MD trajectories.
The details on LTC calculations for a-Ga2O3 are described in
supplementary material (see the section “LTC calculations for
a-Ga2O3”).
The results of LTC calculations for a-Ga2O3 were com-
pared with the experimental data30. As shown in Fig. 9, the
calculated values were slightly lower than the experimental
thermal conductivity. However, the theoretical and experi-
mentally measured values had the same order of magnitude.
Furthermore, our calculations successfully captured the weak
dependence of thermal conductivity on temperature observed
experimentally across the studied temperature range.

ence between the crystalline and amorphous Ga2O3 is the fol-

Overall, the electron-phonon coupling and thermal trans-
port properties were systematically investigated for crystalline
and amorphous gallium oxide. The calculations were done
by means of DFT and machine-learning moment tensor in-
teratomic potentials. Based on the analysis of the obtained
results, the following conclusions were made.
The first conclusion was drawn from the investigation of
electron-phonon coupling in β-Ga2O3. We found that zero-
point nuclear vibrations made a notable contribution to band
gap renormalization (the calculated zero-point renormaliza-
tion was ∼0.2 eV). In contrast, the anharmonicity of nuclear
motion and the effect of lattice thermal expansion were found
to have a minor influence on the temperature dependence of
the band gap within the temperature range from 0 K to 900 K.
This relatively low anharmonicity of β-Ga2O3 was quantita-
tively supported by low values of the anharmonicity measure
σA for β-Ga2O3. The final BGR values, incorporating both
quantum and anharmonic effects (BGRanh+Q), aligned well
with the experimental data on BGR in β-Ga2O3, validating
our computational approach.
The second conclusion addressed the effect of structural
disorder on electron-phonon interactions in Ga2O3. It was
found that amorphization affected BGR induced by lattice vi-
brations. The dependence of BGR on temperature was weaker
in amorphous Ga2O3 than in β-Ga2O3. The significant BGR
observed in both phases (BGRMD(900 K) = 0.66 ± 0.03 eV
for β-Ga2O3 and 0.48 ± 0.03 eV for a-Ga2O3) underscored
the general importance of accounting for temperature effects
when computing the electronic properties of Ga2O3.
The third conclusion regarding thermal transport in Ga2O3
was that amorphization led to a drastic reduction of LTC in
this material. The calculated LTC for amorphous Ga2O3 was
about 10 times less than that of its crystalline β-phase coun-
terpart. This was attributed to the loss of long-range atomic
order in the amorphous state, which suppressed the propaga-
tion of phonons responsible for heat conduction in β-Ga2O3.
In addition, the computed LTC values for a-Ga2O3 had the
same order of magnitude as the experimental data.
