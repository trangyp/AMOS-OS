The Anatomy of a Blockchain Prediction Market:
Polymarket in the 2024 U.S. Presidential Election
Kwok Ping Tsang and Zichao Yang∗
May 8, 2026
Abstract
Using on-chain Polygon data, we analyze Polymarket’s 2024 U.S. Presidential Election
market and develop a transaction-level accounting framework with two components:
a volume decomposition that separates exchange-equivalent turnover from share mint-
ing and burning, and trader-level disagreement measures. Naive aggregation reports
$958M of October Trump-market volume, compared with $391M under our decomposi-
tion. Market quality improved as arbitrage-deviation half-lives fell from hours to under
a minute and Kyle’s λ dropped from 0.53 to 0.01.
During October’s large-account
episode, capital flowed into both sides simultaneously, consistent with heterogeneous-
beliefs trading rather than one-sided manipulation. The framework generalizes to other
tokenized prediction markets.

Introduction
Prediction markets, in which participants trade contracts whose payoffs are tied to the out-
comes of future events, have long been studied as mechanisms for aggregating dispersed
information (Wolfers and Zitzewitz, 2004; Arrow et al., 2008). The 2024 U.S. presidential
election brought these markets to wider public attention. Polymarket, a blockchain-based
prediction market platform, processed over $3.6 billion in trading volume on the presiden-
tial race alone,1 and its implied probabilities became part of the public debate over how to
read the race. Yet this headline figure is itself ambiguous as a measure of trading activ-
ity. Because raw on-chain transaction flows mix ordinary exchange trades with issuance and
redemption of tokenized outcome shares, the endogenous supply of traded claims creates a
basic measurement problem for such markets. A naive aggregation of transaction flows can
systematically misstate turnover (Slivkoff, 2025). In October 2024 alone, a naive aggregation
reports $958.48 million of Trump-market volume, more than twice the $391.03 million of
exchange-equivalent turnover identified by our decomposition.
Polymarket differs from earlier prediction market platforms, such as the Iowa Electronic
Markets (IEM), Intrade, or PredictIt, because matched trades are settled on the Polygon
blockchain. The resulting ledger provides a publicly auditable record of settled transactions.
Earlier work on prediction markets typically had access only to aggregate price and volume
data, whereas the on-chain ledger allows transaction-level analysis at a granularity those
settings did not permit. At the same time, Polymarket’s design introduces trade mechanisms,
including the on-chain minting, burning, and conversion of outcome shares, that do not exist
in traditional financial markets and require new measurement tools.
This paper provides a transaction-level accounting framework for measuring activity in to-
kenized prediction markets and applies it to Polymarket’s 2024 Presidential Election market.
We make two methodological contributions. First, we develop a volume decomposition that
separates exchange-equivalent trading volume, net inflow, and gross market activity. The
institutional discussion describes share exchange, minting, burning, and conversion.
The
formal decomposition focuses on separating exchange-equivalent turnover from issuance and
redemption flows. Second, we introduce three trader-level disagreement measures: exposure
dispersion, headcount polarization, and volume-weighted polarization. These measures use
the on-chain order ledger to construct high-frequency, flow-based proxies for trader disagree-
ment. The same accounting logic can be adapted to other tokenized prediction markets and
used for consistent cross-platform comparison.
We then use this framework to trace how a major political event market evolves over

reported October large-account episode is informative in this respect. Large pro-Trump bets
coincided with fresh capital entering the Democratic side and with a later rebound in volume-
weighted polarization, a pattern consistent with trading under heterogeneous beliefs (Harris
and Raviv, 1993) rather than with a single trader steering the price unopposed.
The paper contributes first to the literature on prediction-market interpretation. Predic-
tion markets have long been studied as institutions for aggregating information and forecast-
ing political and economic outcomes (Wolfers and Zitzewitz, 2004; Berg et al., 2008). At the
same time, the mapping from prices to beliefs is not mechanical. Manski (2006) shows that
equilibrium prices can reflect budget-weighted beliefs rather than mean beliefs, while Wolfers
and Zitzewitz (2006) derive conditions under which prediction-market prices can be read as
probabilities. Transaction-level work such as Rothschild and Sethi (2016) shows that trader
behavior and market microstructure matter for interpreting observed prices. This literature
provides the natural starting point for our analysis, but it generally treats price and volume
as already-defined objects. In Polymarket, volume itself has to be constructed before one can
study forecasting, liquidity, disagreement, or price impact.
The paper also contributes to the emerging literature on blockchain-based prediction
markets.

markets, while Ng et al. (2026) compare price discovery across Polymarket, Kalshi, PredictIt,
and Robinhood. Other recent work uses Polymarket’s on-chain data to study political trading

Polymarket: Architecture and Trade Mechanisms
Polymarket is a hybrid-decentralized prediction market launched in 2020.
The platform
combines an off-chain order book with on-chain settlement, which distinguishes it from fully
centralized exchanges such as Kalshi and fully decentralized protocols such as Augur. The
design has two primary components:
1. Off-Chain Central Limit Order Book (CLOB): A centralized “operator” manages the
order book and matches buy and sell orders off-chain. Traders can create, cancel, and
update orders without paying blockchain transaction fees for each order-management
action.
4


## Page 5


2. On-Chain Settlement Layer: Smart contracts deployed on Polygon hold participant
collateral, manage positions, and execute final settlement once the outcome is verified.
Collateral is denominated in USDC, a stablecoin pegged one-for-one to the U.S. dollar.
Prediction markets on Polymarket are commonly categorized into two payoff formats2:
1. Binary: A market with two mutually exclusive outcomes (YES/NO) that resolves to
either $1 or $0. For example, a market may ask whether the Federal Reserve will cut
rates in 2025.
2. Categorical: A market with multiple mutually exclusive outcomes, where the winning
outcome resolves to $1 and all others resolve to $0. For example, a market may ask
how many times the Federal Reserve will cut rates in 2025.
Although Polymarket maintains a centralized off-chain CLOB for speed, completed matches
are settled on-chain on Polygon by submitting the signed orders to one of two exchange
contracts: CTFExchange for binary markets and NegRiskCTFExchange for categorical mar-
kets. These contracts emit a rich set of logs that make executions observable from Polygon
blockchain data. In particular, OrderFilled records fill-level execution details (maker/taker,
token IDs, quantities, etc.), while OrdersMatched provides a summary of multiple orders that
are matched within one transaction.
This paper focuses on the 2024 U.S. Presidential Election market (Polymarket: Presiden-
tial Election Winner 2024) as the main empirical setting. The market contains 17 outcomes.3
Donald Trump, Joe Biden, and Kamala Harris account for the vast majority of trading vol-
ume. We therefore use these three outcomes to describe how the categorical market works
and to analyze participant behavior.
2.1
Outcome shares and P2P trading
The fundamental asset traded on Polymarket is the “outcome share,” which represents a
tradable position on a specific event’s outcome. For a given market, Polymarket issues YES
and NO shares, representing the two mutually exclusive positions for that outcome. Tech-
nically, these shares are ERC-1155 conditional tokens created using the Gnosis Conditional
Token Framework (CTF). Traders buy YES shares when they take positions on the outcome
occurring and NO shares when they take positions on the outcome not occurring. Upon

resolution, shares corresponding to the correct outcome become redeemable for 1 USDC each

The primary activity on the platform is peer-to-peer (P2P) trading of existing shares,
which facilitates price discovery. This process is managed by the hybrid architecture:
1. A trader signs an EIP-712-compliant order message off-chain with their private key.
This order, which is not a blockchain transaction, specifies the asset (e.g., Trump YES
shares), the amount, and the price.
2. The Polymarket operator receives this signed message and places it in the off-chain
CLOB.
3. The Polymarket operator’s matching engine finds one or more complementary orders
from other traders.
4. Once a match is found, the operator submits the signed orders from all parties to the ap-
propriate exchange contract (CTFExchange for standard markets, or NegRiskCTFExchange
for categorical markets) in a single on-chain transaction.
5. The smart contract atomically executes the trade by calling the fillOrder(.) and
matchOrders(.) functions. This entire process is recorded on-chain in two primary
events: OrderFilled and OrdersMatched.
A simple P2P trade works as follows:
(a) Trader X wants to sell three YES shares of the candidate A outcome for 0.70 USDC.
(b) Trader Y wants to buy three YES shares of the candidate A outcome for 0.70 USDC.
The Polymarket operator matches these orders and submits them to the NegRiskCTFExchange
contract, which executes the token swap. Traders therefore do not need to hold their shares
until resolution; they can sell shares before the event settles, locking in profits or cutting
losses.
2.2
Position split, merge, and conversion
While P2P trading facilitates price discovery, the creation of new shares is governed
by a separate set of on-chain functions.
These functions are executed through Polymar-
ket’s custom NegRiskAdapter smart contract, which is built on the base Gnosis CTF smart
contract to accommodate categorical markets with multiple outcomes.
Three functions
are central for understanding the protocol: splitPosition(.), mergePositions(.), and
convertPositions(.).

2.2.1
Position split
The splitPosition(.) function creates new outcome shares and is recorded as PositionSplit
events. It operates in two ways:
(1) Counterparty order matching (share creation)
This activity is the main method for creating new shares and underlies the market’s “$1.00
Rule.”
Suppose two traders submit complementary orders:
(a) Trader X is willing to pay 0.70 USDC for a YES share of the candidate A outcome.
(b) Trader Y is willing to pay 0.30 USDC for a NO share of the candidate A outcome.
After receiving the matched orders, the NegRiskCTFExchange contract calls the NegRiskAdapter
contract to execute the splitPosition(.) function.4 The combined 1 USDC is locked as
collateral. A new, full set of (1 YES, 1 NO) shares is minted and transferred to Trader X
and Trader Y accordingly.
(2) Unilateral arbitrage (price ceiling)
This function can also be used by arbitrageurs when the YES-NO bundle is overpriced.
If the combined price of shares on the open market is greater than $1.00, an arbitrageur
can call splitPosition(.) directly, provide 1 USDC in collateral, receive 1 YES and 1 NO
share, and immediately sell them for a risk-free profit, applying downward pressure to the
prices.
2.2.2
Position merge
The mergePositions(.) function is the inverse operation. It burns a full set of out-
come shares and releases the underlying collateral. These activities are recorded on chain as
PositionsMerge events. The function also operates in two ways:
(1) Counterparty order matching (share burning)
Polymarket can match two traders wishing to sell their opposing shares. For example:
(a) Trader X wants to sell a YES share of the candidate A outcome for 0.70 USDC.
(b) Trader Y wants to sell a NO share of the candidate A outcome for 0.30 USDC.
After receiving the full set of one YES and one NO share, the NegRiskCTFExchange
smart contract calls the NegRiskAdapter contract to execute mergePositions(.) and burn
the shares. This process unlocks the 1 USDC of collateral, which is then distributed to the
traders: 0.70 USDC is sent to Trader X and 0.30 USDC to Trader Y.
(2) Unilateral arbitrage (price floor)

Position conversion
While the splitPosition(.)

an additional mechanism to maintain price consistency between mutually exclusive outcomes

The convertPositions(.)
function plays this role.
This function is implemented
through the NegRiskAdapter smart contract and is used for capital efficiency and inter-
market arbitrage. On chain, these actions are recorded as PositionsConverted events.
This function allows traders to convert a portfolio of NO shares into an economically
equivalent portfolio of YES shares and USDC. Consider a “winner-take-all” market for an
election with three candidates: A, B, and C, where exactly one will win.
Consider two portfolios:
• Portfolio 1: 1 NO A share + 1 NO B share
• Portfolio 2: 1 YES C share + 1 USDC

scenarios.
The two portfolios are economically identical because their resolution values are the same
in all scenarios. The convertPositions(.) function allows a trader to transform Portfolio
1 into Portfolio 2 unilaterally. This improves capital efficiency because the trader can release
1 USDC of collateral while preserving the same expected payoff.
This same mechanism enables inter-market arbitrage. If the market prices of these portfo-
lios diverge (e.g., if Price(NO A) + Price(NO B) becomes cheaper than Price(YES C) + $1),
an arbitrageur can buy the cheaper Portfolio 1, call convertPositions(.) to transform it
into Portfolio 2, and sell the assets from Portfolio 2 for a risk-free profit. This arbitrage activ-
ity applies pricing pressure that maintains consistency across all outcomes in the categorical
market.
More generally, in a market with N total outcomes, a trader can convert Q units of NO
shares across M outcomes into Q units of YES shares for the remaining N −M outcomes,

categorical market.

share burning, and position conversion, means that a naive aggregation of on-chain flows
would misrepresent actual trading volume. We therefore develop a transaction-level volume
decomposition in the next section.

The examples in Table 2 illustrate why measuring trading volume on Polymarket requires
care. In a simple trade, volume is straightforward because the transaction resembles a con-
ventional secondary-market exchange. In the example above, the corresponding volume is
123.9 USDC. By contrast, share minting and share burning reflect primary issuance and re-
demption against collateral, so these flows should be tracked separately by share type rather
than treated as ordinary turnover. Mixed transactions create the main accounting problem
because they combine secondary-market exchange with minting or burning within the same
matched transaction. In such cases, naive aggregation of on-chain records can misstate the
underlying economic activity.
For each matched transaction, Polymarket emits two types of on-chain events: OrderFilled
and OrdersMatched.
The OrderFilled event is recorded for each matched order, while
OrdersMatched summarizes the transaction as a whole. Table 3 reports the full event log
for the mixed trade in Table 2. Economically, this transaction contains two components:
(1) a conventional exchange in which 200 Trump YES shares trade for 84 USDC, and (2)
a minting component that creates 38.095237 pairs of Trump YES and Trump NO shares
backed by 38.095237 USDC of collateral.
A first naive approach is to measure volume using only the OrdersMatched event. In
the mixed-trade example, this approach assigns 99.999999 USDC of volume to Trump YES
alone. That measure captures the buyer’s total payment, but it omits the Trump NO volume
created through minting and therefore misallocates volume across assets. The same problem
arises in pure share-minting and share-burning transactions, where OrdersMatched records
only one side of the underlying economic activity.
A second naive approach is to aggregate the OrderFilled events. In the same example,
this method implies Trump YES volume of 84 + 99.999999 = 184.999999 USDC and Trump
NO volume of 22.095238 USDC. This approach recovers the omitted Trump NO trading
volume, but it overstates Trump YES turnover because the exchange component is counted
once through the seller’s fill and again through the buyer’s fill. The same double-counting
problem also appears in simple trades.
These examples show that raw event logs do not map one-to-one into economically mean-
ingful trading volume. We therefore use the transaction-level decomposition described below
to convert raw event logs into measures of market activity.

The exchange-equivalent trading volume treats offsetting mint and burn flows over the inter-
val as analogous to secondary-market turnover, thereby avoiding double counting. Because
Polymarket’s matching algorithm can determine whether a given order is filled through di-
rect exchange or share minting, the split between trade and mint volumes partly reflects the
matching mechanism rather than trader intent. Matching aggregate mints against aggregate
burns is therefore a reasonable approximation of counterfactual secondary-market volume.
Two features of V E deserve emphasis. First, V E is defined over a measurement window,
not over an individual trading decision. Within a window T, our definition pairs aggregate
mints against aggregate burns, so lengthening T generally raises V E because more mints be-
come eligible for matching against later burns. Second, this interval dependence is intrinsic,
not a byproduct of our definition. In a market where the supply of traded claims is endoge-
nous, a mint at time t1 and an offsetting burn at time t2 > t1 are economically equivalent to
a peer-to-peer trade executed over the interval [t1, t2], but the matching engine records them

and arbitrage tool: it transforms one set of positions into an economically equivalent set with-
out changing directional exposure to the event. We therefore exclude it from F, which is
designed to capture directional capital commitments. We describe conversion in Section 2
for institutional completeness but exclude it from the decomposition by design.

correction.8 These frictions are most binding in thin markets, where even a small arbitrage
trade can move quotes enough to eliminate the apparent gain (Shleifer and Vishny, 1997;

Price Impact and Manipulation Risk
Polymarket’s 2024 presidential market drew large trading volume and public attention, with
its implied probabilities routinely quoted in news coverage as a real-time forecast of the race.
In a market whose prices are read as forecasts by voters, donors, and media, the stakes of price

the manipulation channel emphasized by Hansen et al. (2004) for political stock markets and
illustrated by the 2012 Intrade Romney episode in Rothschild and Sethi (2016), in which
concentrated trading by a single participant produced sustained price differences from other
prediction venues. We therefore ask: how much capital was needed to move prices, and how

six outcomes we examine, the Trump YES market attracts the largest trading volume and

Conclusion
Polymarket’s 2024 U.S. Presidential Election market processed over $3.6 billion in headline
trading volume, but raw on-chain flows combine secondary-market exchange with primary
share minting and burning. The headline number should therefore not be read as conven-
tional turnover. This paper provides a transaction-level accounting framework for measuring
activity in a blockchain-based prediction market.
We make two methodological contributions. First, we develop a transaction-level vol-
ume decomposition that separates exchange-equivalent trading volume from net inflow and
gross market activity, resolving the measurement challenge created by mint and burn flows.
The same accounting logic can be adapted across tokenized prediction markets and used for
consistent cross-platform comparisons. Second, we introduce trader-level disagreement mea-
sures, exposure dispersion, headcount polarization, and volume-weighted polarization, that
utilize the on-chain ledger to construct high-frequency trader-disagreement proxies grounded
in observed trading behavior and capital at risk rather than surveys or analyst forecasts.
Using this framework, we document a market that matured over the 2024 U.S. Presidential
Election period. Kyle’s λ for Trump YES fell from 0.53 in the early months to about 0.01
by October, and half-lives of deviations from the YES-plus-NO identity fell from hours to
25


## Page 26


under a minute. These results suggest an order-of-magnitude reduction in price impact and
arbitrage frictions, consistent with a limits-to-arbitrage view in which depth and broader
participation discipline mispricings.
Two limitations point to future extensions.
First, our analysis of the October large-
account episode relies on public reporting rather than direct identification of trader iden-
tity. More precise classification of trader types may become feasible as blockchain-analytics
tools improve. Second, the welfare implications of prediction market activity remain open.
Whether these markets improve forecasting, distort information, or create new channels for
political influence is a separate question, but the measurement framework developed here
provides a basis for studying it.

Algorithm 1 Transaction-Level Volume Decomposition
Require: Fill records F = {(mi, ti, Mi, Ti)}K
i=1 from one transaction; outcome share IDs y (YES)
and n (NO), with USDC ID 0
Ensure: V tr(y), V tr(n), V mint(y), V mint(n), V burn(y), V burn(n)
I(a) ←{i : mi = 0, ti = a} and J (a) ←{i : ti = 0, mi = a} for a ∈{y, n}
B(a) ←10−6 P
i∈I(a) Mi and S(a) ←10−6 P
i∈J (a) Ti for a ∈{y, n}
V buy ←B(y) + B(n);
V sell ←S(y) + S(n);
vtr ←min{V buy, V sell}
Initialize V tr(a), V mint(a), V burn(a) ←0 for a ∈{y, n}
Let abuy ∈{ti | mi = 0} and asell ∈{mi | ti = 0} be arbitrary.
if V buy > V sell then
▷Share minting
a⋆←asell if
{mi : i = 1, . . . , K}
 > 1, else abuy;
¯a ←y if a⋆= n, else n
V tr(a⋆) ←vtr
V mint(a⋆) ←B(a⋆) −vtr;
V mint(¯a) ←B(¯a)
else if V buy < V sell then
▷Share burning
a⋆←abuy if
{ti : i = 1, . . . , K}
 > 1, else asell;
¯a ←y if a⋆= n, else n

V burn(¯a) ←S(¯a)

▷Pure exchange
a⋆←abuy;
