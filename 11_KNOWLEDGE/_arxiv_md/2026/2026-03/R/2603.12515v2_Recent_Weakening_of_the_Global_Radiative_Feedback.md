Recent Weakening of the Global Radiative Feedback
Senne Van Loon1,∗, Maria Rugenstein1, Mark D. Zelinka2, and Timothy Andrews3,4
1Department of Atmospheric Science, Colorado State University, Fort Collins, Colorado, USA
2Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, Livermore, California
3Met Office Hadley Centre, Exeter, UK
4School of Earth and Environment, University of Leeds, Leeds, UK
Earth’s climate stability, characterized by the global radiative feedback parameter (𝜆), varies decadally due to changing surface temperature
patterns. Recent variations in 𝜆are poorly understood as coordinated model simulations typically end in 2014. We apply a convolutional
neural network trained on climate model simulations to observation-based surface temperature reconstructions to estimate variations in 𝜆up to
2025. We find that 𝜆reached a minimum (maximum stability) around the mid 1990s (𝜆≃−3Wm−2/K), but has since weakened significantly
(𝜆≃−2Wm−2/K). We confirm these results with climate model simulations extended to 2022. The recent 𝜆weakening is not significantly
affected by El Ni˜no Southern Oscillation or Pacific Decadal Oscillation. Attribution reveals that warming in the subtropical Northeast Pacific is
an important driver of the recently weakened feedback, confirmed by targeted experiments in E3SMv2. Our approach enables near real-time
monitoring of Earth’s climate stability.
Plain Language Summary: As Earth warms, it radiates excess heat to space.
This cooling mechanism, known as radiative feedback, keeps the
climate stable. The strength of the feedback depends on the geographical pattern of surface temperature changes. We use machine learning and climate
model simulations with observed surface temperature reconstructions to estimate how the feedback has changed since the beginning of the 20th century
to present day. We find that the climate was most stable in the 1990s. Since then, the feedback has weakened, that is, the climate is currently less
efficient at losing heat than in the 1980s and 1990s. We attribute this weakening to spatially changing temperature patterns, especially in the subtropical
Northeast Pacific Ocean. By using machine learning to process current temperature fields, we can now monitor changes in Earth’s climate stability in
near real-time, helping us better understand how the planet’s warming rate is evolving.
Key points:
• Neural network applied to observed temperature and extended model simulations predict variations of the global feedback parameter up to 2025
• Radiative feedbacks experienced a minimum around 1983-2012 but have since been trending towards a less stable climate
• The warm subtropical Northeast Pacific weakened the radiative feedback recently
1. Introduction
Variations in the Earth’s surface temperature influence the energy
flux at the top of the atmosphere. The sensitivity of the global-
mean radiative response (𝑅) to changes in global-mean surface
temperature (Δ𝑇) is characterized by the global radiative feedback
parameter 𝜆: 𝑅= 𝜆Δ𝑇. The global feedback is negative in the
current climate, indicating that the Earth is able to release excess
energy by warming. A strong (in magnitude) 𝜆indicates a more
stable climate system, while a weak 𝜆indicates a less stable
climate system. The Earth’s energy imbalance (𝑁) is influenced
by both 𝑅and effective radiative forcing (𝐹), such that 𝑁=
𝐹+ 𝑅+𝜀= 𝐹+𝜆Δ𝑇+𝜀, with 𝜀denoting internal variability.
The feedback 𝜆varies on decadal timescales due to changing
spatial surface temperature patterns (e.g., Senior and Mitchell
2000; Armour et al. 2013; Gregory and Andrews 2016; Andrews
et al. 2015, 2022; Rugenstein et al. 2023). This phenomenon is
termed the “pattern effect”. Variations in 𝜆have been linked to
lapse rate and low cloud feedbacks, which are sensitive to surface
temperature patterns (e.g., Andrews and Webb 2018; Ceppi and
Gregory 2017; Dong et al. 2019; Zhou et al. 2016), but other
cloud types, surface albedo (including sea-ice), and water vapor
play a role as well (e.g., Andrews et al. 2018, 2022; Quan et al.
2025; Zhou et al. 2025).
Since 𝑅cannot be observed directly, we often use model simula-
tions to estimate 𝜆. Coordinated model efforts, such as the Cloud

Feedback Model Intercomparison Project (CFMIP3, Webb et al.
2017), have allowed for quantification of the pattern effect and its
expression in different climate models, by running atmosphere-
only climate models with observed sea surface temperature (SST)
boundary conditions. However, these simulations end in 2014,
limiting our understanding of more recent changes in 𝜆. Previ-
ous estimates have indicated that 𝜆weakened (i.e., became less
stable) after 2014, coinciding with the strong 2015-2016 El Ni˜no
event, a sign change of the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO)
index, and enhanced Northeast Pacific warming (Andrews et al.
2022; Loeb et al. 2020, 2021).
Here, we use machine learning to estimate 𝜆from historical
surface temperature reconstructions, allowing us to extend the
analysis to the present day (Rugenstein et al. 2025; Van Loon
et al. 2025).
We use a convolutional neural network (CNN)
trained on climate model simulations to learn the relationship
between spatial surface temperature and global-mean 𝑅. We then
apply this CNN to different surface temperature reconstructions
to estimate historical variations in 𝑅and 𝜆. We compare this
statistical approach to extended climate model runs after 2014 and
confirm that 𝜆has weakened more recently (i.e., more positive
feedbacks and a less stable climate system; section 3) and attribute
the weaker 𝜆to varying surface temperature patterns (section 4).

b. Convolutional neural network
We follow the methodology of Rugenstein et al. (2025) and
Van Loon et al. (2025) to predict the global radiative response
𝑅from surface temperature anomalies with a CNN (see SI for
details). Whereas Rugenstein et al. (2025) and Van Loon et al.
(2025) used only internal variability to train the CNN, here we
include the forced signal. The input to the CNN is T and the
output is 𝑅in the same year. We first train the CNN on Thist
to predict 𝑅hist from large initial-condition ensemble ESM runs
(SI Table S1). We use 14 ensemble members from each ESM
to train, three for validation, and three for testing. The gradient
of the CNN, ∇T𝑅CNN, quantifies the sensitivity of 𝑅to tempera-
ture perturbations in T and can be interpreted as local feedback
(similar to, e.g., Green’s functions; Zhou et al. 2017; Dong et al.
2019; Bloch-Johnson et al. 2024).
The gradient (SI Fig. S2)
shows that the pattern of local feedback is consistent with our
theoretical understanding of the pattern effect (Rugenstein et al.
2025). Moreover, the CNN performs well on unseen test data
(SI Fig. S3), indicating that the CNN has learned a robust, phys-
ical relationship between T and 𝑅.
Then, we finetune the CNN on TCFMIP and 𝑅CFMIP. We take the
CNN trained on historical data and continue training on amip-
piForcing data only. Since CNNs generally require large datasets
to train, this two-step process allows the CNN to learn from the
limited CFMIP dataset. We randomly select 70 years to train,
35 to validate, and 40 to test, and use those years from every
AGCM. Validation data is used to stop the training process, to
avoid overfitting. Note that, because CFMIP experiments use a
historical SST reconstruction (PCMDI AMIP, the same dataset
for each AGCM; Taylor et al. 2000), the CNN is finetuned on
2 m temperature that is strongly constrained by observations over
ocean areas. Temperature over land is freely evolving in AGCM
experiments. No extended AGCM runs are used to train the CNN.
The gradient of the CNN does not change notably after finetuning
(SI Fig. S2) and hence, historical variability of 𝜆does not change
much (SI Figs. S4-S5). However, finetuning the CNN improves
the magnitude of predicted 𝜆(Section c).
Finally, we apply the trained and finetuned CNN to Trc from dif-
ferent spatially complete reconstructions (rc; SI Table S2). We
use 2 m temperature from three atmospheric reanalyses [(ERA5,
Hersbach et al. 2020); (JRA-3Q, Kosaka et al. 2024); and
(MERRA-2, Gelaro et al. 2017)] and four datasets that com-
bine SST with land surface air temperature [(Berkeley Earth,
Rohde and Hausfather 2020); (DCENT-I, Chan et al. 2026b);
(HadCRUT5, Morice et al. 2021); and (NOAAGlobalTempV6,
Huang et al. 2024b)]. Spatial SST anomalies are similar to 2 m
temperature anomalies. We process Trc the same as the climate
model data: yearly anomalies are defined with respect to 1980-
1999 and bilinearly regridded to 2.8◦×2.8◦. Reconstructions Trc
are not used to train the CNN, but only for inference.

3. Historical variations of the global radiative feedback pa-
rameter
We confirm that𝜆(𝑡) varied substantially throughout the twentieth
century (Fig. 1a). Estimates from the CNN and AGCM exper-
iments show a maximum in the early-to-mid twentieth century
(𝜆≃−1Wm−2/K) and a minimum in the late twentieth to early
twenty-first century, although the exact maximum and minimum
periods differ. Some AGCMs show more pronounced decadal
variability than others (SI Fig. S6). HadGEM3-GC31-LL shows
a more gradual variation compared to the CNN and E3SMv2 es-
timates. The latter two indicate a minimum around 1981-2010 to
1985-2014 (𝜆≃−3Wm−2/K) and a sharp increase (weakening)
afterwards (𝜆≃−2Wm−2/K). The E3SMv2 ensemble mean pre-
dicts 𝜆≲−3Wm−2/K at its minimum, which is more negative
than most other estimates, suggesting that E3SMv2 is particularly
sensitive to varying SST patterns (Qin et al. 2022, 2024).
The sharp increase in 𝜆after 1985-2014 is confirmed by the
satellite-based estimate (purple lines in Fig. 1), in line with Loeb
et al. (2020) and Andrews et al. (2022).
However, 𝜆satellite is
weaker than 𝜆CNN and 𝜆E3SMv2, reaching 𝜆satellite ≳−1Wm−2/K
in 1995-2024. This might be due to the estimate of effective ra-
diative forcing from Forster et al. (2025) used to calculate 𝑅satellite
and thus 𝜆satellite (Section c). Van Loon et al. (2025) suggest that
the Forster et al. (2025) forcing trend estimate is biased low in
the last two decades, implying an unphysical underestimation of
𝜆. The CNN estimate 𝜆CNN uses the same method as Van Loon
et al. (2025) to determine 𝑅and is thus consistent with the strong
forcing trend found therein.
The mean CNN estimate deviates from the CFMIP ensemble
mean, even though the CNN was finetuned on that dataset. When
applying the CNN to TCFMIP, the CNN predicts 𝜆similar to the
CFMIP multimodel mean (SI Fig. S4). Therefore, 𝜆CNN has a
“model-average” pattern effect, because it has been trained on
AGCMs with a wide range of temporal 𝜆variability (SI Fig. S6).
The CNN could be finetuned on separate models to investigate
model differences in the pattern effect, which we leave for future
work. The difference between 𝜆CNN and 𝜆CFMIP is mainly ex-
plained by temperature over land, but differences in near-surface
temperature over ocean also play a role, even though SST is the
same in each CFMIP simulation. Reconstructions Trc include
observed land surface temperature, while TCFMIP has simulated
land temperature. Land surface temperature in TCFMIP neglects

observed T, most estimates indicate a 𝜆-minimum around 1981-
2010 (Fig. 1b, yellow). Since the CNN can make fast predictions
for any temperature pattern, it can be used to test such hypotheses
by applying it to artificial surface temperature anomalies.

The year 1980 is considered a turning point for the pattern effect
(Fueglistaler and Silvers 2021; Andrews et al. 2022; Wu et al.
2025), with 𝜆strengthening (stabilizing) for 30-year windows
ending after 1980 (Fig. 1). After 1980, surface temperature pat-
terns changed notably in the Pacific Ocean, with slower warm-
ing or even cooling in the eastern Pacific (Fig. 2a,b,g).
This
pattern change is associated with a weakened positive feedback
contribution from the equatorial and subtropical eastern Pacific
(Fig. 2d,e,i). This is consistent with a switch from positive to neg-
ative low cloud feedbacks over the same period, driven mostly
by marine cloud decks in the subtropical eastern Pacific (Myers
et al. 2023). Other regions contributed positively to the feedback,
such as the central Pacific and the West coast of South America,
but the global net effect was a stronger, more stabilizing feed-
back (Δ𝜆≃−1.6Wm−2/K between 1951-1980 and 1981-2010).
The strong feedbacks in the early 2000s might be connected to
stabilized global mean temperatures during the global warming
hiatus (e.g., Medhaug et al. 2017; Hedemann et al. 2017; Modak
and Mauritsen 2021), but shorter timescales than 30 years need
to be considered since the hiatus lasted only around 2000-2012.
For 30-year windows ending after 2010, 𝜆rapidly weakened
(Fig. 1). This weakening is associated with a sign-change of the
temperature pattern in the Northeast Pacific (Fig. 2b,c,h), which
contributed to a strong positive feedback (Fig. 2f,j). Also the
equatorial East Pacific contributed positively to the feedback.
These positive contributions appear once 2015-2016 is included
in the 30-year window, consistent with prior studies (Andrews
et al. 2022; Loeb et al. 2020), and intensify over subsequent
years. Removing 2015-2016 from the regression does not signif-
icantly affect the increased 𝜆, indicating that the weakening trend
is not due to a single ENSO event. The subtropical North At-
lantic contributed less positively to the feedback after 1981-2010,
but globally the feedback weakened (Δ𝜆≃1.1Wm−2/K between
1981-2010 and 1996-2025). In 1996-2025, 𝜆is still stronger than
predicted by abrupt CO2 forcing experiments (e.g., Andrews et al.
2022), possibly due to residual effects of other climate forcings
(Gregory et al. 2020, e.g.,). Delayed warming in the Southeast
Pacific kept 𝜆stronger relative to uniform warming (SI Fig. S9).
With more uniform warming, 𝜆would weaken further, getting
closer to 𝜆predicted from CO2-only forcing experiments.
Over the full historical period, different regions contributed to
temporal variability of 𝜆. Ocean temperature dominates the vari-
ability of 𝜆, but land temperature has a considerable contribution.
The CNN attributes the overall magnitude of the feedback mostly
to land temperature changes (SI Fig. S10), possibly because of
the stronger global warming signal over land. Therefore, we do
not use the CNN to attribute the overall magnitude of 𝜆, but only
its predicted variations in time. Figs. 2k-p show the regional
attribution of changes in 𝜆, by summing attribution maps over
regions indicated in Fig. 2d.
Among different ocean regions, the Southeast Pacific (Fig. 2k)
and Ni˜no 3 (Fig. 2n) regions correlate most closely with the global
feedback and contributed to the strengthening after 1951-1980.
The contribution from the Ni˜no 4 region anticorrelates with 𝜆
(Fig. 2p), in line with results of Guillaume-Castel et al. (2025),
who found that different ENSO flavors can influence top-of-
atmosphere radiation in opposite ways on inter-annual timescales.
The high correlation of 𝜆with its contribution from ENSO re-
gions indicate that long-term variability in the Tropical Pacific
has a strong influence on the pattern effect, even though the in-
fluence of single ENSO events on 𝜆variations is relatively weak
(Fig. 1b and SI Fig. S8), highlighting the distinction between
decadal trends and interannual variability. After 1981-2010, the
Northeast Pacific (NEP, Fig. 2m) stands out as a contributor to
the weakening of 𝜆(see also Fig. 2j). Between 1981-2010 and
1996-2025, the NEP contributed Δ𝜆NEP ≃0.33Wm−2/K to the
feedback weakening (∼30% of the total change). The West Pa-
cific Warm Pool (WPWP, Fig. 2l) and Southern Ocean (Fig. 2o)
have the weakest contribution to the global feedback variations.
The WPWP contributes to the overall negative sign of the feed-
back (Fig. 2d-f), but the temperature has little interannual to
decadal variability (Fig. 2a-c, g-h).
Since the relative warm
pool temperature with respect to the tropical-mean temperature
is more important for the pattern effect than the absolute tem-
perature (e.g., Dong et al. 2019; Fueglistaler and Silvers 2021),
the CNN might attribute some changes in the feedback to where
the temperature and top-of-atmosphere radiation change are oc-
curring, not where they are caused (e.g., low clouds in the East
Pacific might respond to warming in the WPWP). The CNN in
its current setup cannot distinguish between these local versus
remote processes.
The net radiative feedback is influenced by many different pro-
cesses, including lapse rate, cloud, and sea-ice feedbacks. De-
composing the net radiation into short-wave/long-wave and clear-
sky/cloud components can give additional insights into these
different components (Andrews et al. 2018). Cloud fluxes are
calculated as all-sky minus clear-sky fluxes. Training CNNs on
the the different components is beyond the scope of this paper, but
we investigate the decomposition in the E3SMv2 ensemble and
find that all components of 𝜆weaken after 1985-2014 (Fig. 3).
Aside from variations in the shortwave cloud feedback (predom-
inantly driven by low clouds) and longwave clear-sky feedback
(predominantly lapse rate feedback), also the shortwave clear-sky
feedback shows a significant increase after 1986-2015. The latter
might be due to a decline in Antarctic sea-ice after 2015 (e.g.,
Riihel¨a et al. 2021; Duspayev et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2025). The
local feedback in the Southern Ocean shows a positive change of
the same order as the feedback change in the NEP (SI Fig. S11),
mainly driven by the SW clear-sky feedback, highlighting the
role of Antarctic sea-ice in the weakening of 𝜆.

5. Discussion and conclusions
The CNN is a surrogate model that relates any surface temper-
ature pattern to 𝑅and 𝜆. Therefore, the CNN can be used for
fast experimentation on the effect of temperature variations on
𝜆, exemplified here by linearly removing ENSO and PDO from
Trc (Fig. 1b) or local attribution (Fig. 2). Such experiments can
inform climate model runs, as done here to study the influence
of the Northeast Pacific on the recent 𝜆weakening in E3SMv2,
to gain robust understanding of the pattern effect.
We update 𝜆with various surface temperature reconstructions up
to present day. This means that we can now monitor changes
in 𝜆in near real-time, without relying on new climate model
simulations, currently lagging more than a decade. Our estimate
generally follows the CFMIP3 multi-model mean before 2014,
but additional uncertainty could be introduced by applying the
CNN to much warmer (out-of-distribution) surface temperature
maps. Because the CNN uses global surface temperature maps,
not just ocean temperature, we capture the effect of land temper-
ature on 𝜆. In future work, it is important to compare the CNN
results with climate model simulations with fixed land surface
temperature (e.g., Andrews et al. 2021) to confirm the impor-
tance of land temperature variability for 𝜆variations in a causal
framework instead of a statistical one.
Regional attribution of changes in 𝜆confirms the importance
of the East Pacific for the pattern effect (e.g., Andrews et al.
2022; Loeb et al. 2020; Myers et al. 2023). The CNN attributes
the strengthening of 𝜆after 1980 to cooling in the tropical and
subtropical eastern Pacific, while the weakening after 2010 is
mainly attributed to warming in the subtropical Northeast Pacific
(Fig. 2).
AGCM experiments with SSTs fixed in this region
confirms the regional attribution suggested by the CNN.
We only use near-surface temperature to predict top-of-
atmosphere radiation with the CNN. In reality, other factors,
such as sea-ice or direct aerosol effects, also play a role (e.g.,
Riihel¨a et al. 2021; Andrews et al. 2022; Duspayev et al. 2024;
Zhou et al. 2025). Still, the CNN performs well on unseen test
data, indicating that near-surface temperature contains most of
the information needed to predict 𝑅, with 𝑟2 values above 80 %
(SI Fig. S3). Future work could include sea-ice as a predictor to
the CNN, which can give additional insight into the SW clear-
sky feedback (Fig. 3b). AGCM experiments with varying SST
but fixed sea-ice could confirm the role of sea-ice on the recent
weakening of the feedback.
In summary, the climate was the most stable in recent history be-
tween the mid-1980s and early 2010s (𝜆≃−3Wm−2/K). Over
the last three decades, 𝜆has been less stable (𝜆≃−2Wm−2/K).
This result is found both with a statistical (CNN) approach and
in two AGCMs (HadGEM3-GC31-LL and E3SMv2). Temper-
ature pattern differences between 1981-2010 and 1996-2025 are
opposite in sign to those between 1951-1980 and 1981-2010
(Fig. 2g-h), suggesting that the climate is returning to a state with
a less stabilizing feedback, possibly explaining the recent acceler-
ated warming (Alessi and Rugenstein 2023; Foster and Rahmstorf
2026). If the current surface temperature pattern persists, future
global mean warming rates might be large (possibly larger than
anticipated by freely running climate models). Weakening of 𝜆
might be counteracted or enhanced by ocean heat uptake and T,
calling for research on their local drivers and interactions.

Text S1: Details on the CNN architecture and training procedure
We use the same CNN architecture as in Van Loon et al. (2025), see Fig. S1 for a schematic. This architecture was chosen after
trying multiple hyperparameter setups, as shown in Fig. S3. Hyperparameter testing was performed using historical training data
(without finetuning), and we selected the architecture that performed best on the validation dataset.
We train the CNN using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10−5 and a mean squared error loss function in PyTorch (Paszke
et al. 2019). We use early stopping with a patience of 20 epochs and select the model with the lowest validation loss. We train the
CNN for a maximum of 1000 epochs, but it typically converges much earlier. To finetune the model on CFMIP data, we continue
training on the CFMIP training dataset, with early stopping and the same learning rate and loss function as before.
