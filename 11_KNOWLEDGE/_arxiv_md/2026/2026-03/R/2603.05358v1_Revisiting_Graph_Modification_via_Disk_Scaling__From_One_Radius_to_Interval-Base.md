Abstract
For a fixed graph class Π, the goal of Π-Modification is to transform an input graph G into a
graph H ∈Π using at most k modifications. Vertex and edge deletions are common operations, and
their (parameterized) complexity for various Π is well-studied. Classic graph modification operations
such as edge deletion do not consider the geometric nature of geometric graphs such as (unit) disk
graphs. This led Fomin et al. [ITCS’ 25] to initiate the study of disk scaling as a geometric graph
modification operation for unit disk graphs: For a given radius r, each modified disk will be rescaled
to radius r.
In this paper, we generalize their model by allowing rescaled disks to choose a radius within
a given interval [rmin, rmax] and study the (parameterized) complexity (with respect to k) of the
corresponding problem Π-Scaling. We show that Π-Scaling is in XP for every graph class Π
that can be recognized in polynomial time. Furthermore, we show that Π-Scaling: (1) is NP-hard
and FPT for cluster graphs, (2) can be solved in polynomial time for complete graphs, and (3) is
W[1]-hard for connected graphs. In particular, (1) and (2) answer open questions of Fomin et al.
and (3) generalizes the hardness result for their variant where the set of scalable disks is restricted.

Introduction
For a fixed graph class Π, the task in the Π-Modification problem is to transform an
input graph G into a graph H ∈Π using at most k modifications. Common modification
operations include vertex deletion, edge deletion, and edge addition [4], but other operations
such as vertex-splitting have also been studied [1]. The parameterized complexity of Π-
Modification is well-understood, especially when parameterized by the number k of
modifications [4,5,19,27,28,34]. Usually, we aim for an FPT-algorithm, i.e., an algorithm
with running time f(k) · nO(1), where f is a computable function that depends only on k,
and n is the overall instance size [5]. Some parameterized problems, however, are W[t]-hard
with respect to k for some t ≥1. This rules out an FPT-algorithm for k under common
complexity assumptions, and we then aim for an XP-algorithm, i.e., with running time nf(k).
Due to their applications in wireless communication [23] and capabilities to model sensor
networks [32], geometric intersection graphs are an important graph family. There, each
vertex corresponds to a geometric object and two vertices are adjacent if and only if the two
corresponding objects intersect. The classical (non-parameterized) complexity of problems
restricted to geometric intersection graphs is well-understood [14,21], and recently attention
shifted towards studying their parameterized complexity [6,17,18,30,33]. Disk graphs are

Traditionally, vertex- and edge-based modifications were the primary modification op-

However, some of these operations, such as edge addition, ignore the underlying geometry as
seen in Figure 1. This raises the question of their appropriateness for unit disk and other
geometric intersection graphs. As a response to this, two “geometry-preserving” modification
operations were recently studied: (a) disk dispersion [8–10,15], where disks can move within
a bounded radius, and (b) disk scaling [16], where disks can receive a radius different from 1.
We focus on the latter model, motivated by the observation that in applications such as
sensor networks, the position of a sensor is fixed but its transmission range, modeled as the
radius, can be adapted [32]. More precisely, in the model introduced by Fomin et al. [16], the
input contains a non-negative number α ̸= 1, and each modified disk receives radius α, i.e.,
all scaled disks are either shrunken (α < 1) or expanded (α > 1). They studied disk-scaling
for obtaining an independent set, an acyclic graph, or a connected graph, and provided
FPT-algorithms with respect to k, as well as (complementing) W[1]- and NP-hardness results.
A limitation of their model is that each scaled disk receives the same radius α. In particular,
all modified disks are either shrunken or expanded.1 In this work, we generalize their model
by allowing each rescaled disk to choose a radius within a given interval [rmin, rmax], hence
enabling disk shrinking and expanding simultaneously whenever rmin < 1 and rmax > 1:

Input:
Set S of n points in the plane, 0 < rmin ≤rmax ∈R, and k ∈N.
Question:
Does there exist a set T ⊆S and a radius assignment r: S →R>0 such that
|T | ≤k, rmin ≤r(p) ≤rmax for all p ∈T and r(p) = 1 otherwise, and G(S, r) ∈Π?

Our Contributions.
We first show that Π-Scaling is in XP with respect to k for every
graph class Π whose recognition problem can be solved efficiently (Theorem 3.3). At first
glance this might be surprising, since, although we can guess the scaled disks T , we cannot
branch on the radius assignment r or the target graph H ∈Π. To this end, we first guess for
each p ∈T its furthest unscaled neighbor far(p) in G(S, r). This allows us to reconstruct all
neighbors of p and eventually the target graph H due to the geometric nature of the problem.
Afterwards, we devise a linear programming (LP) formulation (Section 3.1) that determines
suitable radii once the disks to scale, i.e., T , and the resulting intersection graph H is fixed.
Next, we focus on specific graph classes and start with cluster graphs (see Section 2 for
the definition of the graph classes). Cluster graphs are in particular challenging since they
can require to choose a specific radius inside the interval [rmin, rmax]; see also Figure 2. We
improve the upper bound implied by Theorem 3.3 and provide an FPT-algorithm for Scaling
To Cluster parameterized by k (Theorem 4.5). The algorithm uses a branch-and-bound
procedure to iteratively determine a new disk p to scale. However, since we do not know the
final radius of p beforehand, we have to further branch to determine the adjacencies of p;
essentially gradually fixing the target cluster graph H. To ensure FPT-time, we exploit the
geometry within our problem. In particular, we show that there is only a bounded number
of “ambiguous” neighbors and non-neighbors of p: far(p), this time the furthest unscaled
disk in the same cluster as p, and clo(p), which is the closest unscaled disk not adjacent
to p, i.e., clo(p) and p are in different clusters. Once far(p) and clo(p) are determined, we
can essentially “read-off” the (unscaled) neighbors of p in H. To complement this result,
we show that Scaling To Cluster is NP-hard for any fixed rationals 0 < rmin ≤rmax
(Theorem 5.9), except for the case when rmin = 1 = rmax.
As the second special graph class we consider complete graphs. Here, it is optimal to
scale each modified disk to rmax. We show that the corresponding problem Scaling To
Complete can be solved in polynomial time (Theorem 6.3) by leveraging the polynomial-
time algorithm for Clique in unit disk graphs [13]. Theorems 4.5 and 6.3 settle two open
questions of Fomin et al. [16]; for cluster graphs, even in our more general model with
interval-based radii, for complete graphs, there is no (algorithmic) difference in the models.
As our last result, we show that the general XP-algorithm (Theorem 3.3) is for some
graph classes Π the best we can hope for, i.e., cannot be improved to an FPT-algorithm. For
example, if we want to obtain a graph that contains an independent set of size at least c, the
problem Scaling To c-IS is W[1]-hard for c, even when k = 0. This follows from the known
W[1]-hardness of detecting a size c independent set in a unit disk graph [5, Theorem 14.34].

Preliminaries
We assume familiarity with the basic notions from graph theory [7] and the foundations of
parameterized complexity theory [5]. For our algorithmic results, we assume the Real RAM
computational model, i.e., that we can perform arithmetic operations on real numbers in
unit time [12,16]. For an integer p ≥1, we define [p] := {1, 2, . . . , p}.
Let G be a simple and undirected graph with vertex set V (G) and edge set E(G). We
write V and E if G is clear from the context. For a vertex set V ′ ⊆V , we let G[V ′] denote
the subgraph of G induced by V ′. For a vertex v ∈V , we let NG(v) = {u ∈V : uv ∈E}
denote the neighborhood of v in G.
Graph Classes.
Let G be a graph. The graph G is complete if and only if uv ∈E for every
pair u, v ∈V . We call G a cluster graph if every connected component C of G is a clique,
also referred to as cluster. Equivalently, G is a cluster graph if and only if it does not contain
an induced P3, i.e., three vertices u, v, w ∈V such that uv, vw ∈E but uw /∈E. Throughout
this paper, we only consider induced P3s. Hence, a P3-free graph is a graph without an
induced P3, i.e., a cluster graph.
Disk Graphs.
For two points p, p′ ∈R2 in the plane, we denote with ∥p −p′∥2 their Euclidean
distance. We use x(p) and y(p) to denote the x- and y-coordinates of p, respectively. Let
S = {p1, . . . , pn} be a set of n points in the plane. A radius assignment r: S →R>0 assigns
to each point p ∈S a positive radius r(p). Given S and r, we define the corresponding disk
graph G(S, r) := (S, {pipj : i < j, ∥pi −pj∥2 ≤r(pi) + r(pj)}) as the graph that contains one
vertex for each point and two vertices pi and pj are adjacent if and only if their closed disks

3
A General XP-Membership Framework
Before we study Π-Scaling for different graph classes Π, we first introduce a general
framework that implies XP-membership with respect to k of Π-Scaling for many graph
classes Π. We condition the applicability of our framework on properties of the problem
Π-Recognition, which asks, for a given graph H, whether H ∈Π holds. In the following
section, we present a linear programming formulation that is a key building block in our
framework.
3.1
A Linear Program for Finding Radii
In this section, we consider the problem Constrained Scaling To Graph (ConScal),
defined as follows.
Given a set S ⊂R2 of n points, a set T ⊆S, two real numbers
rmin ≤rmax ∈R>0, and a graph H = (S, E), the question is whether there exists a radius
assignment r: S →R>0 such that (a) rmin ≤r(p) ≤rmax if p ∈T and r(p) = 1 otherwise,
and (b) G(S, r) = H? ConScal can be seen as a restricted version of Π-Scaling, where
the set of scaled disks is fixed and the target graph H ∈Π is known. Later we will “reduce”
Π-Scaling to ConScal. Let I = (S, T , H, rmin, rmax) be an instance of ConScal. Without
loss of generality, we assume that G(S \ T , 1) = H[S \ T ]; otherwise I is a no-instance. In
the following, we devise a linear programming formulation P(I) that we can use to solve
ConScal in time f(|T |) · nO(1). At this point, we would like to note that also Fomin
et al. [16] used reduction to LPs to determine suitable radii in their algorithms for their
“min-variant” of the problem, where the amount of scaling should be minimized.
Variables.
We introduce one variable xp ∈R for every point p ∈T . The value of xp
determines the radius for the disk p in the final radius assignment r, i.e., r(p) = xp will
hold. Furthermore, we introduce an auxiliary variable ε that will help us to represent strict
inequalities.
Constraints.
Since ε should help us to model strict inequalities, we force ε ≥0. For every
point p ∈T , we introduce the following constraints. First, we introduce the constraints
rmin ≤xp and xp ≤rmax to force that rmin ≤r(p) ≤rmax holds. Next, we consider the
neighborhood NH(p) of p in H and focus on points u ∈NH(p) \ T , the so-called unscaled
neighbors. We must ensure that up ∈E(G(S, r)) for every u ∈NH(p)\T . Since u /∈T implies
r(u) = 1, we can only control the existence of the edge via the variable xp and thus introduce
the constraint xp + 1 ≥∥p −u∥2. We proceed similar for the points u ∈S \ (NH(p) ∪T ),
this time ensuring that the vertex pair up does not exist in G(S, r). More concretely, we
introduce the constraint xp + 1 ≤∥p −u∥2 −ε. We use the variable ε as a slack-variable

3.2
The XP-Membership Framework
We build upon Proposition 3.2 and show that Π-Scaling is in XP with respect to k for
every graph class Π for which Π-Recognition can be solved in polynomial time.
Let Π be such a graph class. The main idea is to provide a Turing-reduction to nf(k)
instances of ConScal. To construct these instances, we exhaustively consider all possibilities
to construct the set T of at most k scaled disks and apply the following steps separately for
each constructed T —in the remainder of the paper we refer to this operation as branching or
guessing. Observe that if the radius of the modified disks would be fixed in the input, then
the graph H that we obtain after scaling is solely determined by our choice of T . However,
since each modified disk can choose a radius from [rmin, rmax], we must further branch to
determine which target graph H we want to obtain. To this end, for each possible set T , we
determine for the scaled disks their (possible) neighborhood in H by guessing for each p∗∈T
its furthest unscaled neighbor far(p∗), i.e., far(p∗) = arg maxp∈(S\T )∩NH(p∗) ∥p −p∗∥2 (if it
exists). Due to the geometric aspect of the problem, this determines all unscaled neighbors
of p∗: For p′ ∈S \ T we have p∗p′ ∈E(H) if and only if ∥p∗−p′∥2 ≤∥p∗−far(p∗)∥2; see
Figure 4. We guess the adjacencies among scaled disks. Note that this captures all relevant,
i.e., possible, graphs H we could obtain by scaling the disks in T . In particular, each of the
2O(k2) · nO(k) branches corresponds to an instance of ConScal. We discard branches with
H /∈Π, which can be checked efficiently since Π-Recognition can be solved in polynomial
time. With this, we obtain:
▶Theorem 3.3. Let Π be a graph class such that Π-Recognition can be solved in polynomial
time. Then, Π-Scaling can be solved in 2O(k2) · nO(k) time.

Proof. Consider a graph class Π such that Π-Recognition can be solved in polynomial
time and let I = (S, rmin, rmax, k) be an instance of Π-Scaling. We now provide a Turing
reduction from I to nf(k)-many instances (S, T , H, rmin, rmax) of ConScal, each representing
a different choice for T and H. This reduction consists of three major steps.
In the first step, we branch on the nk different sets T ⊆S such that |T | ≤k. If I is a
yes-instance, there exists a solution r and at least one of the branches will correspond to
T = T (r). In the following, let us consider one of these branches, i.e., one fixed set T . What
remains from defining (S, T , H, rmin, rmax) is the graph H. Steps two and three will take
care of this.
In the second step, we consider the disks in T and determine their neighborhood with
respect to the unscaled disks S \ T . To this end, consider a hypothetical graph H. For
each disk p ∈T we define far(p) := arg maxq∈(S\T )∩NH(p) ∥p −q∥2. In case of ties, we
break them arbitrarily. It could be that the set is empty, i.e., that we have pq /∈E(H)
for every disk q ∈S \ T . In other words, in a solution r, p either has no incident edge
or is only adjacent to scaled disks in G(S, r). In such cases, we set far(p) = nil. We now
make the following observation. For every unscaled disk q ∈S \ T we have pq ∈E(H) if
and only if far(p) ̸= nil and ∥p −q∥2 ≤∥p −far(p)∥2. This is because, if far(p) ̸= nil, we
have r(p) ≥∥p −far(p)∥2 −1 ≥∥p −q∥2 −1 since far(p) is the furthest unscaled neighbor
of p. In particular, observe that any edge pq with ∥p −q∥2 > ∥p −far(p)∥2 would contradict
the choice of far(p). Thus, once we have far(p), the set NH(p) ∩(S \ T ), i.e., the unscaled
neighbors of p in H, is completely determined. Consequently, we branch for each such p ∈T
into at most n possible choices for far(p). Together, this yields nk different branches for all
disks in T . By above observation, the branching step is correct.
At this point, what is missing from the construction of H are edges (or non-edges) between
two disks from T . In the third step, we branch to determine these edges. Recall that |T | ≤k.
Hence, there are at most 2O(k2) different branches. Since we perform exhaustive branching,
correctness is maintained.
Combining Steps 1–3, we obtain nk · nk · 2O(k2) ∈2O(k2) · n2k different branches, each
corresponding to a different instance I′ = (S, T , H, rmin, rmax) of ConScal. In each branch,
we check if H ∈Π holds. As Π-Recognition can be solved in polynomial time, this
takes nO(1) time, and we reject the branch if the check fails. Otherwise, it only remains to
check if for the particular choice of T and H, there exists a radius assignment r such that

A Fixed-Parameter Tractable Algorithm for Scaling To Cluster
We now consider Π = cluster graphs and show that Scaling To Cluster is FPT param-

thanks to their characterization as being P3-free.
Our algorithm follows the overall strategy of the general XP-approach behind Theorem 3.3,
but uses structural properties of cluster graphs to obtain an FPT-running time. We provide
in Figure 5 an overview of the algorithm. Let us fix an instance I = (S, rmin, rmax, k) of
Scaling To Cluster. Consider a hypothetical solution r. The algorithm has two main
phases. In the first phase, we determine the disks in T (r), i.e., Step 1 of the algorithm
behind Theorem 3.3. For each such disk p ∈T (r), we also fix two, in some sense “extremal”,
unscaled disks that allow us to deduce the membership of p in clusters from G(S, r): We
determine (a) the furthest unscaled disk far(p) which is in the same cluster as p in G(S, r),
and (b) the closest unscaled disk clo(p) which is in a different cluster than p in G(S, r). This
resembles Step 2 of the algorithm behind Theorem 3.3; see also Figure 5b–d. With this, we
can almost completely fix the neighborhood of p in G(S, r) via FPT-many branches thanks
to the structure of cluster graphs and the geometric aspect of the problem. In Phase 2, we
resolve the (non-)edges between the at most k disks in T (r). In principle, we could use the
naïve branching algorithm for Step 3 of our XP-algorithm presented in Theorem 3.3, but this
would result in a overall running time of 2O(k2) · nO(1). We argue that for cluster graphs, this
step can be done in 2O(k·log(k)) · nO(1) time. Finally, analogously to Step 3 of the algorithm
behind Theorem 3.3, we employ the LP from Section 3 to check if suitable radii for the
scaled disks exist. In the following, we first provide in Section 4.1 a more detailed overview
of the two phases before we turn in Section 4.2 to a detailed algorithmic description of them.
Finally, in Section 4.3, we establish correctness of our algorithm and bound its running time.
4.1
The Intuition of the Two Phases
We now present a more detailed overview of the two phases of our algorithm. Without loss
of generality, we assume that |S| > k. Otherwise, we replace the following Phase 1 by simply
guessing the set of scaled disks, which is asymptotically faster than Phase 1.
Phase 1.
In this phase, we aim to determine the set T of scaled disks and, for each
disk p ∈T , the (a) furthest unscaled disk far(p) which is in the same cluster as p in the
solution and the (b) closest unscaled disk clo(p) which is not in the same cluster as p in the
solution. We compute in polynomial time a maximal vertex-disjoint P3 packing P of G(S, 1),
that is, P is a set of vertex-disjoint P3s of G(S, 1) such that no further vertex-disjoint P3
of G(S, 1) can be added to P. By P we denote the set of points in S whose corresponding
disks are contained in at least one P3 ∈P. Since every P3 requires at least one scaling
operation, we can safely output no if |P| ≥k + 1. Moreover, G′ := G((S \ P), 1) is a cluster
graph. Next, we create a complete edge-colored auxiliary graph H on the entire vertex set S.
The colors of H model the connectivity after some scaling operations and represent edges
(blue), non-edges (red), and (still) undefined edges (green) between two scaled disks that we
resolve in Phase 2; see also Figure 5. More precisely, let Ti denote the set of the first i disks
we identified for scaling (hence T0 = ∅). The graph Hi represents the connectivity of the
disk graph resulting from G(S, 1) if Ti is scaled accordingly; recall that we only compute
the precise radii of the scaled disks at the end and now we only determine the final cluster
graph H, which will represent the abstract intersection graph. The graph Hi is helpful to

correspond to a cluster graph, we can abort. Otherwise, we use the LP from Section 3.1 to
check if suitable radii for the selected disks exist.

Generalizations and Concluding Remarks
We contributed to the study of geometric-aware modification operations for geometric graphs,
focusing on disk-scaling in unit disk graphs where each modified disk can choose a radius from
the interval [rmin, rmax]. Our work opens up several promising avenues for future research.
Extension to Disk Graphs.
Most results extend to (non-unit) disk graphs by adapting the
LP formulation (Section 3). The exception is Theorem 6.3, since the complexity of Clique
in disk graphs is unknown [26]. Overcoming this barrier is a promising direction to pursue.
Optimization Variants.
Fomin et al. [16] also studied an optimization variant where we
scale (at most) k disks and minimize P
p∈S |1 −r(p)|. Generalizing our algorithmic results
to this min-variant is non-trivial and remains open. In particular, for Theorems 3.3 and 4.5
we cannot simply adapt the optimization function of the LP due to our (maximized) “slack-
variable” ε (and only determining the disks to scale is not sufficient since disks might require
different radii from [rmin, rmax]).

Other Open Questions.
The most prominent open question from our work is whether we
can obtain a polynomial kernel for Scaling To Cluster. Furthermore, NP-containment of
Scaling To Cluster remains open. Finally, it would be interesting to combine scaling with
other modification operations, such as disk dispersion [15], or study the impact of structural
graph parameters, such as the vertex cover number, on our problem.
