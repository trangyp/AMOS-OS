Real-space topological singularities in structured flexural waves
Tong Fu,1, ∗Pengfei Zhao,2, ∗Liyou Luo,2 Zhiling Zhou,1

Real-space singularities underpin diverse wave phenomena yet remain largely
unexplored in elastic wave systems.
We report the observation of real-space
topological singularities in structured flexural waves on finite-sized solids. These
singularities are robust against perturbations and annihilate only through topo-
logical phase transitions.
Moreover, they imprint dislocation lines on the ra-
diated sound field, generating acoustic vortices in free space from an achiral
source and structure. Our findings bridge continuum mechanics and topological
physics, establishing elastic waves as a platform for exploring complex topologi-
cal textures in real space and paving the way towards singular phononics.
Wave singularities—spatial loci where wave properties are ill-defined—are central to understanding
the topology of wave fields. Recent years have witnessed a growing interest in exploring real-space
wave singularities in both quantum and classical systems [1–4], which bridge topological physics
and structured wave fields. Scalar waves (e.g., acoustic waves) can host phase singularities with
ill-defined phase values [5], while vector waves (e.g., electromagnetic waves) can hold polarization
singularities with indeterminate polarization properties [6, 7]. These real-space singularities can be
characterized by topological indices [6, 8, 9] and underpin exotic structures of wave fields, such as
vortex knots [10, 11], M¨obius strips [12–14], and skyrmions [15–18]. Their unique properties have
enabled diverse applications, including high-capacity communication [19, 20], optical and acoustic
manipulation [21, 22], and superresolution imaging [23, 24]. The interactions between singularity-
bearing waves and matter can give rise to intriguing spin-orbit interactions [25–27] and enable
robust wave control via the geometric topology (genus) of structures [28, 29].
Classical wave systems offer convenient platforms for exploring singular wave physics, owing to
their experimental accessibility and capability for direct field observation. Early studies mainly

Elastic waves encompass a broad spectrum, from macroscopic continuum mechanics to micro-
scopic lattice vibrations [39]. Their full vectorial nature and couplings with other waves provide a
fertile ground for investigating complex wave phenomena. Despite extensive research into the fun-
damental properties of elastic waves [40–45], the real-space topological singularities within elastic
wave systems remain largely unexplored.
In this work, we report the observation of topological singularities in flexural waves on finite-
sized solids. These singularities manifest as spatial points where the normal displacement field
vanishes and the phase becomes ill-defined. They can be characterized by quantized topological
charges (phase winding numbers), whose global sum is constrained to zero by the finite geometry of
the structure. By tuning source parameters, we observe the evolution (merging and annihilation)
of the singularities on the surface. Crucially, we show that these singularities can be imprinted onto
the surrounding air, generating acoustic vortices in free space from an achiral structure driven by
a simple point source. While the present study exploits the scalar wave approximation of flexural
waves on a closed curved surface, it opens a clear pathway toward exploring richer topological
singularities associated with vectorial and polarization textures arising from the full complexity of
elastic waves.
We consider a spherical shell with outer radius r and thickness t, as shown in Fig. 1(a). The
shell supports elastic waves with a displacement field u(r) = u⊥(r)bn + u∥(r), where u⊥(r)bn and
u∥(r) are the field components normal and tangential to the surface, respectively. We will focus on
u⊥(r)bn, corresponding to flexural waves, that can couple to acoustic radiation in free space [46],
as shown in Fig. 1(b). Without loss of generality, we assume the shell is made of photosensitive
resin with density ρ = 1190 kg/m3, Young’s modulus E = 3.4 + 0.136i GPa, and Poisson ratio ν
= 0.35. All numerical simulations are performed using a finite-element package [47].

The flexural wave singularities can only disappear via a topological phase transition. This can
be achieved by adjusting the relative position of the point sources s1 and s2, which act as exter-
nal control parameters of the system. Figure 4(a) shows a topological phase transition involving
the annihilation of the singularities “i” and “ii”, where the azimuthal angle of s2 increases from
φ2 = 270◦to φ2 = 310◦. Experimental results show that the two singularities (star symbols)
gradually approach the equator of the shell and merge at φ2 = 310◦. The measured trajectories
of the singularities agree well with the simulation results denoted by the solid lines. During this
topological phase transition, the total number of singularities on the shell reduces from 6 to 4,
corresponding to a change of the topological configuration from Type-3 to two Type-2, as shown
in Fig. 4(b). Notably, the net topological charges remain zero throughout the transition. The
transition details are further illustrated in Figs. 4(c) and 4(d) for φ2 = 280◦, 300◦, and 320◦. As
shown in Fig. 4(c), the phase transition also manifests in the acoustic domain: the pressure dislo-
cation lines originating from singularities “i” and “ii” approach and merge as φ2 increases. After

In the above discussions, we consider the spherical shell driven by two point sources with
a phase difference of π/2. This requirement of two sources originates from the high rotational
symmetry of the spherical shell: the flexural eigenmodes are highly degenerate, such that a single
point source excites only a symmetry-adapted modal response and cannot generate the modal
interference necessary for singularity formation. As a result, external interference between two
independently controlled sources is used to generate topological singularities on the spherical shell.
We now consider an ellipsoid shell excited by a single point source s, as shown in Fig.
5(a).
In contrast to the spherical shell, the reduced symmetry lifts the modal degeneracy, allowing a
single source to simultaneously excite multiple non-degenerate flexural eigenmodes with distinct
resonating frequencies. Consequently, these modes acquire different phase responses at the driving
frequency, and their interference arises intrinsically from wave propagation along different paths
on the shell.
Counterintuitively, this intrinsic modal interference gives rise to four topological
singularities (denoted by the black stars in Fig. 5(a)), despite that only a single source is used.
Figure 5(b) shows the displacement fields in the vicinity of these singularities, where the simulation
results agree well with experimental results. Among the four singularities, two carry topological
charge q = +1 and the other two carry q = −1, fulfilling the neutrality condition P
i qi = 0.
To elucidate the underlying mechanism, we apply a multipole expansion approach to analyze
the acoustic radiation fields [48, 54], as shown in Fig. 5(c). In the considered frequency range, the
dominant modes excited by the single point source are Ye20 and Ye22 (insets), corresponding to two
quadrupole modes with slightly different resonance frequencies. At the driving frequency of 2620
Hz, Ye22 is on resonance while Ye20 is off resonance, leading to a phase difference of π/2. Their
interference gives rise to the flexural wave singularities on the ellipsoidal shell and the acoustic
vortices in free space. These results demonstrate that the flexural wave singularities can give rise
to chiral elastic and acoustic phenomena without relying on chiral structures or chiral sources.

We report the observation of real-space topological singularities in flexural waves arising from
multipole mode interferences on spherical and ellipsoidal shells. These singularities exhibit vanish-
ing normal displacement fields and can be created, moved, and annihilated via controlled topological
phase transitions. Crucially, they imprint orbital angular momentum onto the radiated pressure
field, enabling the generation of acoustic vortices in free space from an achiral structure driven
by a simple point source. The results establish the singular and topological properties of elastic
waves in real space, paving the way for singular phononics and a global, topology-driven route
to structured wave generation that does not rely on local phase engineering. While the present
study focuses on flexural fields on closed shells, future work may explore tangential elastic fields
on finite structures, which can support complex polarization textures and intriguing polarization
singularities [28, 29]. Even richer topological properties may emerge from the full vectorial nature
of elastic waves in bulk solids. Finally, the concepts could be extended to the quantum regime,
where phonons may host novel forms of quantum singularities enabling unusual electron-phonon
interactions [55].

I.
EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
The experimental setup consists of a scanning laser Doppler vibrometer (LDV, OptoMET) and
a waveform generator (WFG, Keysight 33500B). The LDV laser was directed perpendicular to the
sample surface to measure the out-of-plane velocity distribution. The two samples (spherical and
ellipsoidal shells) were fabricated via 3D printing (iSLA800) using a cured photosensitive resin with
density ρ = 1190 kg/m3, Young’s modulus E = 3.4 + 0.136i GPa, and Poisson ratio ν = 0.35.
The spherical shell has a radius r = 8 cm and a uniform thickness t = 0.3 cm (Figs. 2 and
4 in the main text). Excitation was implemented using two piezoelectric transducers s1 and s2
(radius 0.55 cm), positioned in the xy-plane at (θ1 = 90◦, φ1 = 180◦) and (θ2 = 90◦, φ2 = 270◦),
respectively. The transducers were driven by the WFG with a constant amplitude of 9 V; Channel
1 drove s1 with a 0 phase, while Channel 2 drove s2 with a π/2 phase delay. The ellipsoid shell has
a thickness t = 0.3 cm and radii ry = 1.05rx with rx = rz = 7 cm (Fig. 5 in the main text). For
this geometry, only a single piezoelectric transducer s was used as the point source.
The schematic of the experimental setup, including the LDV, sample, waveform generator,
and data-acquisition system, is shown in Fig.
S1.
Flexural wave propagation is measured by
non-contact optical detection of surface motion via the Doppler effect. A coherent laser beam
is focused onto the surface of the curved elastic continuum, where local vibrations—induced by
piezoelectric excitation—produce a frequency shift (i.e., the Doppler shift) in the backscattered
light. This shift is directly proportional to the instantaneous out-of-plane surface velocity. Using
a heterodyne interferometer, the scattered light is mixed with an internal reference beam that is
frequency-shifted by a known amount, enabling demodulation of the Doppler signal and accurate
reconstruction of the vertical velocity field distribution on the spherical and ellipsoidal shells.

III.
HIGH-ORDER SINGULARITIES AND ACOUSTIC VORTICES
In the main text, the flexural wave singularities carry unit charges q = +1 or q = −1, and they
are topologically protected. Higher-order singularities can also emerge on the shell under additional
symmetry constraints. However, these higher-order singularities are protected by symmetry instead
of topology. In Fig. S3, we numerically demonstrate the emergence of higher-order flexural wave
singularities and acoustic vortices by controlling the rotational symmetry.
As shown in Figs. S3(a) and S3(b), we employ eight point sources uniformly distributed on the
equator of the spherical shell. The source phases are prescribed as eiqφ with q ∈Z and φ being the
azimuthal coordinate. Namely, the sources have an azimuthal phase increment of 2πq. Figure S3(c)
presents the numerical results for the flexural wave singularities with topological charges ranging
from q = −1 to q = +2. The corresponding phase and intensity profiles of the acoustic vortices
are shown in the upper and lower panels, respectively. More generally, arbitrarily higher-order
singularities can be achieved by using more point sources with corresponding phase distribution
eiqφ.

INFLUENCE OF SAMPLE HOLDER
In the experimental measurements, a sample holder was used to support the spherical shell, as
shown in Fig. 2 of the main text. To understand its influence on the singularities, we simulate the
topological phase transition process with the presence of the holder, assuming the holder is glued to
the sample. As shown in Fig. S4(a), the sample holder is cylindrical shell with a thickness of 0.1 cm
and a height of 1 cm, and its material parameters are: density ρ = 1190 kg/m3, Young’s modulus
E = 0.5 + 0.05i GPa, and Poisson ratio ν = 0.35. To measure the flexural fields of singularities “i”
and “ii”, the sample holder was positioned at different locations. For the singularity “i”, the holder
is located at θ = 150◦, φ = 50◦, as shown in the middle panel of Fig. S4 (a). For the singularity
“ii”, the holder is located at θ = 150◦, and φ = 50◦, as shown in the lower panel of Fig. S4 (a).
Figures S4 (b-f) present the numerical results illustrating the effect of the sample holder on the
topological phase transition, as the azimuthal coordinate φ2 of the second source is varied. The
upper panels correspond to the results without the sample holder; the middle and lower panels
correspond to the results in the presence of the sample holder. As seen, in the absence of the
sample holder, the singularities “i” and “ii” evolve symmetrically toward the equator and are
eventually annihilated. The topological phase transition occurs at φ2 ∼130◦. From the middle
and lower panels, it can be seen that while the positions of the singularities are slightly shifted due
to the presence of the sample holder, the topological phase transition still occurs in both cases.
Specifically, for the sample holder located at θ = 150◦and φ = 50◦(corresponding to the middle
panels), the topological phase transition takes place before φ2 ∼310◦, whereas for the sample
holder located at θ = 150◦and φ = 230◦(corresponding to the lower panels), the transition occurs
after φ2 ∼310◦.

VI.
EFFECTS OF GEOMETRY AND TOPOLOGY OF STRUCTURES
The singularities of the flexural waves discussed in the main text are on the surface of a spherical
and ellipsoidal shells. The singularities are topologically protected, and their net charge on the
surface is P
i qi = 0. These characteristics are invariant to neither geometric shape nor structural
topology of the shell that holds the flexural wave. In this section, we investigate the effects of
geometry and topology of structures on the flexural wave singularities.
Figure S5 illustrates the singularities of flexural waves on the spherical shell undergoing con-
tinuous geometric deformations, where all four structures have the same topology (their genus is
g = 0). As shown in Fig. S5 (a), we vary rz while fixing the excitations. When rz = 7.6 cm, the
positions of the singularities are slightly shifted, as shown in Fig. S5(c), whereas the topological
configurations remain unchanged. When rz = 7.2 cm, two new topological singularities, denoted
as “vii” and “viii”, emerge on the back side of the spherical shell, as shown in Fig. S5(d). This
process corresponds to a topological phase transition, a reversed process of the topological annihi-
lation shown in Fig. 4 of the main text. Further changing the rz to 6.8 cm keeps the topological
configuration and the number of the singularities. The lower panels summarize the topological
configurations in the cases of Fig. S5(b-e), where the net charge on the surface is always P
i qi = 0.
These results demonstrate that variations of the structural geometry may induce topological phase
transition and change the numbers of the singularities, yet the global topological property (i.e.,
net charge) remains invariant.
