Revisiting claims of extracranial biophoton detection from the human brain
Vahid Salaria,b , Vishnu Seshana,b,c, Rishabh Rishabha,b,c,
Daniel Oblaka,b, and Christoph Simona,b,c,∗
aDepartment of Physics and Astronomy, University of Calgary, Calgary, AB, Canada
bInstitute for Quantum Science and Technology, University of Calgary, Calgary, AB, Canada
cHotchkiss Brain Institute, University of Calgary, Calgary, AB, Canada
Abstract
Ultraweak photon emission is the spontaneous
emission of extremely low levels of light from a
broad range of biological systems. Recent studies
have reported that UPE measured extracranially
can serve as a potential non-invasive biomarker
of brain activity. Here, we show that this inter-
pretation suffers from serious problems.
First,
when observed under properly dark conditions,
the UPE from the head is much weaker than what
is reported in certain papers on ‘brain UPE’ from
human heads.
Signals detected in these stud-
ies are overwhelmingly dominated by background
light. Second, photons at wavelengths < 600 nm
are strongly attenuated by scalp and skull tissues,
and longer wavelengths fall largely outside the ef-
fective spectral sensitivity of the photomultiplier
tubes (PMTs) used.
As a consequence, even if
UPE from the head is detected under properly
background-free conditions, it is likely to be domi-
nated by emission from the scalp rather than from
the brain, certainly as long as PMTs are used.
Our results emphasize the importance of careful
experimental design to make genuine progress on
this important question.

It is known that reactive oxygen species (ROS)

and UPE can serve as an indicator of cellular and organ-

and various aspects of neural activity, including oxida-
tive reactions, EEG activity, cerebral blood flow, cerebral
energy metabolism, and the release of glutamate [5–9].
For instance, Kobayashi et al. have reported that in vivo
imaging of UPE from rat’s brain continuously for 6 hours
has a dynamics which correlates with theta electrical ac-
tivity of the brain [9].

that the brain - and any other living tissue - emits UPE as
a consequence of metabolic and oxidative processes, and
that correlations between UPE and electrophysiological
activity, such as EEG, are in principle plausible and sci-
entifically interesting. However, establishing such correla-
tions requires rigorous optical, spectral, and noise-control
considerations. In this work, we show that the experi-
mental methodology and signal interpretation presented
in previous studies [10–12] do not adequately meet these
requirements, and therefore do not provide a scientifically
sound basis for attributing the reported signals to brain-
derived UPE.
Results
Optical leakage greatly influences count rates
The photon count rates reported by Casey et al (25-35
kcounts per second, see Fig. 1C), are orders of magnitude
higher than the UPE levels typically documented across
living systems, which in all other studies with similar de-
tectors have been determined to be only a few to several
hundred photons per second [2]. It is necessary in UPE
experiments to ensure that the dark room is really dark.

Here, we perform an experiment to demonstrate the de-
tection procedure of the PMTs and how the signal acqui-
sition is affected by minute variations in background light-
ing. The experiment was conducted in a light-controlled
environment in which a human participant was seated
inside a fully enclosed, opaque dark tent placed within
a dark room, conditions similar to those of Ref. [3]. A
broadband PMT (Hamamatsu R4220-P) was used in this
experiment; the quantum efficiency diagram is shown in
Fig. 2A. Note that the response curves of our PMT are
similar to those used by Casey et al. (Fig. 2B). The active
photocathode area of our PMT (192 mm−2), was approx-
imately half the area of the device used by Casey et al
(380mm−2). The PMT was positioned at a fixed distance
of approximately 5 cm from the participant’s forehead to
monitor UPE from the head. This is inspired by Casey et
al.’s experiments, in which PMTs were positioned approx-
imately 5 cm above the head surface with their apertures
facing the corresponding brain regions of interest.
Prior to any measurements on the participant, the in-
trinsic PMT dark count rate was recorded with the PMT
entrance optically capped to establish the instrumental
background. The intrinsic PMT background was found
to be stable at an average rate of approximately 25 counts
per second. To assess the sensitivity of the measurement
to ambient light leakage, controlled leakage tests were
conducted by switching on the room lights outside the
dark tent while introducing small apertures in the tent
fabric. Linear slits of approximately 5 mm and 10 mm
in length were sequentially applied, and the correspond-
ing changes in PMT count rates were recorded. Fig. 3B
shows that opening a 5 mm gap resulted in PMT count
rates of approximately 1000 counts per second, while a 10
mm gap increased the rate to approximately 40000 counts
per second, comparable to the intensities reported in the
Casey et al. study. Note that under these conditions, the
room still appears completely dark to the naked eye.
Subsequently, measurements were performed with the
PMT uncapped and facing the forehead under complete
darkness, with the slits closed, to record the putative
UPE signal.

Optical attenuation
Previous measurements of light transmission through the
head show that shorter wavelengths are severely atten-
uated by skin and skull. Using experimental transmis-
sion data for human skin, skull bone, and brain tissue,
Hart et al. [14] have shown that for wavelengths below
about 600 nm, the combined transmission through scalp
and skull is effectively negligible for realistic layer thick-
nesses (see Fig. 4A-C). Even at 620 nm, transmission
through skin plus several millimetres of skull can fall in
the range of 0–2%, depending on bone thickness and ge-
ometry.
Only wavelengths above ∼600–650 nm (red
/ near-infrared) achieve non-zero, though still modest,

The PMTs used by Casey et al.
are Sens-Tech
DM0090C, with peak quantum efficiency in the blue–
green range and a steep decline towards longer wave-
lengths (see Fig. 2A). In practice, typical biophoton
PMTs have their highest sensitivity between roughly 350
and 500 nm, show markedly reduced quantum efficiency
near 600 nm to 5%, and are relatively insensitive above
∼650 nm, i.e. below 2%. Consequently, the detectors are
optimized for wavelengths that cannot realistically pass
through the scalp and skull. They are insensitive to the
very wavelengths (red / near-infrared) that can, in prin-
ciple, traverse these tissues with non-negligible transmis-
sion. Even if a tiny fraction of > 650 nm photons escape
the head, they fall into the least sensitive region of the
detector response. A close examination of Casey et al.’s
PMT quantum efficiency diagram (Fig. 2A) and the trans-
mission curves of the human skull and skin (Fig. 4A,C)
indicates that the wavelength best suited for extracranial
detection of ‘brain biophotons’ is approximately 700 nm.
At this wavelength, the peak detection fraction is about
0.0004–0.0005, obtained by multiplying the skull and skin
transmission by the PMT quantum efficiency. To achieve
an extracranial signal of a few tens of thousands photons
per second per cm2, the brain would need to emit on the
order of at least 108 biophotons per second per cm2, which
is five to seven orders of magnitude higher than the ex-
pected levels based on previous UPE studies. Therefore,
even if photons from the head are recorded under prop-
erly dark conditions, they are much more likely to come
from the skin than the brain.
Conclusion
The possibility of detecting UPE from the head raises im-
portant questions about the potential of UPE as a func-
tional brain readout. However, a strict optical blackout is
essential to minimize ambient light leakage into the detec-
tion system, as even trace stray photons can overwhelm
true UPE signals. Further, tissue and detector proper-
ties impose strict limits. Scalp and skull block nearly all
sub-600 nm photons, and the PMTs used are relatively
insensitive above 600 nm. Hence, even if photons from
the head are recorded under properly dark conditions,
they are much more likely to come from the skin than the
brain. It is also worth noting that Casey et al.’s signal
from the head is actually lower than the background (see
Fig. 1B). This suggests that what they observe is the head
blocking the background light.
In conclusion, for UPE to provide a non-invasive win-
dow into brain physiology, it would require the devel-
opment of measurement protocols that eliminate back-
ground light and detectors sensitive to wavelengths trans-
mitted through the skin and skull.
With these safe-
guards in place, UPE could yet become a biomarker
for neuroscience, clinical monitoring, and next-generation
brain–computer interfaces.
