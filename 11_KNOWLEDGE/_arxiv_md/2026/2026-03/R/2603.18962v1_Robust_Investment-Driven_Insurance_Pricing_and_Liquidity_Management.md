Abstract
This paper develops a dynamic equilibrium model of the insurance market that jointly charac-
terizes insurers’ underwriting, investment, recapitalization, and dividend policies under model
uncertainty and financial frictions. Competitive insurers maximize shareholder value under a
subjective worst-case probability measure, giving rise to liquidity-driven underwriting cycles and
flight-to-quality behavior. While an equilibrium typically fails to exist in such dynamic liquidity
management framework with external financial investment, we show that incorporating model
uncertainty restores equilibrium existence under plausible parameter conditions. Moreover, the
model uncovers a novel relationship between the correlation of insurance and financial market
risks and the equilibrium insurance price: negative loadings may emerge when insurance gains
and financial returns are positively correlated, contrary to conventional intuition.
Keywords: Investment-driven insurance pricing; Model uncertainty; Liquidity management;
Underwriting cycles; Flight-to-quality; Ergodicity

Introduction
In recent years, supply-side perspectives have become increasingly important for understanding
the functioning of insurance markets. On the one hand, the post-pandemic era, together with
rapid advances in artificial intelligence and related technologies, has exposed insurers to new and
increasingly complex sources of risk (Feng et al., 2022; Scholes, 2025). Owing to limited historical
data and incomplete understanding of these emerging risks, many remain difficult to price or manage
through transfer, diversification, or hedging. Consequently, insurers are often forced to retain such
risks on their balance sheets or even withdraw coverage entirely.
On the other hand, insurers behave like financial intermediaries: they can raise funds at relatively
low cost through underwriting and actively participate in capital markets. For instance, in the U.S.
corporate bond market, insurers have long been among the largest institutional investors, playing a
crucial role in providing long-term financing to the real economy (Koijen and Yogo, 2023b). However,
empirical evidence suggests that insurers also face substantial financial frictions, particularly in the
aftermath of the 2008 global financial crisis, which have imposed shadow costs on insurance pricing
and capital allocation (Koijen and Yogo, 2015, 2016; Ge, 2022).
Understanding how supply-side product and financial market frictions affect the equilibrium
outcomes of the insurance industry is important, because in a competitive market, these frictions are
ultimately passed on to households, thereby influencing overall social welfare. In this paper, we focus
on a specific form of informational friction, model uncertainty, also known as ambiguity (Knight,
1921; Ellsberg, 1961), which arises from insurers’ limited understanding of the true distribution of
risks they face. Early survey evidence among insurers and actuaries has confirmed that risks with
ambiguous probabilities tend to be priced much higher (Hogarth and Kunreuther, 1992; Kunreuther
et al., 1993). However, the extent to which such shadow cost should be incorporated into pricing
remains a theoretical question, requiring a structural framework to address. Beyond underwriting
risks, insurers are also exposed to financial risks, which are widely recognized to exhibit a high degree
of ambiguity in their return distributions (Epstein and Schneider, 2010; Guidolin and Rinaldi, 2013),
further complicating insurers’ pricing and portfolio decisions.
To address these challenges, this paper develops a continuous-time equilibrium model of the
insurance market that jointly characterizes insurers’ investment-driven pricing and liquidity manage-

ment under model uncertainty. On the one hand, we build on the barrier-type equilibrium framework
of Henriet et al. (2016), in which the aggregate liquid reserves held by the industry serve as the
state variable governing insurers’ strategies. When reserves rise to a sufficiently high level, insurers
distribute dividends to shareholders until capacity falls below the payout boundary; conversely,
when reserves decline below a lower threshold, they raise external capital to restore liquidity to the
financing boundary. Between these two thresholds lies an internal financing region, within which
the equilibrium insurance price, investment amount, and market-to-book ratio are all endogenously
determined as functions of aggregate capacity.
On the other hand, we incorporate insurers’ concerns about model uncertainty following the
robust control framework of Hansen and Sargent (2001, 2008) and Anderson et al. (2003). In this
setting, insurers optimize their decisions under the worst-case probability measure subject to an
entropy penalty, which captures the trade-off between maximizing firm value and guarding against
model misspecification. The resulting worst-case density generator can be interpreted as the market
price of model uncertainty, representing the implicit shadow cost ultimately borne by policyholders.
Compared with the benchmark equilibrium without model uncertainty in Henriet et al. (2016),
introducing robustness concerns leads to a fundamental change in the structure of the equilibrium
equations for both the insurance price and the market-to-book ratio. Specifically, the recapitalization
boundary is no longer zero but becomes strictly positive, preventing the industry from falling into
a liquidity trap. Intuitively, if insurers’ aggregate capacity were to collapse to zero, underwriting
and investment activities would cease, and the market could not spontaneously recover. Under
robustness, both the equilibrium price and the market-to-book ratio are generally higher, reflecting
insurers’ demand for additional compensation for model uncertainty.
This study is closely related to three papers in the literature, to which we contribute by offering
extensions and marginal improvements from distinct perspectives. First, we extend Pang (2026)
by incorporating outside financial investment opportunities, making the model more aligned with
real-world practice. Relative to the benchmark without financial investment, the numerically solved
equilibrium outcomes show that the minimum, maximum, and overall range of aggregate capacity all
expand, indicating that the insurance market can sustain larger cyclical fluctuations. Consequently,
the expected duration of underwriting cycles becomes substantially longer. While the market-to-book
ratio increases markedly, the equilibrium insurance price does not necessarily rise, reflecting a

non-monotonic interaction between pricing and financial exposure. Furthermore, the share of risky
investment increases monotonically with insurers’ aggregate liquid reserves, suggesting decreasing
effective risk aversion as liquidity buffers accumulate.
Second, while Luciano and Rochet (2022) extend the dynamic equilibrium framework of Henriet
et al. (2016) by incorporating financial market exposure and introducing the concept of insurers’
risk appetite, we build upon theirs by explicitly introducing concerns for model uncertainty, thereby
capturing a form of supply-side informational friction. We further generalize their setup by allowing
for correlation between insurance and financial market risks, rather than assuming independence.
More importantly, we would like to gently point out that the equilibrium defined in Luciano and
Rochet (2022) does not, in fact, theoretically exist, at least not in a barrier-type form. The reason is
that, once financial investment is introduced, the aggregate investment amount they derive tends to
diverge to infinity as industry capacity approaches the dividend payout boundary. Consequently,
the endogenous payout boundary cannot be finite, even if an equilibrium were to exist in principle.
An important theoretical contribution of this study is therefore to demonstrate the existence of an
equilibrium within a dynamic liquidity management framework that incorporates outside financial
investment opportunities. While we acknowledge that this existence cannot be rigorously proven for
all parameter values (and indeed may fail under extreme settings), our numerical results show that,
under economically reasonable parameterizations, such a robust equilibrium does emerge.
Third, we uncover an interesting relationship between the correlation between insurance and
financial market risks and the equilibrium insurance price, which contrasts with the pattern reported
in Chen et al. (2025). While Chen et al. (2025) also builds on the equilibrium pricing framework of
Henriet et al. (2016) and examines the joint underwriting-investment problem of a representative
CARA insurer, it finds that the endogenous loading factor (i.e., the equilibrium insurance price) may
become negative when insurance gains and financial returns are negatively correlated, as investment
income can hedge underwriting losses. And when the two markets are independent or positively
correlated, the equilibrium price is proved to remain strictly positive. In contrast, under the existence
of a robust equilibrium, our numerical results reveal an opposite pattern: the equilibrium price can
turn negative for a positive correlation coefficient, rather than a negative one, and tends to decline as
the correlation increases. This seemingly counterintuitive outcome arises from the market structure
under model uncertainty: when insurance and financial risks become more positively correlated, the

market price of model uncertainty increases sharply. Consequently, insurers quote lower premiums
because the perceived probability of extreme adverse outcomes is up-weighted in their robust pricing
measure. Unlike in Chen et al. (2025), where a negative loading implies expected underwriting losses,
here the expected underwriting profit under the worst-case measure (the sum of the market price
of insurance and the price of model uncertainty) remains non-negative even when the equilibrium
price itself becomes negative. Finally, since in practice we can only observe insurance prices and
estimate the correlation between insurance and financial markets, but cannot directly measure
whether insurers exhibit ambiguity aversion, our results provide a theoretical explanation for the
possible coexistence of positive correlation and negative equilibrium prices in reality.
To summarize, our paper contributes to the growing literature on the financial economics of
insurance, which emphasizes extending the supply-side theory of insurance markets by incorporating
financial frictions, regulatory constraints, and imperfect competition (Koijen and Yogo, 2023a).1
Following the idea that equity is costly for financial intermediaries (He and Krishnamurthy, 2013;
Brunnermeier and Sannikov, 2014), we treat equity financing costs as the key form of financial
friction faced by insurers. This mechanism makes liquidity management the central determinant of
insurers’ dynamic equilibrium behavior, giving rise to the underwriting cycles and flight-to-quality
phenomena. In addition, we highlight the informational friction arising from uncertainty in the loss
distribution, which plays an essential role in insurers’ exposure to emerging risks such as climate and
cyber risks (Koijen and Yogo, 2022b). We argue that robust pricing frameworks, which explicitly
account for model uncertainty, should become an integral part of modern insurance practice.
We also complement the classical literature in actuarial science and insurer risk management.
From a pricing perspective, we build on the equilibrium pricing framework, which emphasizes that
insurance prices depend not only on underlying risk characteristics but also on market conditions
and capital constraints (Bühlmann, 1980, 1984; Winter, 1994; Henriet et al., 2016; Feng et al., 2023).
From a risk management perspective, while the implications of ambiguity have been extensively
examined in the context of insurers’ investment and reinsurance strategies,2 its direct impact on
insurance pricing has received comparatively less attention despite its first-order importance. Recent
studies, such as Zhao and Zhu (2011), Anwar and Zheng (2012), Dietz and Walker (2019), and Dietz

Equilibrium Solution
In line with Henriet et al. (2016), as well as with many related liquidity management problems,
we focus on a barrier-type solution. Specifically, let M and M denote the payout and external
financing boundaries, respectively, whose values are to be determined endogenously by the equilibrium
conditions. To ensure the existence of such the solution, we impose the following assumption.
Assumption 1. We impose the following conditions on the equilibrium outcomes, which can be
numerically verified under reasonable parameter settings in Section 4:
(1) Both v(·, ·) and u(·) are smooth functions, continuously differentiable up to the second order.
(2) For M ∈[M, M], it holds that u(M) ≥0 and u′(M) ≤0. In other words, the market-to-book
ratio of the insurance sector is assumed to be a decreasing function of aggregate capacity.
(3) For the payout and recapitalization boundaries, we impose 0 ≤M < M. In addition, m ≥0
is required, as it would be unrealistic to assume that either an individual insurer or the entire industry
continues underwriting when liquid reserves are negative.
This assumption would simplify the equilibrium analysis and ensure that the equilibrium possesses
well-behaved properties. The monotonicity of u(·) is crucial for guaranteeing that the optimal
policy takes a barrier-type form. Otherwise, the payout and recapitalization regions could become
disconnected, leading to multiple thresholds and more complex strategic behavior.

Quantitative Analysis
In this section, we perform numerical simulations to solve the ODE system and characterize the
dynamic equilibrium of the insurance market. As the benchmark setting, we specify the parameter
values as follows. The discount rate is set to λ = 4% for shareholders’ valuation of future cash flows.
Following the estimation of Luciano and Rochet (2022), we set the unit loss to l = 1.0 and the
volatility of unit loss risk to η = 28%. The risk-free rate is r = 1.528%. The portfolio of risky assets
consists of 80% bonds and 20% equity stakes, implying a weighted-average expected excess return
of µ −r = 2.0% and a volatility of σ = 18%. The cost of external financing is parameterized as
γ = 20%, interpreted as the expected return on equity financing, while the risk aversion coefficient
of insurees (general households) is set to α = 2.0. For the correlation between the insurance and
financial markets, due to the limited empirical evidence in the literature, we assume a moderate
negative value of ρ = −0.2. This assumption is broadly consistent with recent macro-financial
conditions, where slower economic growth has been accompanied by higher insurance payouts and
lower interest rates, which in turn increase the value of fixed-income investments. Finally, following
Ling et al. (2021), we adopt θ = 2.8 as the benchmark degree of robustness concern.
In the numerical computation, the free boundaries are solved jointly with the state variables,
starting from a smooth initial guess until convergence is achieved. The equilibrium outcomes are
found to satisfy Assumptions 1 and 2, confirming the internal consistency of the numerical solution.

4.2
Impact of Correlation Coefficient
Fixing all other parameters, we vary the correlation coefficient to examine its impact on equilibrium
outcomes, as shown in Figure 4.3. As the correlation between insurance and financial returns increases
from negative to positive, there is no clear monotonic pattern in the boundary values, the market-to-
book ratio, or the aggregate investment amount. However, the equilibrium insurance price tends
to decline overall for a given level of aggregate capacity. More interestingly, when the correlation
becomes relatively positive, the equilibrium price can even turn negative at high capacity levels.
This finding contrasts with the conventional result in the literature, where insurance prices are
typically lower under negative correlations between the two markets and negative loadings arise due
to insurers’ hedging motives (Chen et al., 2025).
The seemingly counterintuitive outcome here stems from the market structure under model
uncertainty: when risks become positively correlated, the market price of model uncertainty rises
substantially, leading insurers to quote lower premiums as the perceived probability of extreme
adverse outcomes is effectively up-weighted in their robust pricing measure.
Furthermore, we find that when the correlation becomes sufficiently large in magnitude (whether
positive or negative), the system of equations in (3.13) fails to yield a numerical solution satisfying
Assumptions 1 and 2. This indicates that a robust equilibrium may not always exist, even though
we cannot derive the exact parameter thresholds analytically.

Conclusion
This paper develops a dynamic equilibrium model of the insurance market that integrates
insurers’ underwriting, investment, recapitalization, and dividend decisions under the joint influence
of model uncertainty and financial frictions. By embedding robust control into a barrier-type
liquidity management framework, the analysis demonstrates that model uncertainty can restore the
existence of equilibrium that otherwise fails to hold when insurers have access to external financial
investment opportunities. The resulting equilibrium generates liquidity-driven underwriting cycles
and flight-to-quality dynamics consistent with empirical evidence in the literature.
Moreover, the model reveals that ambiguity aversion raises both the market-to-book ratio and
the recapitalization boundary, preventing the market from falling into liquidity traps. Financial
investment expands the range of aggregate capacity, thereby lengthening the duration of underwriting
cycles. In addition, the equilibrium insurance price may turn negative when insurance gains and
financial returns are positively correlated and tends to decline as the correlation increases, contrary to
conventional intuition in models without ambiguity. Together, these findings offer a unified framework
that links insurers’ robustness concerns, financial frictions, and market dynamics, providing new
implications for robust pricing and liquidity management in the insurance industry.
