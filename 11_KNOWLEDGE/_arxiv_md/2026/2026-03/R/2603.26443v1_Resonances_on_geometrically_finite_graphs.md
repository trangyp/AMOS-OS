RESONANCES ON GEOMETRICALLY FINITE GRAPHS
CHRISTIAN ARENDS, CARSTEN PETERSON, AND TOBIAS WEICH
Abstract. In analogy with the spectral theory of geometrically finite hyper-
bolic manifolds, we initiate the study of resonances on geometrically finite
(q + 1)-regular graphs of groups. We prove the meromorphic continuation of
the resolvent of the adjacency operator on such spaces and give a geometric
characterization of the resonant states. In contrast to the hyperbolic surfaces
setting, geometrically finite graphs have only finitely many resonances and may
be computed explicitly, yet exhibit many of the same qualitative phenomena
as in the hyperbolic manifolds setting. Particularly interesting examples arise
from algebraic curves over finite fields.

1. Introduction
Resonances of the Laplacian, also called quantum resonances, are a well estab-
lished spectral invariant on certain classes of noncompact Riemannian manifolds.
On closed manifolds, the Laplacian has discrete L2-spectrum; resonances, which
are also discrete, serve as a suitable replacement for the failure of this fact to
remain true in the setting of infinite volume. A prototypical and yet extremely
fruitful setting is that of geometrically finite hyperbolic surfaces, where the study
of resonances dates back to Patterson [Pat75; Pat76a; Pat76b]. We refer to [GZ97;
Zwo99; BJP05; BD18; MN20] for a small selection of papers studying resonances
on geometrically finite, or more particularly, convex cocompact hyperbolic surfaces.
Over the past decades, the study of the spectral geometry of the Laplace–Beltrami
operator on hyperbolic surfaces has been paralleled by the spectral theory of the ad-
jacency operator on (q + 1)-regular graphs. From an algebraic perspective, there is
already an evident parallel by viewing hyperbolic surfaces as the locally symmetric
space of the group SL(2, R), and viewing (p+1)-regular graphs as the “locally sym-
metric spaces” of SL(2, Qp) (via the Bruhat-Tits tree). Additionally, there is by now
a long tradition of developing ideas in one setting and subsequently adapting them
to the other setting. A notable example of great interest is optimal spectral gaps
on random regular graphs and hyperbolic surfaces. Friedman [Fri08] proved that a
random (q + 1)-regular graph asymptotically almost surely has adjacency operator
spectral gap below 2√q+ε for every ε > 0 as the number of vertices goes to infinity;
this had been previously conjectured by Alon [Alo86]. A new proof was later given
by Bordenave-Collins [BC19] using the notion of strong convergence. These papers
laid a template for tackling the analogous question of optimal Laplacian spectral
gap 1
4 −ε for random hyperbolic surfaces, which was subsequently proven by Hide-
Magee [HM23] and Magee-Puder-van Handel [MPH25] in the setting of random
covers of hyperbolic surfaces, and Anantharaman-Monk [AM25b; AM25a; AM25c]
(inspired by Friedman’s approach) and Hide-Macera-Thomas [HMT25] (inspired by
the approach of Bordenave-Collins) in the Weil-Petersson model of random hyper-
bolic surfaces. See also the recent survey article by Monk-Naud [MN26].
The aim of this article is to establish a notion of resonances for the adjacency
operator on a certain class of infinite graphs which are the “right” analogues of
geometrically finite manifolds in the setting of graphs.
In fact, the objects we
study must be properly understood not as graphs in the usual sense, but as graphs
of groups. We defer a detailed discussion of the precise definition of geometrically
finite graphs (and the justification for why they deserve to be named as such) to
Sections 2 and 3. However, for now we state that, analogously to the setting of
hyperbolic surfaces, geometrically finite graphs are those which can be decomposed

In contrast to the setting of hyperbolic manifolds, geometrically finite (q + 1)-
regular graphs of groups have only finitely many resonances.
Theorem 1.3. The number of resonances in finite. More specifically:
(1) The number of resonances, counted without multiplicity, is bounded by 2|L|,
where |L| is the number of vertices in the compact core.
(2) The multiplicity of a given resonance is bounded by 3(|L|+nc +(q +1)nf)2,
where nc and nf are the number of cusps and funnels, respectively.
The bound on the number of resonances without multiplicity is essentially sharp.
On the other hand, the bound on the multiplicity is likely far from sharp; in this
work we simply sought to get an explicit bound.
Generically we should expect
resonances to have multiplicity one. Theorem 1.3 is proven by combining Propo-
sition 5.2 (where a slightly sharper bound is given than in (2) above) and Corol-

It is a consequence of our results that resonances can be computed explicitly
via reduction to a finite dimensional linear algebra problem. In Section 7 we com-
pute resonances explicitly for several example. A particularly interesting class of
examples are constructed via algebraic curves over finite fields; we summarize this
construction in Section 3.4. In Section 7.7, we compute the resonances explicitly
for two examples constructed from elliptic curves. In particular, we observe that
the resonances correspond to the “trivial” ℓ2-eigenvalues (±√q), the remaining ℓ2-
eigenvalues (which are known to all lie on the unit circle as a result of Drinfeld’s

In the setting of geometrically finite hyperbolic manifolds, the theory of res-
onances is very closely connected with the Lax-Phillips scattering theory [LP76].
Roughly speaking, the resonances correspond to the poles of the scattering operator.
The Lax-Phillips scattering theory is itself defined in terms of the (automorphic)
wave equation, and in general the resonances control the long term behavior of waves
on the manifold. In follow-up work, we plan to connect the theory of resonances
on geometrically finite graphs developed in this paper with the wave equation on
regular trees and graphs [BL14; BL13; Ank+13], and in particular with the Lax-
Phillips scattering theory in this setting studied by the second author in [Pet25].
This should in turn be very closely related to the analogue of Eisenstein series in
this setting which we plan to also develop; this should also allow us to rigorously
describe in representation theoretic terms the structure of the resonances for arith-
metic examples mentioned in the previous paragraph. More generally, there is a
plethora of natural follow-up questions by analogy with the manifolds setting, such
as the connections to Ruelle resonances of the geodesic flow (see Hilgert’s recent

2. Geometrically finite graphs of groups: definitions
2.1. Graphs of groups. In order to properly discuss our results, we must utilize
the language of graph of groups, which lies in the domain of Bass-Serre theory. The
standard reference for this theory is Serre [Ser80]. When a group Γ acts on a tree
T , we want to construct a quotient “graph” Γ\T which will allow us to in turn
recover Γ. In case Γ acts freely on T , then we can make sense of Γ\T as a graph
in the usual sense, and we can recover Γ from the quotient via the usual covering
space theory. We shall however be particularly interested in the case that Γ does
not act freely.
Arguably the most important example of a discrete subgroup of SL(2, R) is
Γ = SL(2, Z). However, seeing as this group has torsion elements, the quotient
Γ\H has orbifold points. We naturally would want to view Γ as an example of a
geometrically finite group, but this would require in general working with orbifolds
rather than manifolds.
On the other hand, the Selberg lemma tells us that Γ
(and more generally any finitely generated subgroup of a linear group over a field
of characteristic zero) has a finite index torsionfree subgroup Γ′.
Consequently
Γ′\H is a genuine manifold.
In light of the Selberg lemma, we see that in the
manifold setting, there is essentially no need to work with orbifolds since, up to
finite (orbifold) covering (which from a spectral theoretic perspective is easy to
handle), we can always assume we are working with a genuine manifold.
In the setting of discrete subgroups of the isometry group of the tree there is no
analogue of the Selberg lemma. In fact, torsion is an essential part of the theory
and, in particular, the function field theoretic analogue of the modular curve, which
is the discrete subgroup Γ = SL(2, Fq[T −1]) inside of SL(2, Fq((T))), contains no

2.2. Geometrically finite graphs of groups. We are now ready to define the
class of graphs of groups which we will study, namely geometrically finite graphs of
groups. These are graphs of groups which can be “cut” into finitely many pieces,
each of which is of a specified form. To make precise the notion of “cutting” a graph
of groups, we slightly extend the notion of graphs of groups to that of graphs of
groups with boundary. This means that we allow some vertices to have “half-edges”
e which are “edges” with specified terminus vertex v, but not origin vertex, and
with Ge and ϕ−
→

from v. Heuristically these are the objects one obtains when one “cuts” a graph
of groups along a collection of edges, and one just considers some subcollection of
the resulting connected components. Clearly two graphs of groups with boundary
can be “glued together” along a prong from each assuming the edge groups are the
same (and upon choosing an isomorphism between these edge groups).
We can now define the main constituents of geometrically finite graphs of groups,
namely orbifold funnels and cusps.
Definition 2.1. Let G = (V, E) be a locally finite rooted tree with root o. An
orbifold funnel is a graph of groups with boundary whose underlying vertex and
edge set are the same as G, but with a prong h emanating from o. The vertex and
edge groups are such that Go = Gh and more generally Gv = G{v,w} ≤Gw for
every vertex v such that there is an edge {v, w} with vertex w closer to o (and h)
than v. In case Go = 1, and thus all other Gv = 1, we simply call it a funnel. See
Figure 2.1.
Note that an orbifold funnel is simply the quotient of a funnel by a finite group
(which necessarily preserves the root and the prong).
Definition 2.2. Let G be a ray graph, whose vertices are labelled by Z≥0. We
call the vertex labelled by 0 the root, and denote it by o. A cusp is a graph of
groups whose underlying vertex and edge set is the same as G, but with a prong h
emanating from o, and such that Gi = G{i,i+1} for every i ∈Z≥0. See Figure 2.2.
If we ignore the prong, then a cusp consists of an infinite sequence of inclusions
Gi ,→Gi+1. This is also called a Nagao ray in the graph of groups literature.
Definition 2.3. A graph of groups G is called geometrically finite if it can be cut
into finitely many pieces, one of which is a finite graph of groups with boundary

3. Geometrically finite graphs of groups: motivations
The purpose of this section is to motivate the term “geometrically finite” for the
graphs defined in Definition 2.3.1 We begin by discussing a result of Paulin [Pau04]
defining and characterizing geometrically finite graphs of groups by appropriately
adapting a general definition of geometric finiteness given by Bowditch [Bow95].
We then motivate the definition of funnels and cusps in the setting via explicit
analogies with hyperbolic geometry. We also prove that convex cocompact graphs
of groups are exactly those which are geometrically finite but without any cusps.
Finally we discuss a rich source of geometrically finite graphs of groups which are the
analogues of arithmetic hyperbolic surfaces. Their construction utilizes algebraic
curves defined over finite fields.
3.1. Geometric finiteness in negative curvature. Originally, a hyperbolic 2-
or 3-manifold was called geometrically finite if it admitted a finite-sided convex
polyhedral fundamental domain in the corresponding hyperbolic space. However,
this definition no longer makes sense if one works more generally with negatively
curved manifolds; in fact this definition is no longer even suitable if one restricts to
higher dimensional hyperbolic manifolds.
Let X be a Hadamard manifold with pinched negative curvature. Let Γ be a
group acting discretely and isometrically on X. Bowditch [Bow95] discussed sev-
eral competing definitions of geometric finiteness in this setting (and which were all
known to be equivalent to the classical definition of geometric finiteness for hyper-
bolic 2- and 3-manifolds), and he proves that these definitions are all equivalent.
We shall focus on one particular definition which ultimately also makes sense more
generally in the setting of groups acting on negatively curved metric spaces (such
as trees).
