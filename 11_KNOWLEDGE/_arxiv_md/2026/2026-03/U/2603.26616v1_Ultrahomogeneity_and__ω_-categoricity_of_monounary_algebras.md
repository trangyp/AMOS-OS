Ultrahomogeneity and ω-categoricity of
monounary algebras
Thomas Quinn-Gregson
March 30, 2026
Abstract
Ultrahomogeneity and ω-categoricity are two central concepts arising
from model theory, with strong connections with oligomorphic permuta-
tion groups and quantifier elimination. In particular, both are conditions
on the automorphism group of a structure.
The aim of this paper is to describe both the ω-categorical monounary
algebras and the ultrahomogeneous monounary algebras of arbitrary car-
dinalities. We show that a monounary algebra is ω-categorical [ultraho-
mogeneous] if and only if every element has finite height and Aut(A) has
only finitely many 1-orbits [A is 1-ultrahomogeneous]. Our classification
of ultrahomogeneous monounary algebras is then viewed in the context
of previously studied variants of ultrahomogeneity, including (partial)-
homogeneity and transitivity.
Keywords: Monounary algebra, ultrahomogeneity, homogeneity, partial homo-
geneity, ω-categoricity
Subject Classification 2020: 08A60, 03C35, 03C10

Introduction
A monounary algebra is a set together with a single unary function. Their transparent
structure has allowed for a complete description of those monounary algebras which
satisfy certain properties which arise in model theory. Examples include transitiv-
ity [18], homomorphism-homogeneity [21], and polymorphism-homogeneity [12]; these
are conditions on the automorphism group, the endomorphism monoid, and the poly-
morphism clone of a monounary algebra, respectively. Such a complete picture is rare
amongst structures, and indeed no such classifications exist even for classical structures
such as graphs, groups, or partially ordered sets. Spurred by this, we consider two
of the most widely studied model theoretic properties of this form; ultrahomogeneity
and ω-categoricity.
A (first order) structure M is called n-ultrahomogeneous if every isomorphism
between n-generated substructures of M extends to an automorphism of M, and is
ultrahomogeneous1 if it is n-ultrahomogeneous for each n ∈N. For several key classes
of relational structures, ultrahomogeneity proves to be a strong enough condition to

[22], digraphs [6], and partially ordered sets [31]. For algebras the problem appears
far more difficult in general, and indeed Saracino and Wood [30] showed the existence
of 2ω ultrahomogeneous countable groups.
Despite this, ultrahomogeneous abelian

(see, e.g., [7, 9]) and for semilattices and semigroups (see, e.g., [11, 25, 26]).
Our second fundamental property is ω-categoricity, where a countable structure M
is called ω-categorical if it can be defined uniquely, up to isomorphism, by its first order
properties. The much celebrated Ryll-Nardzewski Theorem states that ω-categoricity
is equivalent to the purely algebraic condition that the automorphism group Aut(M)
is oligomorphic. That is, for each n ≥1 the coordinate-wise action of Aut(M) on M n
has only finitely many orbits. However, ω-categoricity is often too weak to allow for
classifications, with the exception of more simplistic structures such as abelian groups
[29] and linear orders [28]. For non-abelian groups, it follows from the work in [30] that
there exists 2ω ω-categorical groups, and a meaningful classification of ω-categorical
groups thus seems unlikely. Nevertheless, advances in the ω-categoricity of algebras
such as groups, rings, and semigroups continue to be made (see, e.g., [1, 2, 27]).
The main results of this article is a classification of both the ultrahomogeneous
and the ω-categorical monounary algebras. The former result answers in far greater
generality the first half of Problem 1 in [32], which asks for all countable locally finite
ultrahomogeneous monounary algebras (here stated in the language of Fra¨ıss´e limits).
We will show that much of what determines whether a monounary algebra A is
ultrahomogeneous or ω-categorical is whether it has the corresponding pointwise prop-
erty, i.e., is 1-ultrahomogeneous or Aut(A) has finitely many orbits on M, respectively.
Our results on ultrahomogeneity will allow for further classifications of other similar
conditions on the automorphism group of a monounary algebra. Namely, we describe
all [partial] homogeneous monounary algebras, that is, ones in which isomorphisms
between finite [partial] subalgebras extend to an automorphisms. Moreover, we show
the following pleasing connection in greater generality in Theorem 4.15:
Corollary. For the class of monounary algebras we have
Transitive automorphism group ⇒partial homogeneous ⇒ultrahomogeneous
⇒homogeneous.
This paper is ordered as follows.
In Sections 2 and 3 we give a self-contained
introduction to model theory and monounary algebras, respectively. In Section 4 we
produce a classification of ultrahomogeneous monounary algebras of arbitrary cardi-
nalities. In the context of monounary algebras, a strong link is then shown between
ultrahomogeneity and similar conditions on an automorphism group, namely n-partial
homogeneity, n-homogeneity, and transitivity. This is then used to classify the ul-
trahomogeneity of directed pseudoforests. We begin Section 5 by considering locally
finite and uniformly locally finite (ULF) monounary algebras. By the Ryll-Nardzewski
Theorem these properties are weaker than that of ω-categoricity. By forming induced
semilinear orders from a monounary algebra, where ω-categoricity is known, we are
then able to describe all ω-categorical monounary algebras. Recent results on monadic
stability are then used to show that there exists only countably many ω-categorical
(mono)unary algebras. In Section 6 we discuss the limits of generalizing our results to
unary algebras.

Model theory preliminaries
In this section we give an introduction to the required model theoretic notions, and
refer the reader to [16] for a detailed study.
2.1
First order structures
A signature τ is a set of relation symbols and function symbols, where each symbol
is associated with a natural number, called its arity.
A τ-structure M is a tuple
(M; (XM)X∈τ) where M is a set (call the domain) and XM ⊆M n if X is a relation
symbol of arity n, and XM : M n →M if X is a function of arity n; in the latter
case we allow n = 0 to model constant symbols. When no confusion may arise we
use the same symbol for a relation and its relation symbol, and likewise for functions.
Unless stated otherwise, we will assume that the structures A, B, C, . . . have domains
A, B, C, . . . .
Definition 2.1. Let M and N be a pair of τ-structures. A bijection g : M →N is
called an isomorphism if
• (x1, . . . , xn) ∈RM ⇔(g(x1), . . . , g(xn)) ∈RN for every relation symbol R ∈τ, and
• g(f M(x1, . . . , xn)) = f N (g(x1), . . . , g(xn)) for every function symbol f ∈τ.
If, further, M = N then g is called an automorphism. The group of automorphisms
of M is denoted by Aut(M).
A (binary) relational structure is one in which its signature has only (binary)
relation symbols.
2.2
Permutation groups and orbit growth
For a set X, we write Sym(X) for the group of all permutations of X. Let G be a
permutation group on a set X, that is, a subgroup of Sym(X). An orbit of G is a set
of the form {g(x) : g ∈G} for some x ∈X. For each n ∈N there is a natural action
of G on Xn, where g(x1, . . . , xn) = (g(x1), . . . , g(xn)); we define an n-orbit of G to be
an orbit of this action. We let on(G) denotes the number of n-orbits of G.
Definition 2.2. Let G be a permutation group on a set X.
• If on(G) is finite for each n ∈N then we call G oligomorphic.
• If o1(G) = 1, that is, if for every x, y ∈X there exists g ∈G with g(x) = y, then
we call G transitive.
Given a structure M, we let on(M) := on(Aut(M)), and by an n-orbit of M we
will mean an n-orbit of the action of Aut(M) on M. Similarly, we call M transitive
if Aut(M) is.
2.3
ω-categoricity and definability
In the following, let M be a τ-structure. Given a (first order) τ-sentence ϕ, we write
M |= ϕ if ϕ is true in M; the set of all τ-sentences true in M is called the theory of
M, denoted Th(M).

Definition 2.3. A countable structure M is called ω-categorical if Th(M) uniquely
defines M up to isomorphism, that is, if every countable structure with the same
theory as M is isomorphic to M.
Classical examples of ω-categorical structures include the dense linear order with-
out endpoints (Q; <) and the random graph; algebraic examples are discussed in Sub-
section 5.1.
Several equivalent characterisations of ω-categoricity were independently shown
by Engeler, Ryll-Nardzewski, and Svenonius, although commonly referred to as the
Ryll-Nardzewski Theorem (see, e.g., [16]). Of particular importance here is the link
to oligomorphicity:
Theorem 2.4 (Ryll-Nardzewski Theorem (RNT)). Let M be a countable structure.
Then M is ω-categorical if and only if Aut(M) is oligomorphic.
As a consequence, an ω-categorical structure is locally finite, that is, is such that
every finitely generated substructure is finite. Oligomorphicity also forces a bound on
the sizes of these substructures (a proof of the corollary below can be found in [16]).
Definition 2.5. A structure M is called uniformly locally finite (ULF) if there is a
function f : ω →ω such that, for any n ∈ω, each substructure of M generated by n
elements has at most f(n) elements.
Corollary 2.6. An ω-categorical structure is ULF.
A relation R ⊆M n is said to be definable (in M) if there is a τ-formula ϕ(x)
such that a ∈R ⇔M |= ϕ(a). A function g : M n →M is definable if its hypergraph
Rg ⊆M n+1 is definable, where
Rg = {(x1, . . . , xn, g(x1, . . . , xn))}.
Let τ ⊆σ be signatures and let M be a τ-structure and N be a σ-structure over
the same domain.
If RM = RN for each relation R ∈τ and f M = f N for each
function f ∈τ then M is called a reduct of N, and N is an expansion of M. An
expansion N of M is called first-order if every relation and function in σ\τ is definable
in M. A reduct of a first-order expansion of M is called a first-order reduct. A pair
of structures which are first-order reducts of each other are called interdefinable.
Lemma 2.7. Let M be an ω-categorical structure and let N be a structure with the
same domain as M. Then N is a first-order reduct of M if and only if Aut(N) ⊇
Aut(M).
In particular, every first-order reduct of an ω-categorical structure is ω-
categorical.
Consequently, a pair of ω-categorical interdefinable structures have the same au-
tomorphism groups.
2.4
Ultrahomogeneity
Definition 2.8. A structure M is called n-ultrahomogeneous if every isomorphism
between n-generated substructures of M extends to an automorphism of M, and is
ultrahomogeneous if it is n-ultrahomogeneous for each n ∈N.

Countable ultrahomogeneous relational structures with finite signatures are neces-
sarily ω-categorical (and thus so too are first-order reducts of such structures), while
the converse needs not hold in general.
An alternative description of countable ultrahomogeneous structures was given
by Fra¨ıss´e [13], originally only for linear orders and then extended to all countable
signatures. Let C be a class of finitely generated τ-structures for some signature τ.
We say that C has the
• hereditary property (HP) if it is closed under substructures;
• joint embedding property (JEP) if every pair A, B ∈C embeds into some C ∈C;
• amalgamation property (AP) if whenever A, B1, B2 ∈C and fi : A →Bi is an em-
bedding (i = 1, 2), then there exists C ∈C and embeddings gi : Bi →C such that
g1 ◦f1(a) = g2 ◦f2(a) for each a ∈A.
The age of a structure M, denoted age(M), is the class of all finitely generated
structures which embed in M.
Theorem 2.9 (Fra¨ıss´e’s Theorem). Let τ be a countable signature and let C be a non-
empty class of finitely generated τ-structures which is countable, up to isomorphism,
and has the HP, JEP, and AP. Then there is a unique countable τ-structure M which
is ultrahomogeneous and with age(M)= C; we call M the Fra¨ıss´e limit of C.

Monounary algebras preliminaries
A partial monounary algebra is a pair A = (A; θ), where A is a nonempty set and θ
a partial unary operation on A; we let dom(θ) denote the domain of θ. If dom(θ)=A,
then A is a monounary algebra. More generally, a unary algebra is a set together with
a finite collection of unary operations.
Each non-empty subset B of a monounary algebra A forms a partial monounary
algebra B = (B; θB), where dom(θB)={b ∈B : θ(b) ∈B}, and θB(b) = θ(b) if b ∈
dom(θB); we call B a partial subalgebra of A. If, further, B forms a monounary algebra
then it is called a subalgebra. We call A connected if for every x, y ∈A there exists
n, m ∈ω with θn(x) = θm(y).
A maximal connected subalgebra of A is called a
connected component, and the set of connected components of A is denoted by C(A).

otherwise. The set of all cyclic elements of A is denoted cyc(A) and forms a subalgebra.
A cyclic algebra is a connected monounary algebra in which every element is cyclic.
For each n ∈N, there exists a cyclic algebra of size n (also called an n-cycle), which
is unique up to isomorphism and denoted by Zn = ({0, 1, . . . , n −1}; Suc), where
Suc(i) = i + 1 mod n.
The height of an element x ∈A, denoted ht(x), is the least n ∈ω such that θn(x)

is the least n ∈ω such that ht(x) ≤n for every x ∈A; if no such n exists then we
write ht(A) = ∞.

A path is a sequence of mutually distinct elements x1, x2, . . . of A such that θ(xi) =
xi+1 (i ≥1). An anti-path is a sequence of mutually distinct elements x1, x2, . . . of A
such that θ(xi+1) = xi (i ≥1). An element a ∈A is called a leaf if θ−1(a) = ∅.
Lemma 3.2. Let A = (A; θ) be a monounary algebra in which every anti-path is
finite, and let L ⊆A be the set of leaves of A. Let K be a transversal of the set of
cyclic connected components of A. Then L ∪K is a minimal generating set of A, and
every minimal generating set of A is obtained in this way.
Proof. Each minimal generating set of A is the disjoint union of minimal generating
sets of its connected components. Let B ∈C(A). If B is cyclic, then it is generated
by any of its elements. Assume instead that B is non-cyclic, so that its set of leaves
L′ is non-empty (as every anti-path in B is finite). Then for every b ∈B there exists
x ∈L′ and n ∈ω with θn(x) = b. Hence B is generated by L′. Since the leaves L′ are
precisely those elements which are not in the image of θ, it follows that any generating
set of B must contain L′. The result follows.
If A is finitely generated then every anti-path is finite, and thus we have that A is
generated by its finite set of leaves and a finite collection of cyclic elements. We call a
monounary algebra n-generated if it can be generated by n-elements. It follows by the
lemma above that any connected non-cyclic n-generated monounary algebra which is
not (n −1)-generated must have precisely n leaves.

The relational form
Definition 3.4. Given a partial monounary algebra A = (A; θ), its relational form is
the digraph A∗= (A; Rθ) where (x, y) ∈Rθ if and only if xθ = y.

Ultrahomogeneous monounary algebras
Several alternative notions of ultrahomogeneity have been investigated for monounary
algebras, and we describe several here. A structure M is called n-homogeneous for
some n ∈N if every isomorphism between substructures of M of size n extends
to an automorphism of M. For locally finite structures, being ultrahomogeneous is
equivalent to being n-homogeneous for each n. For arbitrary structures this needs
not hold, since structures without finite substructures are trivially n-homogeneous
for every n, but need not be ultrahomogeneous.
For example, N = (N; Suc) has
no finite substructures and is not ultrahomogeneous, since the isomorphism mapping
⟨2⟩= ({2, 3, . . . }; Suc) to ⟨1⟩= N cannot be extended to an automorphism of N.
The n-homogeneity of monounary algebras is classified in [17] for n = 1 and in
[19] for n = 2. This property is strengthened in [17] and [20], where isomorphisms
are taken between partial subalgebras of some fixed size n (called here “partially-n-
homogeneous”); we discuss this in more detail in the subsequent subsection. Transitive
monounary algebras were classified in [18] (called here “homogeneous”). For each car-
dinal α > 0 there exists a unique, up to isomorphism, transitive connected monounary
algebra with |θ−1(x)| = α for every element x. We follow their notation by denoting
the resulting monounary algebra by Bα, and refer to Notation 2.2 of their paper for a
construction.
Finally, homomorphism-homogeneous and polymorphism-homogeneous monounary
algebras are described in [21] and [12], respectively.
While ultrahomogeneity is a
property on the group of automorphisms, these are the corresponding properties on
the endomorphism monoid and polymorphism clone, respectively.
Despite these breakthroughs, the ultrahomogeneity of monounary algebras has
largely been unstudied. One exception is [32], which considers Ramsey properties of
unary algebras and gives a countable list of Fra¨ıss´e classes of monounary algebras; the
class of all finite monounary algebras F and, for each k ≥1, the class Fk of all finite
monounary algebras (A; θ) in which |θ−1(a)| ≤k for each a ∈A. In the first half of
Problem 1 of their paper, they ask for all Fra¨ıss´e subclasses of F or, alternatively, all
countable locally finite ultrahomogeneous monounary algebras.
In this section we answer their problem in greater generality by describing all ultra-
homogeneous monounary algebras of arbitrary cardinality. Our first main result is the
following simple consequence of Corollary 3.11, which shows that 1-ultrahomogeneity
is sufficient for ultrahomogeneity.
This result has precedent; in [21], the proofs of
Lemma 2 and the forward direction to Lemma 4 requires only that any homomor-
phism between 1-generated subalgebras extends to an endomorphism.
It is then a
simple exercise to show that every 1-homomorphism-homogeneous monounary algebra
is homomorphism-homogeneous.

Homogeneity
The 1- and 2-homogeneity of monounary algebras were described in [17, 19].
Theorem 4.13. A monounary algebra A is homogeneous if and only if there exists
a cycle-free monounary algebra A1 and a locally finite ultrahomogeneous monounary

Proof. Every monounary algebra A can be written as A1 + A2, where A1 is the sub-

those appearing in Theorem 4.10 with β = 0.

Lemma 5.4. Let A be an ω-categorical monounary algebra. Suppose B is a partial

5.1
The number of ω-categorical unary algebras
In [15] Henson constructed uncountably many pairwise non-isomorphic ω-categorical
ultrahomogeneous digraphs. The corresponding result was also shown for groups [30],
while Rosenstein [29] showed that there exists only countably many ω-categorical
abelian groups, and in [28] only countably many ω-categorical linear orders, up to
isomorphism. The main result of this section is to prove the following:
Theorem 5.12. The class of ω-categorical unary algebras is countable, up to isomor-
phism.
For monounary algebras, a proof of the theorem above may be achieved via our
classification of ω-categorical monounary algebras. To show that this extends to all
unary algebras we shall make use of the recent work of Bodor et al. on strong forms
of stability and finitely bounded ultrahomogeneous structures. We do not require a
formal definition of (monadic) stability, but refer the reader to [24].
The following result is given in far greater generality in [24] (see the remark after
Corollary 10):

6
Towards unary algebras
Given the success in determining the ultrahomogeneity and ω-categoricity of mo-
nounary algebras, it seems reasonable to attempt to extend to unary algebras.
One obvious first step is to ask to what extend does the ω-categoricity of the
monounary reducts of a unary algebra A dictate the ω-categoricity of A?
If A =
(A; θ1, . . . , θn) is ω-categorical then it follows from the RNT that each (A; θi) is ω-
categorical, since Aut(A) ⊆Aut(A; θi). Unfortunately, the example below shows that
the converse needs not hold, even for commutative unary algebras, i.e., those in which
θiθj = θjθi for each i, j ≤n.
Example 6.1. Let A0 = {c, ai : i ∈N} and A = A0 ∪B for some countably infinite set
B disjoint to A0. We define a pair of unary functions θ1 and θ2 on A such that each
(A; θi) is isomorphic to the ω-categorical monounary algebra A[1; ω, ω] as follows. Let
{Xi : i ∈N} and {Yi : i ∈N} be a pair of partitions of B such that |Xi| = |Yi| = ℵ0
and |Xi ∩Yi| = i for each i ∈N. Let θk(A0) = c (k = 1, 2) and θ1(Xi) = θ2(Yi) = ai
for each i ∈N. Let A = (A; θ1, θ2), noting that A is commutative as
θ1θ2(A) = θ1(A0) = c = θ2(A0) = θ2θ1(A).
