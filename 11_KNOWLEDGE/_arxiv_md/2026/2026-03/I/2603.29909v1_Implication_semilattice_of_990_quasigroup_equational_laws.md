Abstract. In his quest to disprove a claim by Peirce that all lattices are
distributive, Ernst Schröder considered 135 years ago a list of 990 equational
laws on quasigroups, analogous to associativity, such as (x//y) ∗z = (y//x)\\z.
A quasigroup is a non-associative analogue of groups, specifically a set equipped
with multiplication and right/left conjugate-division operations that are com-
patible. Each equation of interest identifies two three-variable expressions
built from these operations. I determine all 114 equivalence classes of their
conjunctions, and all implications between them. This includes as a small
corner the five-element non-distributive lattice identified by Schröder.

1.1. Equational laws. Schröder considered equational laws of the form
(1)
∀x, ∀y, ∀z,
x?y?z = α?β?γ
where ? are replaced (independently) by any of the three quasigroup operations,
α, β, γ are the variables x, y, z in some order, and the operations are associated
either as x?(y?z) or (x?y)?z independently on both sides.
Some subsets of these 990 equations are closed under logical implications (in
short good subsets). One can define a lattice structure by introducing two binary
operations on pairs of such good subsets A and B.
• The logical sum A + B, which contains the set-theoretic union A ∪B, is the
set of all consequences of A ∧B among the 990 laws.
• The product AB is the set-theoretic intersection A ∩B.
Another operation that can be derived from these is implication: for good subsets,
A =⇒B if B ⊂A; equivalently A + B = A and equivalently AB = B.
Schröder studied a few such subsets: the set A1 of 16 consequences of associativity,
the set C1 of 30 consequences of commutativity of ∗, the set O1 = A1 + C1 of
150 consequences of associativity and commutativity, the set C00 of 18 consequences
of x ∗(y ∗z) = x//(y//z), and he showed that
(2)
A1C00 + C1C00 = ∅,
(A1 + C1)C00 ̸= ∅,
so the distributive law fails in the lattice.
1.2. Quasigroup varieties. Any set A of equational laws defines a variety V (A)
consisting of quasigroups2 satisfying every law in A. The quasigroups automatically
satisfy any logical consequence of the laws in A. This makes the logical sum operation
A + B very natural, as any quasigroup satisfying the laws in A and in B satisfies all
those in A + B, and conversely. While any quasigroup satisfying all laws in A or
all laws in B satisfies those in AB, the converse is not true, as AB may be a very
meager set of laws. Thus,
(3)
V (A + B) = V (A) ∩V (B),
V (AB) ⊃V (A) ∪V (B).
In most of the text I mostly focus on the + operation and correspondingly use the
terminology semilattice.
For instance,
• V (∅) is the variety of all quasigroups;
• V (A1) is the variety of groups (associative quasigroups);
• V (C1) is the variety of commutative quasigroups;
• V (O1) is the variety of Abelian groups;

1.3. Main result. The viewpoint of prescribing a lattice as some given set equipped
with suitable operations given by explicit tables was only developed later on by
Dedekind. Schröder’s five-element lattice is quite trivial to describe in retrospect,
and does not require any reference to quasigroups. Even though the original lattice
motivation is gone, the question of exploring varieties of quasigroups remains, and I
completed the unfinished quest.
There are 114 quasigroup varieties described by subsets of the 990 Schröder
equations, listed in section 3. It is interesting to distinguish them according to the
minimal number of equations needed to characterized such quasigroups.
• 1 is the variety V (∅) of all quasigroups;
• 47 can be described by a single equation, see subsection 3.1;
• 50 can be described by two equations (but not one), see subsection 3.2;
• 15 can be described by three equations (but not two), see subsection 3.3;
• 1 needs at least four equations, see subsection 3.4.
Some of the varieties are easily described, such as the aforementioned varieties of
groups or commutative quasigroups, and some were identified as being interesting in
the context of the Equational Theories Project. It would be interesting to describe
all of these varieties in more conventional mathematical terms. The only variety
that requires four equations may be of particular interest; it can be described (for
instance) by
(4)
x ∗(y//z) = y ∗(x//z),
x ∗(y\\z) = z ∗(y\\x)
x//(y\\z) = y//(x\\z),
x\\(y ∗z) = y\\(x ∗z).
The main outcome is presented as a list of the good subsets of equations, given
in quasigroup_long_classes.json. Given two good subsets A and B, the logical
operations of interest are determined as follows:
• A + B is the smallest good subset containing both A and B;
• AB is the intersection of A and B, which is automatically good;
• A =⇒B if and only if B ⊂A.
Given a (not necessarily good) subset of the 990 equations, the closure under logical
implications is the smallest good subset (as listed in the data file) that contains all
of these equations.

2. Background, methods and results
2.1. Quasigroup equations. A quasigroup is a set Q equipped with three binary
operations ∗, //, \\: Q × Q →Q such that for all x, y ∈Q one has
(5)
(y//x) ∗y = x,
y//(x ∗y) = x,
x ∗(y\\x) = y,
(x ∗y)\\x = y,
x\\(y//x) = y,
(x\\y)//x = y.
Alternatively, the quasigroup Q can be described as being equipped with a single
binary operation ∗whose right and left multiplications are bijective. Inverting these
maps defines right and left division operations / and \ characterized by (x/y)∗y = x
and y ∗(y\x) = x. It is more convenient to work with their conjugates x//y = y/x
and x\\y = y\x. Indeed, the resulting definition of quasigroups (5) features a
symmetry under which one cyclically permutes the operations (∗, //, \\).
2.1.1. Numbering and counting Schröder’s laws. Since the original work did not
appear to specify an ordering of the equations, I decided arbitrarily on a numbering.
Namely, as can be seen in the data file quasigroup-equations.txt, equations are
ordered
• first by how the two binary operations on the left and right are associated,
namely x?(y?z) = α?(β?γ) before x?(y?z) = (α?β)?γ before (x?y)?z =
(α?β)?γ;
• then by the choice of the four operations ? in (1) in lexicographical order,
namely (∗, ∗, ∗, ∗), then (∗, ∗, ∗, //), then (∗, ∗, ∗, \\), then (∗, ∗, //, ∗), etc.;
• then by the choice of variables.
Following Schröder, and following what we did in the Equational Theories Project [1],
all tautological equations are omitted, as are any equation that can be obtained
from earlier ones by relabelling variables or swapping left and right hand side. This
explains the absence of any equation associated as (x?y)?z = α?(β?γ).
The left-hand side always uses the variables x, y, z in this order, while they can
appear in any of the six orders on the right-hand side, unless both sides involve
the same operations associated in the same way, in which case one may not take
(α, β, γ) to be (x, y, z) in the same order (tautology) nor (z, x, y) because this is
equivalent to the choice (y, z, x).
This gives 6 × 9 × 9 laws associated as x, (y, z) = (?, ?), ?, 6 × (9 × 8/2) of the
form x, (y, z) =?, (?, ?) with different pairs of operations on the two sides and 4 × 9

2.2. The Equational Theories Project. The Equational Theories Project [1]
considered magmas, which are sets equipped with one binary operation, and the
4694 simplest equational laws, that is, all laws that involve at most 4 operations.
The collaborative research project determined all implications between these laws.3
Automated theorem provers were particularly effective tools to obtain positive
implications: in all cases where an equation E implies another equation E′, a
short proof could be found automatically. For counterexamples, the situation was
somewhat trickier: small countermodels could be obtained directly by automated
tools, but larger finite countermodels could only be found by imposing well-chosen
assumptions to restrict the search. Even worse, some implications turned out to
hold for all finite magmas but not for infinite magmas. To disprove such implications
it was necessary to build infinite countermodels. Thus, overall, the Equational
Theories Project relied on a collection of techniques:
• Automated theorem provers and SAT solvers such as Vampire, Prover9,
Mace4.
• A Lean formalization of every implication and every countermodel, to ensure
nothing was missed. This was absolutely crucial due to the large number of
collaborators and the tens of millions of implications to be considered.
• Mathematical developments, to come up with new constructions of magmas
based on magma cohomology, greedy constructions, and rewriting theory.
(As well as tools to organize the work, visualize the status of the project, explore
the implication graph, and much more.)
2.3. Methods. The main goal of this project is to determine all logically-closed
subsets A of equations among Schröder’s 990 equations. This relied on two automated
tools developed by McCune: the automated theorem prover (ATP) Prover9, which
given some equations and a goal seeks a proof thereof, and the model builder Mace4,
which given the same setup seeks a finite countermodel, namely (in our case) a
quasigroup that does not obey the target equation. This was combined with very
simple Python scripts.
For any given implication of interest between a set A of equations and a target E,
a useful strategy was as follows. (Here we denote by Q the quasigroup axioms,
see subsection 2.1.) Seek countermodels to Q ∧A =⇒E using Mace4 with a
very small bound in size. Check if the implication can be proven using Prover9,
with a one-second timeout. Finally make a longer attempt at counterexamples
using Mace4.4 This always succeeds for the implications of interest, hence serves as
an oracle for the truth of implications. This is a crucial simplification compared
to the Equational Theories Project: all of the non-implications admitted a finite
counterexample, which could be found automatically.

The strategy to find all inequivalent conjunctions of equations is thus as follows.
• For each pair of equation E, E′ (among the 990), determine whether Q ∧
E =⇒E′.
• Deduce the 47 lowest-numbered representatives of equivalence classes of
equations, which are listed in subsection 3.1. The consequences of a given
equation form a logically closed subset.
• For each logically closed subset A found so far, in turn, and each of the 47
representatives E that is not in A, consider A ∪{E}. Seek all implications
of the form Q ∧A ∧E =⇒E′ for E′ among the 47 representatives. Many
of these searches are skipped thanks to transitivity of implication. If the
resulting set of consequences is new, add it to the list of logically closed
subsets to consider.
One should remember as well that the empty set is logically closed!
