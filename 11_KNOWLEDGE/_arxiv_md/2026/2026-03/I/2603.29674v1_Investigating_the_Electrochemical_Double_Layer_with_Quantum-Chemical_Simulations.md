Abstract
We assess the dielectrically consistent reference interaction site model (DRISM) as
an implicit electrolyte framework for modeling the electrochemical double layer, and
compare it with the Poisson–Boltzmann model and explicit molecular dynamics results
from the literature. We use the gold–electrolyte interface as the main test case and
analyze solvent and ionic density profiles, the differential capacitance, and the solvation
contribution to CO adsorption. The results show a strong sensitivity to the Lennard-
Jones parametrization of metal–ion and metal–water interactions. In particular, we find
that the default Lorentz–Berthelot mixing rules to be inadequate and lead to excessive
Na+ accumulation at the interface, which results in an increase of the differential
capacitance at negative electrode potentials. We demonstrate that introducing pair-
specific metal–ion parameters yields more symmetric charging behavior and provides
greater flexibility.
Our findings suggest that using pair-specific parameters, rather

Introduction
The structure and properties of the electrical double layer (EDL) at charged interfaces play
a central role in electrochemistry. From energy storage in supercapacitors to electrocatalysis
and corrosion, the interfacial region controls ion distributions, potential profiles, and ulti-
mately the rates and selectivity of electrochemical processes.1–3 A quantitative understanding
of the EDL is therefore essential for the design of efficient electrochemical systems.
Atomistic simulations of interfacial phenomena must adequately model the intricate in-
terplay among solvent, ions, and charged surfaces. The most common theoretical framework
for EDL is the Poisson–Boltzmann (PB) model originally developed by Gouy4 and Chap-
man5 and later by Stern.6 Its mean-field formulation, in which ions are treated as point
charges in a continuum dielectric, allows for simple, computationally inexpensive evaluation
of ion density profiles and potential distributions. Thanks to its simplicity, the PB model
(and related models7) is, even today, one of the most common approaches, and it is imple-
mented in various well-known electronic structure software packages.8–12 Nevertheless, its
approximations neglect molecular granularity and correlation effects, which become critical
at high ionic strength or in the immediate vicinity of electrodes.
A much more detailed picture can be obtained from first-principles methods like ab ini-
tio molecular dynamics (AIMD), where the electrolyte is modeled explicitly at the density
functional theory (DFT) level.13–16 AIMD is, in principle, providing the most accurate de-
scription; however, it is computationally very demanding and therefore limits the size of
the simulation box and the time scale of the simulation. To reduce the computational cost,
the electrolyte is often treated classically in a quantum mechanics (QM)/molecular me-
chanics (MM) framework.3 But even with this substantially decreased computational cost,
2


## Page 3


sufficient sampling is challenging since long equilibration times and large simulation cells are
usually required.17 This becomes especially challenging in constant-potential simulations,
where specialized algorithms to control the electrode potential while maintaining charge
neutrality need to be applied.17
An alternative approach is provided by treating the electrolyte with the reference in-
teraction site model (RISM). RISM, first introduced by Chandler and Andersen 18, is an
approximation to solve the molecular Ornstein–Zernike equation and obtain the site-site ra-
dial distributions functions for the given system; it has been extended to molecular fluids
with charged sites by Hirata and Rossky 19 also indicated as extended RISM (XRISM) and
then further developed for electrolytes by Perkyns,20,21 who introduced a dielectrically con-
sistent variant which apport a correction to accounts for the solvent polarizability. RISM
has been first embedded to QM calculations by Sato et al. 22 and further extended for elec-
trochemical simulations by Nishihara and Otani 23 and combined with the effective screening
medium (ESM),24 in short ESM-RISM, which effectively removes the periodicity in the direc-
tion perpendicular to the slab, enabling a non-repeated vacuum/slab/solvent configuration.
The QM/ESM-RISM framework has been employed in various studies25–28 and proved to
yield essential insights into the structure of the electrode–electrolyte interface. In two recent
works, ESM-RISM has been compared to the linearized PB model for studying of the CO
reduction reaction at the Cu(100) facet29 and with the charge-asymmetric nonlocally deter-
mined local electric response (CANDLE) model9 (which also belongs to the family of the
linearized PM models) for the studying of the standard hydrogen electrode.30 Both studies
report that ESM-RISM generally leads to destabilization of the adsorption energies within
the employed parametrizations. In the second study, Demeyere and Skylaris compared the
differential capacitance computed with CANDLE and ESM-RISM, noticing considerable dif-
ferences between the two models.
In this work, we extend the previous studies by further evaluating the capabilities of
QM/ESM-RISM and of the newly developed dielectrically consistent version dielectrically-
3


## Page 4


consistent RISM (DRISM),31 for the modeling of the electrode-electrolyte interface. In the
first part, we focus on the solvent and ionic density profiles at an Au(111) facet. In the
second part, we study the differential capacitance and its dependence on the electrolyte
parametrization, compositions, and concentrations, since this quantity can be compared
rather directly to experimental studies. Finally, we test the impact of the parametrization
for the adsorption energy of CO at the Au surface. We systematically compare the DRISM
results with the recently implemented non-linear PB model VASPsol++.32

Results and Discussion
2.1
Density Profiles
The most direct outputs of a QM/DRISM calculation are the classical particle density dis-
tributions for the implicit DRISM electrolyte around the explicit QM system. The solvent
and ionic density profiles are the x- and y-averaged particle densities as a function of the slab
distance z. Since these profiles cannot be directly measured experimentally, computational
models such as DRISM are valuable tools for elucidating the structure of the EDL.
2.1.1
Solvent density profiles
The structure of the metal–water interface has been extensively investigated.
For non-
absorbing metals like Au, water is known to form a layer in which the water molecules lie
parallel to the surface.13,33,34 For adsorbing metals like Pt, the peak for the oxygen appears
split, due to its specific adsorption on the surface.13 As already reported by Demeyere and
Skylaris 30, QM/RISM is unable to predict the double-peak structure, given the lack of
specific chemical bonding interactions in the metal–water potential, and we would expect
the same deficiency in a fully classical molecular dynamics (MD) or a QM/MM simulation,
where the electrolyte is modeled classically.

treatments of metal polarization.
We now turn to the comparison between DRISM and the PB model. In Fig. 2, we show a
side-by-side comparison of the two models for both negative σ = −8.3 µC/cm−2 and positive
σ = +8.3 µC/cm−2 surface electrode charge, which correspond to a negative and positive
applied potential relative to the potential of zero charge (PZC).
In the PB model, the solvent is modeled as a dielectric continuum, as shown in panel
(b). In the presence of the solute, the dielectric medium generates a polarization field P(r);
the bound (or polarization) charge is then defined as ρb(r) = −∇P(r). As we can see, while
the charge profile predicted by DRISM exhibits complex oscillatory behavior, the bound
charge obtained from the PB model shows mainly one peak situated at the boundary of the
dielectric cavity, followed by an exponential peak due to explicit ionic charge screening.

Additionally, we observe that the cation peak is located between 2 and 3 ˚A from the
metal surface, i.e., within the same region as the oxygen peak of the first water layer. This
indicates that the cations are found directly at the inner Helmholtz plane (IHP). The cation
accumulation becomes more pronounced at negative electrode charge and we attribute this
as the cause of the high differential capacitance, as discussed in Sec. 2.2.
This tendency for Na+ to accumulate directly at the surface is not specific to DRISM
but is also observed in explicit molecular dynamics simulations. In a recent experimental
and theoretical work from Alfarano et al. 34, it was found that Na+ and Cl−behave asym-
metrically at the Au-water interface. Under negative bias, Na+ enters the IHP already at
very small potentials and partially loses one hydration water, not because of strong Na+-Au
bonding, but because the applied negative field disrupts the interfacial 2D hydrogen-bond
network, making insertion energetically favorable. In contrast, under positive bias, Cl−re-
mains fully hydrated in the outer Helmholtz plane (OHP) over a broad potential range and
only desolvates at high positive potentials.
Given the approximations on which DRISM is based, we cannot expect it to capture
the exact interfacial 2D hydrogen-bond network and its disruption under applied potential.
However, we still consider the ionic density profiles obtained from explicit MDs as a useful
benchmark for the model. In Fig. 4, we compare the density profiles obtained from a classical
MD simulation of the Au(111) interface in contact with 1 M NaCl at the PZC by Ntim and
Sulpizi 43 with the corresponding DRISM results.
As shown in panel (a), the position of the first Na+ peak agrees well between the DRISM
and MD results. Specific cation adsorption has also been observed by Serva et al. 44 for
the Au(100) facet, where the adsorption strength was found to increase with the metallic
character of the electrode. Interestingly, for the anion (panel (b)), we observe the main
density peak at ≈6 ˚A, in correspondence with the second water layer, and a minor peak at
≈3.5 ˚A. The positions of both peaks are in qualitative agreement with the MD simulation.

From the classical theory of the electrical double-layer,45 however, small cations such as
Li+ and Na+ are known to typically retain their solvation shell and reside at the OHP, rather
than populating the IHP, directly at the surface.
One potential limitation of the QM/RISM implementation in Quantum Espresso is the
use of LB mixing rules to construct the cross pair-potential parameters.23 In the GolP-
CHARMM force field,46 which is specifically parameterized for the Au-water interface, the
mixing rules are applied only to generic species, whereas the metal-water interactions are
treated with pair-specific parameters fitted to reproduce DFT adsorption energies of a single
water molecule.
In the classical MD studies of Hughes and Walsh 35 and Park and Mc-
Daniel 37, both employing GolP-CHARMM, no cation accumulation directly at the interface
is observed. In another recent work from Shin et al. 3, for an Ag(111) electrode, Na+ does
not exhibit specific adsorption at cathodic potentials but remains solvated in the OHP,

preserving its first hydration shell (coordination number ≈6) and avoiding direct contact
with the Ag(111) surface. In this case, the metal-electrolyte interaction was modeled using
first-principles-derived Buckingham-type van der Waals potentials for Ag-water and Ag-ion
interactions. We also note that an extensive study by Berg et al. 47 concluded that the LJ
pair potential itself is insufficient for the Au-water interaction, and that alternative potentials
such as the Morse or Buckingham potentials are more suitable options. Considering the dif-
ferent observations and conclusions of the studies discussed above, the microscopic structure
of the electrical double layer at metal-aqueous interfaces seems still not fully understood,
and several details remain open for debate.
It is therefore not straightforward to establish a unique benchmark for the DRISM results.
For this reason, we proceed by examining the EDL structure predicted by DRISM, when
pair-specific parameters are used, instead of the default LB mixing rules.
In Fig. 5 (a) we compare the density profiles at the PZC and for a small electrode surface
charge obtained at a lower electrolyte concentration of 0.01 M with the Au Heinz / mSPCE
NaCl Joung parametrization. For the segmented curves, the default LB mixing rules are
used and the sodium ion exhibits a defined peak within the IHP. This peak is also present
for a positive surface charge applied of σ = +0.27 µC/cm2, in panel (c); at positive electrode
charge we expect the electrostatic repulsion to dominate and we therefore consider this peak
as unphysical. By selectively decreasing the LJ interaction (see Fig. S2) between gold and
sodium (continuous line), we observe, as expected, the disappearance of the first sodium
peak.

2.2
Differential Capacitance
The differential capacitance quantifies how the surface charge responds to changes in the ap-
plied potential. It has been extensively studied and remains one of the primary experimental
probes of the structure of the electrical double layer.
In Fig. 6, we report the differential capacitance as a function of the electrode potential
relative to the PZC for the Au(111) surface in NaCl and HCl (H3O+Cl−) electrolytes at
1 M, using different parameters. For NaCl, we find that the differential capacitance increases
steeply at negative electrode potentials.
Experimentally, this steep increase in the differential capacitance is observed at posi-
tive electrode potentials and is ascribed to specifically adsorbed anions,49,50 however the
measurements are typically done between 0.01 M and 0.1 M concentration.

of Devanathan and Ramakrishnaiah 51, differential capacitance was measured at 1 M con-
centrations for different metals and different electrolytes; capacitance spikes at negative
electrode potentials were also not observed. Furthermore, for a non-adsorbing electrolyte,

When Na+ is used as a cation, the strong peak discussed in the previous section causes a
divergence in the computed differential capacitance at negative electrode potentials.
We therefore regard this excessively large differential as unphysical, and we trace it back
to the strong peak in the sodium density profile discussed in the previous section 2.1.2.
Notably, the Au UFF / mTIP3P / NaCl Jensen parametrization, which corresponds to the
weakest Au-Na+ LJ interaction (ε = 0.0044, kcal/mol), yields the largest capacitance. This
result highlights the important role of the Au-H2O interaction in preventing excessive accu-
mulation of Na+ at the interface. In the Au UFF / mTIP3P parametrization the Au-H2O
interaction is relatively weak, whereas it is stronger in the Au Heinz / mSPCE parametriza-
tion. We also tested the pair-specific Au-H2O LJ parametrization optimized by Berg et al. 47.
Although this model employs Au-O LJ parameters similar to those of the Heinz parametriza-
tion, it introduces a predominantly repulsive Au-H interaction. Nevertheless, the resulting
differential capacitance is largely unchanged. The Heinz and Berg parametrizations lead,
however, to different solvation contributions in the adsorption energy of CO, as discussed in

We mention that the increase of the capacitance at negative potentials was observed
in a previous study employing the QM/ESM-RISM framework, by Karnes et al. 52 for the
Cu(111) and Cu(100) facets in contact with the mTIP5P water and different electrolyte ion
species, where they show a clear asymmetry in the charging curves with respect to the PZC
(see their supplementary information, and Fig. S4 where we replot the charging curve and
the respective differential capacitance). In Fig. S3, we additionally show the differential
capacitance obtained using the LJ parameters of Haruyama et al. 25 for Pt, which were
fitted to reproduce the interaction energy between a Pt surface and a Xe atom from a DFT
calculation. Even with these parameters, we still observe pronounced cation accumulation
at the interface, leading to divergent differential capacitance values at negative electrode
potentials.
In contrast, when H3O+ is used as the cation in the DRISM electrolyte, this behavior is
not observed. In DRISM, Na+ is represented as a single-site classical particle with its positive
charge located at the ion center, which allows it to approach the electrode surface closely.
H3O+, instead, is modeled as a multi-site species with the positive charge distributed over
the three hydrogen atoms. As a result, the effective charge cannot approach the interface
as closely, leading to a smaller accumulation of positive charge at the electrode surface. In
agreement with Demeyere and Skylaris 30, we also observe fluctuations in the computed Fermi
level, particularly for simulations using the mTIP5P water model and the H3O+ cation and
we found the convergence significantly more difficult, requiring and a convergence threshold
for the 3D-RISM solver of 10−6 Ry instead of 10−7 Ry, as reported in the methods section 4.
Moreover, we report that when using the mTIP5P model and the H3O+ cation with DRISM,
some calculations did not converge. This behavior may be linked to the larger number of
interaction sites in both mTIP5P water and H3O+, which increases the complexity of the
site-site correlations.

3
Conclusion
In this work, we assessed the dielectrically consistent DRISM embedding for modeling the
EDL and compared its predictions with explicit MD data and continuum PB models. For the
gold–water interface, DRISM reproduces the main features of the solvent density profiles and
yields a qualitatively reasonable microscopic description of the interfacial liquid structure.
For ionic density profiles, the method also captures the position of the main peaks observed
in explicit MD simulations. Additionally, our results show that the predicted structure of
the compact layer depends strongly on the parametrization of the metal–ion interactions.
With the default LB mixing rules, Na+ can accumulate too strongly in the IHP, which
leads to divergent differential capacitance at negative electrode potentials for concentrated
NaCl electrolytes, a behavior that we regard as unphysical. We show that by introducing
pair-specific Au-Na+ parameters, this excessive cation accumulation is reduced, and this is
reflected in a more symmetric differential capacitance curve.
In the dilute limit, DRISM recovers the expected exponential decay of the diffuse charge
distribution, and charge profiles lie between the linear and non-linear PB model.
For CO adsorption, the solvation contribution depends strongly on the chosen metal–
water parameters, with differences up to 6 kcal/mol but the trends across different adsorption
sites are consistent across different parametrizations.
The dependence of the adsorption

of the electrode potential, where the asymmetry in the adsorption energy follows the same
behavior observed in the differential capacitance.
Also in this case we suggest that the
introduction of pair-specific parameters for the Au-Na+ interaction leads to a more symmetric
behavior of the adsorption energy with respect to the PZC, which we regard as more accurate.
Overall, our results demonstrate that the adoption of pair-specific parameters within the
QM/DRISMframework can improve the flexibility of the model. We believe that a systematic
optimization of the pair-wise parameters, could significantly enhance the accuracy of the
model and can be objective of future work.

Methods
We used the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package57 (VASP) with the VASPsol++32 Poisson–
Boltzmann (PB) model and Quantum ESPRESSO58,59 with the built-in implementation of
the reference interaction-site model (RISM).23,31 For the calculations with VASP, we builtin
PAW pseudopotentials while with Quantum ESPRESSO, we used the PAW pseudopoten-
tials from the PSlibrary.60 The initial atomic structures were generated using the Atomic
Simulation Environment (ASE).61
Density Profiles and Differential Capacitance For computing the density profiles
and the differential capacitance, we chosen a 1 × 1 × 6 slab model, with the default experi-
mental lattice parameters in ASE. To ensure that the cell size would not affect the results,
we compared the density profiles obtained from a slab with a larger surface 3 × 3. As shown
in Fig. S1, the profiles overlap well at both high and low electrolyte concentrations, proving
that a 1×1 surface cell is sufficient to investigate the EDL’s properties within the QM/ESM-
RISM framework. We employed the PBE density functional62 and Gaussian smearing with
a broadening parameter of 0.2 eV (≡0.0147 Ry). We employed a plane wave cutoff of 500 eV
(≡36 Ry, with an additional ×8 cutoff for the electron density in Quantum ESPRESSO).
23


## Page 24


For sampling the Brillouin zone, we used a 11 × 11 × 1 Γ-centered k-point grid. For the
VASP calculations, we used an energy threshold of 10−6 eV for the electronic energy, while
for Quantum ESPRESSO we set the threshold to 10−8 Ry. For RISM, two additional thresh-
olds must be defined: the first establishes the convergence of the 1D-RISM calculation, for
which we used a value of 10−8 Ry; the second is related to the 3D-RISM calculation, which
we set to 10−7 Ry. We used a tighter convergence than previously reported,25 since we found
the Fermi level to be very sensitive to the 3D-RISM convergence. We report, however, that
for the mTIP5P water model, it was not possible to reach the desired convergence, and
therefore, we increased the threshold to 10−6 Ry. Finally, for RISM, an additional plane
wave cutoff is required to expand the 3D particle densities; we used 300 Ry.
In both the implicit electrolyte models, the electrode potential is defined with respect to
the electric potential in the electrolyte bulk, according to the following expression:
Φel.de = ϕel.yte −¯µe
qe
(1)
Where ¯µe is the electrochemical potential of the electrons in the metal electrode, i.e., the
Fermi level, qe is the elementary charge, and ϕ is the electric potential in the electrolyte bulk
region.
The differential capacitance is computed by performing several calculations at different
surface charges, i.e., by changing the number of electrons in the system. The surface charge
is defined as the excess of electronic charge in the system divided by the surface area of the
cell. For the PB model implementation in VASP, since the electrolyte is present on both
sides of the cell, the surface area must be multiplied by two. The ESM-RISM implementation
in Quantum ESPRESSO allows for a non-periodic vacuum/slab/electrolyte setup; therefore,
the surface area is just the one in contact with the electrolyte. The resulting charging curve is
fitted with a cubic spline and differentiated to obtain the differential capacitance. A different
level of smoothing might be required, given that even small fluctuations in the computed

Fermi level lead to large discontinuities in the first derivative (as previously reported30).
Adsorption Energies For the adsorption energy we used the RPBE density functional63
along with the D3 dispersion correction (with “zero damping” function)64 following previous
studies13,65 on the gold electrode interface. We used the plane wave cutoff of 600 eV (≡
44 Ry, with an additional ×8 cutoff for the electron density in Quantum ESPRESSO). To
construct the slab we employed an optimized lattice constant for a 1 × 1 × 1 cubic cell
(with a 21 × 21 × 21 k-point mesh). The resulting lattice constants are 4.172 ˚A for VASP,
and 4.196 ˚A for Quantum ESPRESSO. The small difference can be ascribed to the different
pseudopotentials. The slab model consists of a 3 × 3 surface cell with four layers, where
the two bottom layers are fixed to the bulk positions and the two top layers are allowed to
relax. The Brillouin zone is sampled with a 3 × 3 × 1 Γ-centered k-point grid, and Gaussian
smearing with a broadening parameter of 0.1 eV (≡0.0073 Ry) is used. The force convergence
threshold for the geometric optimization is set to 0.01 eV/˚A for VASP and 0.002 Ry/Bohr
for Quantum ESPRESSO. As we highlight on the main text, for some of the Quantum
ESPRESSO calculations, denoted with the hollow markers in Fig. 10 (a), was not possible
to achieve the desired convergence of the forces, and therefore, we report the adsorption
energy computed from single point calculations, with the geometry optimized at the PZC.

of the solvent, in our case water, the radius of the dielectric cavity, the radius of the ionic
cavity, and the maximum ionic packing diameter. We used the default settings, as suggested
in the original implementation.32
Within the RISM formalism, the pairwise potentials among the interaction sites must be
provided. In the specific implementation by Nishihara and Otani 23, the pairwise potentials
are given in the form of Lennard-Jones 12–6 expression and the Lorentz–Berthelot combina-
tion rules are adopted σAB = σAA+σBB

case by case in the main text (to define pair-specific parameters we used our own modified
version of Quantum ESPRESSO, since at the present moment only the Lorentz–Berthelot
mixing rules are implemented in the official release).
For water, we use the SPCE,66 the TIP3P,67 and the TIP5P68 models, with the modifica-
tions adopted in.25 The modifications consist of additional LJ potentials for hydrogen atoms
and lone charge pairs in the TIP5P model. The repulsive part of the LJ potential is needed to
compute site-site particle correlations, which would otherwise diverge at short distances. To
highlight this difference from the original models, we denote them as “modified”: mSPCE,
mTIP3P, and mTIP5P. For the ions, we used the parameters optimized for the SPCE water
