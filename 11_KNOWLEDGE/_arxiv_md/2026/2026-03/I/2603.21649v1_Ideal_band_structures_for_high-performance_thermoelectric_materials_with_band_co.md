Abstract 
We investigate optimal band structures in band-converged systems to achieve high 𝑧𝑇 using 
numerical calculations based on a virtual spectral conductivity model. We consider a two parabolic 
band system, in which multiple band parameters can be independently controlled. Despite its 
simplicity, this model provides theoretical validation of empirical trends observed in 
thermoelectric materials. Our results provide a physically transparent set of design principles for 
band-structure engineering, offering quantitative design guidelines for the development of a wide 
range of thermoelectric materials. The main conclusions are as follows: (i) When a band does not 
cross the chemical potential and |𝜇−𝐸𝑒𝑑𝑔𝑒| > 5𝑘𝐵𝑇 , the contribution of the band to 𝑧𝑇 is 
negligibly small; (ii) To suppress the bipolar effect, a band gap 𝐸𝑔 satisfying 𝐸𝑔> 5𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑜𝑝 , 
where 𝑇𝑜𝑝 is the operating temperature, is required; (iii) In band-converged systems, the energy 
separation between the band edge Δ𝐸 should satisfy Δ𝐸~0 to maximize 𝑧𝑇 when interband 
scattering is insignificant; (iv) Achieving high spectral conductivity Σ (high band degeneracy 𝑁, 
density of states effective mass 𝑚𝐷𝑂𝑆
∗
, and relaxation time 𝜏) near the band edge is essential for 
achieving high 𝑧𝑇.

Introduction 
 
Thermoelectric materials enable the direct conversion of waste heat into electricity. Thermoelectric 
energy conversion devices are particularly attractive for applications requiring long operational 
lifetimes with minimal maintenance, such as those in space environments. Achieving high 
thermoelectric conversion efficiency requires large values of the figure of merit 𝑧𝑇, defined as 
𝑧𝑇= 𝜎𝑆2𝑇/(𝜅𝑒𝑙+ 𝜅𝑙𝑎𝑡), where 𝜎 , 𝑆 , 𝑇 , 𝜅𝑒𝑙 , and 𝜅𝑙𝑎𝑡 represent the electrical conductivity, 
Seebeck coefficient, absolute temperature, electronic thermal conductivity, and lattice thermal 
conductivity, respectively1. Despite extensive experimental and theoretical efforts, improvement 
of 𝑧𝑇 remains challenging because these transport parameters, especially 𝜎 and 𝑆, are strongly 
interdependent. 
 
From a band-structure perspective, the transport distribution function Σ(𝐸, 𝑇) , or the spectral 
conductivity, plays a central role in determining thermoelectric performance2–5. In particular, the 
strategy known as band convergence6–9, where multiple bands contribute to charge transport near 
the chemical potential 𝜇, serves as one of the most practical strategies to achieve high 𝑧𝑇. Several 
high-performance materials have been successfully developed based on this concept, including 
PbTe-based systems with 𝑧𝑇~2.5 1,,11 and SnSe-based systems with 𝑧𝑇 approaching 3.,12–14.


## Page 4

Despite these empirical successes, a systematic and quantitative understanding of band 
convergence remains incomplete. For example, the optimal values of key band parameters, such 
as the band offset Δ𝐸 (see Fig. 1a), the density of states (DOS) effective mass, relaxation time, 
and chemical potential 𝜇, have not yet been fully clarified.  
 
One difficulty in clarifying the physics of band convergence is that thermoelectric properties can 
be strongly influenced by complex scattering phenomena, such as temperature- and energy-
dependent scattering15,16, intervalley scattering17,18, and impurity scattering that scales with dopant 
concentration. As a result, it is challenging to reveal the intrinsic role of band structure from 
conventional transport experiments commonly employed in thermoelectric research. To address 
this issue, a complementary and conceptually transparent approach is to employ simplified model 
calculations4,5,19–23 in which the spectral conductivity can be artificially designed. Despite its 
simplicity, such a model allows a systematic exploration of band structures with a high degree of 
flexibility, and it has been effective in identifying general design principles for high thermoelectric 
performance5,21,22.  
 
In this study, we employ a virtual spectral conductivity model for two-parabolic-band systems with

conditions that maximize thermoelectric performance in band-converged systems. The resulting

Optimal chemical potential 
Unlike the absolute value of the spectral conductivity or the band offset Δ𝐸, it is not possible to 
specify a universal optimal position of the chemical potential 𝜇 that maximizes 𝑧𝑇 . This is 
because the optimal 𝜇 strongly depends on the relative magnitude of the electronic thermal 
conductivity 𝜅𝑒𝑙 and the lattice thermal conductivity 𝜅𝑙𝑎𝑡. We discuss this point in detail below. 
In the limit of 𝜅𝑙𝑎𝑡≫𝜅𝑒𝑙, maximizing 𝑧𝑇 essentially reduces to maximizing the power factor 
𝜎𝑆2. Within the constant relaxation time approximation for a three-dimensional single parabolic 
band, the optimal chemical potential can be calculated to be located at 𝜇~𝐸𝑒𝑑𝑔𝑒−2.5𝑘𝐵𝑇 (see 
Supplementary Fig. 2b), indicating that the chemical potential should cross the band edge. In 
contrast, in the opposite limit (𝜅𝑒𝑙≫𝜅𝑙𝑎𝑡= 0 ), the figure of merit can be written as 𝑧𝑇=
𝜎𝑆2𝑇/𝜅𝑒𝑙= 𝑆2/𝐿, where the Wiedemann-Franz law has been used in the last equation and 𝐿 is 
the Lorenz number. Under this condition, 𝑧𝑇 increases monotonically as the chemical potential 
moves deeper into the band gap, implying that the optimal 𝜇 would be located at the middle of 
the band gap. However, this conclusion leads to an apparent contradiction when a finite lattice

thermal conductivity 𝜅𝑙𝑎𝑡 is taken into account. When the chemical potential is placed near the 
center of the band gap, the electronic thermal conductivity 𝜅𝑒𝑙 becomes sufficiently small that 
𝜅𝑒𝑙 is no longer larger than 𝜅𝑙𝑎𝑡. Consequently, the assumption 𝜅𝑒𝑙≫𝜅𝑙𝑎𝑡 breaks down. Thus, 
the lattice thermal conductivity must be explicitly taken into account when determining the optimal 
chemical potential for maximizing 𝑧𝑇.

Modifications of our model 
In the present study, we employ a simplified transport model to identify optimal band parameters 
in band-converged systems. Several effects were deliberately neglected, including the energy 
dependence of the relaxation time15,37 and interband scattering17,18.  
 
Since first-principles calculations on PbTe indicate that the energy dependence of the relaxation 
time for longitudinal optical phonon modes is relatively small9, the energy dependence of the 
relaxation time is neglected in the main text of the present study. Similar calculations can be 
performed with an energy-dependent relaxation time; however, the qualitative trends remain the


## Page 17


17 
 
same (see Supplementary Fig. 4). The energy dependence of the relaxation time has often been 
approximated as a simple power-law: 𝜏(𝐸) = 𝜏0𝐸𝛾. The values of 𝛾 are −1/2, 1/2, and 3/2 
for acoustic phonon scattering, polar optical phonon scattering, and ionized impurity scattering, 
respectively. Since all calculations in this work are performed at 𝑇= 900 K, ionized impurity 
scattering is expected to be negligible. In Supplementary Information, we performed similar 
calculations to those shown in Figs. 2 and 5 for 𝛾= −1/2 and 1/2. Although the absolute values 
of 𝑧𝑇  differ from those obtained with 𝛾= 0  in the main text, the overall trends remain 
unchanged: 𝑧𝑇 is enhanced when the absolute value of the spectral conductivity is large, and 
when the band offset Δ𝐸 is zero.  
 
Interband scattering was also neglected in the present calculations. In realistic materials, strong 
interband scattering reduces carrier mobility and, in some cases, could suppress the power factor 
18. In light of the present results, if band convergence leads to a reduction in the spectral 
conductivity, the 𝑧𝑇 value is expected to decrease. In this case, the optimal condition may shift 
from Δ𝐸= 0 to a finite positive Δ𝐸, where the band with larger spectral conductivity dominates 
the transport properties. Nevertheless, even in such cases, the essential requirement for achieving 
high 𝑧𝑇 remains the enhancement of the spectral conductivity near the band edge; that is, high


## Page 18


18 
 
band degeneracy 𝑁, DOS effective mass 𝑚𝐷𝑂𝑆
∗
, and relaxation time 𝜏 are required. 
 
In conclusion, by employing a virtual spectral conductivity approach, we have identified a set of 
band-structure parameters that are favorable for achieving high thermoelectric performance. 
Although the model is deliberately simplified, it provides a theoretical framework that rationalizes 
empirical trends reported in experimental studies. The present work thus provides a unified and 
physically transparent set of design principles for band-structure engineering, offering quantitative 
insights for the development of a wide range of thermoelectric materials. The main conclusions of 
this study are as follows: (i) When a band does not intersect the chemical potential and 
|𝜇−𝐸𝑒𝑑𝑔𝑒| > 5𝑘𝐵𝑇, the contribution of the band is negligible. (ii) To suppress the bipolar effects, 
the band gap must satisfy 𝐸𝑔> 5𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑜𝑝 , where 𝑇𝑜𝑝 is the operating temperature. (iii) Band 
convergence with Δ𝐸 close to zero provides optimal thermoelectric performance when interband 
scattering is insignificant. (iv) Large values of the spectral conductivity (high band degeneracy 𝑁, 
DOS effective mass 𝑚𝐷𝑂𝑆
∗
, and relaxation time 𝜏) are crucial for enhancing 𝑧𝑇.
