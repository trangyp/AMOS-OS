Abstract 
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) develops over a prolonged preclinical phase, during which neuropathological 
changes accumulate long before cognitive symptoms appear. Identifying cognitive functions affected at 
early stages is critical for the preclinical detection of asymptomatic individuals at-risk of AD. Early risk 
identification could enable timely interventions aimed at mitigating the development of significant future 
cognitive impairment. While episodic memory decline typically appears after substantial medial temporal 
lobe damage, spatial navigation has emerged as a particularly sensitive cognitive function in preclinical 
AD. In this review, we provide an overview of spatial navigation computations and the tasks used to assess 
them, highlighting how spatial navigation relies on neural circuits corresponding to the earliest sites of 
AD pathology. We synthesize evidence from cognitively unimpaired individuals with AD biomarkers, i.e. 
individuals at-risk of AD, and discuss future research directions. Overall, performance on spatial 
navigation tasks, particularly path integration and wayfinding, correlates with plasma and CSF biomarkers 
of AD pathology, notably p-tau. Spatial navigation assessment can represent a sensitive and scalable 
approach for early detection of individuals at-risk of AD in preclinical stages, and will inform future 
interventions to mitigate the progression toward clinically significant cognitive impairment.  
 
 
Introduction 
The growing impact of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) on public health has become a pressing concern in the 
context of global population aging. As of today, approximately 57 million people worldwide are living 
with a major neurocognitive disorder (i.e. dementia), a number projected to triple by 2050 (Nichols et al., 
2019). This sharp increase raises critical challenges related to diagnosis and access to care, particularly


## Page 2


due to the complexity of diagnostic procedures and significant geographical disparities in healthcare 
access (Frisoni et al., 2025). 
 
Today, AD is often diagnosed at a moderate stage when symptoms are associated with significant 
functional decline (Pradier et al., 20214). Current diagnostic procedures combine neuropsychological 
testing, neuroimaging, and, in some cases, pathophysiological biomarkers of Alzheimer’s pathology from 
cerebrospinal fluid via lumbar puncture or PET imaging (Delrieu et al., 2018). These procedures are time-
consuming, expensive, and typically conducted in specialized centers. Moreover, the limited availability 
of specialized clinicians, especially in under-resourced or rural areas, further contributes to diagnostic 
delays and inequalities (Defontaines et al., 2016).  
Blood-based biomarkers represent a major breakthrough in preclinical AD detection and are increasingly 
positioned as frontline tools in both primary and secondary care within current diagnostic frameworks 
(Palmqvist et al., 2024; Gemma Salvadó et al., 2025; Perrin et al., 2026; O'Brien et al., 2024). The revised 
NIA-AA criteria now rely exclusively on a biological definition of AD (Jack et al., 2024), while the IWG 
and FDA frameworks maintain a combined biological and clinical definition (Dubois et al., 2021; Dubois 
et al., 2024). In this context, cognitive assessments serve as essential complements to biomarker findings: 
they help reduce false-positive rates by situating biomarker results within a broader clinical picture and 
offer sensitive outcome measures for monitoring disease progression and treatment response (Bouteloup 
et al., 2025). While screening cognitive tests exist (for example, the Mini-Mental State Examination - 
MMSE), they are not sufficiently sensitive to detect the earliest cognitive changes of the disease and 
strongly influenced by non-pathological factors such as education (Villarejo et al., 2011, D’Ignazio et al., 
2025).  
 
Preclinical Detection of Alzheimer’s Disease: The Potential of Spatial Navigation 
 
Traditionally, episodic memory deficits have been considered the hallmark of early AD (Dubois et al., 
2021), but such impairments only become measurable after substantial medial temporal lobe pathology 
has accumulated, and are not specific to AD (Coughlan et al., 2018). This has motivated the search for 
earlier and more specific cognitive markers, among which spatial navigation has emerged as particularly 
promising. While previous reviews established the theoretical and neurobiological basis for this link 
(Coughlan et al., 2018), the present review extends this foundation by incorporating recent neurobiological 
evidence on the earliest stages of AD pathology, and by synthesizing the rapidly expanding literature on 
validated digital and experimental navigation tasks investigated in the context of in vivo AD biomarkers 
in cognitively unimpaired individuals, which constitutes the specific and novel focus of the present review. 
Research findings increasingly point to spatial navigation as an early cognitive function impaired in AD 
(Coughlan et al., 2018; Tu et al., 2015; Yew et al., 2012; Čepukaitytė et al., 2024). As a fundamental 
ability enabling individuals to identify, plan, and maintain a route toward a destination, spatial navigation 
represents a multifaceted cognitive function crucial for autonomy, safety, and quality of life in older adults 
(Van der Ham et al., 2013 ; Gazova et al., 2013). Spatial navigation deficits in AD are linked to early 
pathological changes in key brain regions such as the entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, and retrosplenial


## Page 3


areas, disrupting the neural networks that support spatial orientation (Clifford et al., 2013 ; Coughlan et 
al., 2018). Moreover, digital tools have emerged allowing the assessment of spatial navigation on tablet, 
mobile phone and virtual reality platforms, making the measurement of spatial navigation accessible for 
clinical use and efficient (Coutrot et al., 2018 ; Colombo et al., 2024 ; Sánchez-Escudero et al., 2025), 
beyond specialized memory clinics.  
 
The incorporation of spatial navigation assessment into clinical practice could therefore offer a low-cost, 
sensitive approach for detecting cognitive decline in aging populations. Unlike traditional episodic 
memory measures, spatial navigation is less influenced by verbal factors (Salouhou et al., 2025) than 
conventional cognitive assessments, and may offer a more standardized, internationally applicable means 
of identifying cognitive signatures of AD and improving outcome measures in multicentre trials. For this 
reason, spatial navigation testing is considered to hold substantial diagnostic value and may provide a 
more ecologically valid alternative to conventional memory assessments (Coughlan et al., 2018).  
Beyond diagnostics, spatial navigation could become increasingly important as disease monitoring tools 
when symptoms emerge, as functional outcome measures reflecting real-world difficulties, and as 
sensitive indicators for treatment response. 
 
Literature reviews and meta-analyses have consistently shown that spatial navigation skills decline 
progressively throughout the course of AD, with performance measures demonstrating a large effect size 
in distinguishing healthy older adults and patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI, i.e. prodromal 
stage of neurocognitive disorders), particularly those with amnestic MCI, and AD dementia (Plàcido et 
al., 2022).  
But spatial navigation could be impaired at even earlier, preclinical stages of AD, with neuropathological 
changes such as amyloid-beta deposition and tau tangle formation beginning 10 to 15 years before any 
observable clinical symptoms (Dubois et al., 2021). In its earliest, preclinical stage, subtle 
neurodegeneration can be observed, during which the brain appears to recruit compensatory mechanisms 
to maintain normal cognitive functioning despite the presence of emerging pathological processes. 
Individuals at this stage typically show no noticeable impairment in cognition and preserved autonomy in 
daily activities (Sperling et al., 2011). However, very subtle cognitive changes may be detectable, notably 
navigation impairments, which is promising for early screening. Early diagnosis of the risk of AD and 
identifying preclinical stages of AD may offer an opportunity to anticipate and track the individual course 
of the disease. This opens the door to non-pharmacological multidomain interventions, pharmacological 
strategies and cognitive enhancement approaches aimed at maintaining or enhancing navigational skills 
and cognition function in at-risk individuals. It may help delay or mitigate the progression of cognitive 
impairment, an area that remains to be fully explored and developed (Wallon et Garnier-Crussard, 2025; 
Cammisuli et al., 2024 ; Plàcido et al., 2022; Frisoni et al. 2023). 
The growing development of disease-modifying treatments underscores the need for early detection, as 
these therapies are most effective when initiated in the early phases of the disease trajectory (Villain et al., 
2022). The recent pharmacological advancements, such as monoclonal antibodies targeting amyloid 
pathology, have shown promise in slowing cognitive decline and maintaining functional independence in


## Page 4


individuals at early stages of AD (Wallon et Garnier-Crussard, 2025; Van Dyck et al., 2023; Sims et al., 
2023), even if to date there is no evidence of efficacy of pharmacological interventions in preclinical AD 
(Sperling et al., 2023).  
 
Our review’s position 
 
We define preclinical AD as asymptomatic at-risk individuals and presymptomatic AD following the 
International Working Group (IWG) framework, which correspond to individuals without cognitive 
symptoms but showing pathophysiological biomarkers such as amyloid or tau markers measured via CSF, 
PET imaging, or emerging blood-based assays (Dubois et al., 2021; Dubois et al., 2024). Preclinical AD 
represents a probabilistic state in most cases and does not inevitably progress to clinical AD over an 
individual’s lifetime (Frisoni et al., 2022). 
Previous literature reviews on spatial navigation and AD have mainly focused on individuals with MCI 
or AD dementia, that is, at symptomatic stages of the disease, or have concentrated on a single component 
of spatial navigation or on the digital tools used to assess it (Placido et al., 2022 ; Quimas et al., 2020 ; 
Sànchez-Escuerdo et al., 2024 ; Tuena et al., 2021). To our knowledge, no previous review has specifically 
examined spatial navigation in preclinical AD, nor synthesized the rapidly expanding evidence linking 
experimental navigation tasks to in vivo AD biomarkers in cognitively unimpaired individuals. 
In this review, we focus on cognitively unimpaired individuals at a preclinical stage of AD. We first 
summarize early pathophysiological changes in AD, disrupting spatial navigation computation. We then 
overview tasks currently used to assess spatial navigation, synthesize the existing work on spatial 
navigation performance in preclinical populations, summarize the main findings, and outline future 
directions for advancing research in this emerging field. 
To ensure a focus on the preclinical stage, we selected studies involving cognitively unimpaired 
participants with either biomarker evidence of AD pathology or subsequent conversion to AD dementia. 
We conducted a literature search on PubMed, Web of Science and Google Scholar using a combination 
of keywords targeting spatial navigation, Alzheimer’s disease, preclinical stages, relevant biomarkers, and 
autosomal-dominant AD genes. Spatial navigation terms included: “spatial navigation” OR navigat* OR 
allocentric OR egocentric OR wayfinding OR “path integration”; disease terms included: “Alzheimer’s” 
OR Alzheimer* OR AD; population/stage terms included: preclinical OR “pre-clinical” OR 
presymptomatic OR “pre-symptomatic” OR “early detection” OR predementia OR “pre-dementia” OR 
“subjective cognitive decline” OR SCD OR “isolated cognitive complaints” OR ICC OR “subjective 
cognitive impairment” OR PSEN1 OR PSEN2 OR APP; and biomarker terms included: “Neurofilament 
light” OR NfL OR “Amyloid beta” OR Aβ42 OR Aβ40 OR “tau protein” OR “phosphorylated tau” OR 
“p-tau” OR tau OR biomarker. The same conceptual search strategy was applied across all platforms, but 
the syntax was modified to suit each database. 
 
We included studies assessing spatial navigation in cognitively unimpaired individuals with plasma or 
CSF AD biomarkers, using experimental navigation tasks. Studies restricted to MCI participants or 
defining cognitively healthy at-risk groups solely based on APOE genotype or family history via

questionnaire were excluded. Although studies on autosomal-dominant AD would have been relevant, 
none meeting our criteria were identified.  
 
Spatial Navigation Computation and Early AD Pathology  
 
Spatial navigation is a remarkably complex cognitive function. It depends on the integration of both self-
motion signals (derived from one’s own movement) and environmental cues (stable features of the 
surrounding environment) (Lester et al., 2017). 
These sources of information give rise to two complementary spatial reference systems that support the 
construction of lasting internal representations of the environment: an egocentric system (centered on the 
self) and an allocentric system (centered on the external world) (Werner & Schmidt, 1999). 
 
These complementary egocentric and allocentric systems support two key mechanisms for navigation: 
path integration and cognitive mapping. Path integration uses self-motion cues, including vestibular and 
proprioceptive signals, motor efference copies, and optic flow, to continuously track one’s displacement 
from a starting point (Etienne & Jeffrey, 2004; Segen et al., 2022). This system can operate independently 
of landmarks, though environmental cues can recalibrate position and heading when available (Lester et 
al., 2017). In parallel, the cognitive map represents a mental model of the environment, encoding spatial 
relationships among landmarks and supporting flexible navigation strategies, such as planning novel 
routes or inferring shortcuts. Together, these mechanisms allow the brain to integrate internal motion 
signals and external environmental information, providing a robust framework for spatial orientation and 
movement within complex environments (Epstein et al., 2017). Importantly, in AD, these same circuits 
are among the first to be affected both by amyloid deposition and tau pathology. We will focus on early 
tau pathology (leading to neurofibrillary tangles) as it may specifically disrupt the neural computations 
underlying spatial navigation. Neurofibrillary tangles first appear in the most superficial layer of the trans-
entorhinal cortex and in subcortical nuclei (Braak stage I) before extending to the entorhinal cortex and 
Ammon’s horn of the hippocampus (Braak stage II) (Braak & Tredici 2015). These early-affected regions 
are central hubs for spatial navigation processing. 
Within this network, navigation relies on the activity of specialized neurons: place cells in the 
hippocampus encode specific locations, grid cells in the medial entorhinal cortex fire in a regular 
hexagonal pattern across the environment, and border and boundary cells in the entorhinal cortex and 
subiculum respond to environmental limits (Epstein et al., 2017; O’Keefe and Dostrovsky, 1971; Bicanski 
& Burgess, 2020). Speed cells in the medial entorhinal cortex further contribute by encoding the animal’s 
movement velocity (Kropff et al., 2015). Importantly, the entorhinal and trans-entorhinal cortices also 
receive strong vestibular inputs, which are crucial for maintaining the stability of place and head-direction 
cells (Yoder et al., 2014). Consistent with this, neuroimaging studies show that reduced vestibular 
function, including saccular impairment, is associated with morphometric alterations in these cortices 
(Vania et al., 2025 ; Hitier et al., 2014; Jacob et al., 2020). Thus, the first regions affected by tau pathology 
overlap with those supporting the neural computations essential for spatial navigation.


## Page 6


In Braak stages III–IV, tau pathology extends from the medial temporal lobe to limbic and associative 
cortices. Aggregates accumulate within lesions from the II stages, before spreading to the neocortex of the 
fusiform and lingual gyri (Braak stage III). Pathology then progresses to the high-order association 
cortices in the prefrontal, parietal, and temporal lobes (Braak stage IV). The retrosplenial cortex (RSC), 
which becomes functionally implicated during these stages, plays a central role in spatial navigation. 
Strongly interconnected with the entorhinal cortex (Groen et al., 1990), the RSC contains head direction 
cells and participates in the integration of egocentric and allocentric spatial frames of reference (Alexander 
et al., 2015). These interactions are thought to mediate the communication between parietal egocentric 
and medial temporal allocentric systems (Vass et al., 2013; Dhindsa et al., 2014). Note that at these stages, 
cognitive symptoms (beyond spatial navigation) appear, and individuals enter the clinical stage of AD 
(MCI or dementia). 
 
The hippocampus and entorhinal cortex construct spatial representations resembling internal maps, while 
posterior regions such as the parahippocampal and retrosplenial cortices provide contextual cues that 
anchor and stabilize these maps with respect to external landmarks. These spatial representations are 
subsequently integrated with frontal circuits to support goal-directed route planning and navigation 
(Epstein et al., 2017). 
Ultimately, tau aggregates extend into neighboring neocortical regions, resulting in widespread cortical 
atrophy (Braak stages V–VI). The pathology subsequently progresses to first-order sensory association 
and premotor areas (stage V) and eventually reaches the primary neocortical fields (stage VI).

Spatial Navigation Framework and Experimental Designs 
 
Several factors influence how cognition is engaged during navigation. These include the spatial scale of 
the environment (for example, navigating a small room versus a large city), the availability and 
reliability of environmental cues, and the navigator’s prior knowledge or familiarity with the space, 
including awareness of starting points and intended destinations (Lester et al., 2017). Moreover, 
individual and socio-demographic factors can also modulate navigation performance (Coutrot et al., 
2018; Yavuz & Spiers, 2026). Therefore, there are several ways to assess spatial navigation. Everyday


## Page 9


navigation represents a highly integrative behavior that draws on perceptual, memory, and executive 
systems. It involves     combining multiple types of spatial information, selecting context-appropriate 
strategies, and flexibly switching between them when circumstances change. Moreover, effective 
navigation requires the coordination of numerous sub-processes, making it one of the most cognitively 
demanding and multifaceted human abilities (Lester et al., 2017). 
 
In the context of evaluating spatial navigation as a screening tool for AD, several different types of tasks 
have been used, including path integration tasks, cognitive mapping tasks, wayfinding tasks, GPS-based 
driving measures, static visuospatial tasks, and spatial navigation questionnaires. 
Path integration tasks require participants to follow a predefined path in three dimensions environment 
and then return to their starting location, typically without any external landmarks or visual cues. Various 
performance metrics can be derived from these tasks, such as rotation error, distance error, or drop error. 
Several variants of path integration tasks exist, for instance, those including or excluding landmark support 
(Colmant et al., 2025; Shima et al., 2024; Chen, 2024; Laczó et al., 2024).  
Another approach is the cognitive mapping task, in which participants are asked to locate landmarks on a 
2D map after exploring an environment, either physically or virtually. Examples include the Super Market 
Task (Coughlan et al., 2023). 
Wayfinding tasks assess participants’ ability to navigate toward a goal within a virtual or real-world 
environment. Navigation may rely on a previously studied 2D map or on memory from self-guided 
exploration. Performance can be evaluated based on the accuracy and efficiency with which participants 
reach specific landmarks or target locations such as Sea Hero Quest, the Navigation Test Suite (Laczò et 
al., 2022), or the Floor Maze Task (Tangen et al., 2022) the Barn Ruins (Chatterjee et al., 2025) (see figure 
2).  Importantly, performance on some navigation tasks has been shown to predict real-world navigation 
behavior, supporting their ecological validity. For instance, scores on Sea Hero Quest correlate with GPS-
measured navigation during a similar task in the real world (Coutrot et al., 2019; Goodroe et al., 2025), 
and laboratory task performance has been shown to reflect real-world wayfinding difficulties in AD 
patients (Puthusseryppady et al., 2022). 
GPS-based driving measures represent another way to assess spatial navigation abilities, by analyzing 
real-world driving behavior through GPS tracking, focusing on various navigation-related metrics. For 
example, Ghosh et al. (2022) adopt a mechanistic, fine-grained approach using machine learning applied 
to features from complementary domains, including mobility and spatial entropy measures, trajectory 
shape descriptors, and graph-theoretical topology analyses, enabling the detection of subtle alterations in 
navigation patterns. 
Static visuospatial tasks, such as the Four Mountains Task (Coughlan et al., 2023; Lorentzen et al., 2023) 
or the Visual Perspective Taking Task (Allison et al., 2019), assess spatial processing in static conditions, 
without active movement through space. 
Finally, spatial navigation questionnaires, such as the Santa Barbara Sense of Direction Scale (Alisson 
et al., 2019), can be administered to participants or close informants to assess perceived navigation abilities 
and everyday orientation performance.

After summarizing the different approaches used to estimate and measure spatial navigation, we now 
describe studies assessing the associations between spatial navigation and Alzheimer’s disease pathology 
measured in vivo in cognitively healthy individuals. 
 
Evidence Linking Spatial Navigation Deficits to Preclinical AD 
 
Several studies have evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of spatial navigation measures in preclinical AD, 
with ROC-based analyses providing the clearest estimates of classification accuracy. Among the most 
robust findings, Shima et al. (2025) showed that path integration (PI) errors strongly discriminated plasma 
p-tau181 levels (AUC = 0.86, sensitivity = 91.7%, specificity = 77.8%), with PI errors also correlating 
with GFAP, and NfL. Allison et al. (2019) similarly reported AUC values ranging from approximately 
0.72 to 0.77 across several components of a cognitive mapping task, which corresponds to a moderate  (or 
fair) level of discrimination according to commonly used interpretations of ROC curve analyses, with 
landmark identification emerging as the strongest discriminator of CSF Aβ42 status (AUC = 0.77). Tangen 
et al. (2022) found that the Floor Maze Test moderately predicted conversion to AD dementia at 2 and 4 
years (AUC ≈ 0.74–0.75), with PI performance linked to CSF p-tau and NfL but not CSF Aβ42/40 ratios 
or APOE ε4. 
 
Beyond ROC analyses, several studies have identified meaningful associations between behavioral 
navigation performance and AD biomarkers. Colmant et al. (2025) reported that rotation and drop errors 
in PI increased with medial temporal tau burden (Table 1), while Chen (2024) found significant 
correlations between plasma p-tau217/p-tau181 and errors in a human analog of the Morris Water Maze


## Page 13


(r ≈ 0.34). Coughlan et al. (2023) further demonstrated that higher CSF p-tau181 and lower Aβ1–42 
predicted poorer performance on both their navigation task and the Four Mountains Test, although the 
magnitude of effect was small and with the p-tau181/Aβ1–42 ratio emerging as the strongest predictor. 
However, the literature also documents numerous null or inconsistent findings, particularly for amyloid-
related measures. Amyloid status did not reliably predict path integration errors in Colmant et al. (2025), 
nor Morris Water Maze performance in Laczó et al. (2024). Similarly, Lorentzen et al. (2025) found no 
biomarker-related differences on the Four Mountains Test, and several studies reported no associations 
between navigation performance and plasma markers such as NfL, GFAP, or Aβ42/40 (Shima et al., 2024; 
Chen, 2024). These discrepancies may reflect methodological variability or small effect sizes. 
Results from spatial navigation questionnaires and static visual tasks are mixed. Levine et al. (2024, 2022) 
reported very weak or nonsignificant associations between self- or informant-rated spatial navigation 
questionnaires and AD biomarkers, whereas Allison et al. (2018) found moderate predictive value for CSF 
Aβ42 in self-informant questionnaire (AUC = 0.72, sensitivity = 0.867, specificity = 0.667). Similarly, 
egocentric tasks like the 4MT show inconsistent biomarker correlations (Coughlan et al., 2023).  
GPS-derived measures have shown excellent predictive performance. Bayat et al. (2021) reported very 
high accuracy (AUC = 0.96; 95% CI 0.903–0.981) using one year of continuous GPS data, with age and 
APOE ε4 status being the strongest predictors to differentiate Ab- from Ab+. Similarly, Roe et al. (2023) 
and Chen & Babulal (2025) found that biomarker-positive individuals tended to reduce their driving 
frequency, trip distance, and route complexity. However, as noted by the authors, the strongest predictive 
metrics appear to reflect self-regulatory driving behaviors rather than pure spatial navigation ability, 
although the results remain highly promising for early detection of preclinical AD-related changes. 
Despite generally positive trends, cross-study comparisons remain challenging due to substantial 
methodological heterogeneity, including differences in navigation paradigms, biomarker modalities, and 
cutoff values for biomarker positivity. These inconsistencies likely contribute to the variability in reported 
effect sizes. 
Taken together, current evidence supports the potential of objective, well-characterized navigation tasks 
as indicators of early AD-related changes. Their clinical applicability would be strengthened by 
standardized task design, broader adoption of ROC-based analyses and also compute NPV and PPV 
according to the clinical context of use, as pretest probability of having amyloid pathology for example 
influence considerably NPV and PPV (Bouteloup et al., 2025). Combined with accessible plasma 
biomarkers, navigation paradigms may offer a practical and informative approach for identifying 
preclinical AD in cognitively healthy individuals.

Spatial navigation limitation to detect individuals at-risk of AD  
 
Although spatial navigation has emerged as a promising cognitive marker for the early detection of AD, 
there are important limitations preventing its use as a standalone diagnostic tool. To date, no existing 
spatial navigation task demonstrates sufficient diagnostic accuracy to be used at the individual level, with 
reported classification performances often yielding moderate AUC values. A major challenge lies in the 
intrinsic complexity of spatial navigation itself, which relies on a wide range of cognitive processes, 
including episodic memory, attention, executive functions, visuospatial abilities, and strategy use. As a 
result, even within a single task, different outcome measures or error types may reflect distinct underlying 
cognitive mechanisms. This heterogeneity complicates the interpretation of navigation deficits and limits 
the specificity of spatial navigation measures for AD–related processes. While spatial navigation tasks are 
generally less influenced by verbal or linguistic abilities and cognitive reserve than traditional cognitive 
screening tools (Salouhou et al., 2025), they remain sensitive to several non-pathological factors. Cultural 
background, educational level, and characteristics of the environment in which individuals live can 
substantially modulate navigation performance (Coutrot et al., 2018 ; Gilles et al., 2024). Large-scale 
studies using the Sea Hero Quest cohort have highlighted the influence of global determinants such as 
education, gender, and country of origin on spatial navigation abilities, as well as the impact of 
environmental factors such as urban layout and city entropy. These findings indicate that spatial navigation 
performance is shaped by lifelong experience and environmental exposure, raising important challenges 
for cross-cultural comparability and normative modeling. 
 
Taken together, these limitations indicate that spatial navigation measures are not yet fully validated for 
routine clinical use in AD. Nonetheless, ongoing methodological advances, large population-based 
datasets, and integration with biomarker and longitudinal data highlight the potential of spatial 
navigation as one of several promising avenues for detecting early cognitive changes. Moreover, a 
broader investigation of cognitive functions sensitive to early AD pathological changes alongside spatial 
navigation could further improve our understanding of preclinical AD. 
Promising Research Avenue for Understanding Spatial Navigation in Preclinical AD 
Several emerging research directions may further clarify spatial navigation alterations in preclinical AD. 
These include comorbidities, complementary physiological measures, sex- and hormone-related factors, 
socio-demographic factors, and early subcortical pathology, highlighting the multifactorial nature of 
navigation as an early AD marker. 
Most studies on spatial navigation in AD have excluded comorbidities, despite their high prevalence, 
including hypertension, depression, and hypercholesterolemia (Temedda et al., 2025). Although 
comorbidities are known to negatively affect cognition across the AD spectrum (Haaksma et al., 2017; 
Beach and Malek-Ahmadi, 2021), their specific impact on spatial navigation has been rarely investigated 
(Keynejad et al., 2017; Weniger et al., 2011) and remains unexplored in preclinical AD. Addressing this


## Page 15


gap may provide new insight into disease progression and individual prognosis (Beach and Malek-
Ahmadi, 2020). Beyond general comorbidities, the integration of brain comorbidities (i.e., copathologies), 
notably TDP-43, Lewy bodies or vascular damages will be also interesting in future studies. Early work 
comparing spatial navigation in FTD and AD has shown distinct navigation profiles across these 
conditions (Tu et al., 2015; Tu et al., 2017), and studies in vascular cognitive impairment have similarly 
demonstrated navigation impairments linked to cerebrovascular pathology (Lowry et al., 2020; Lowry et 
al., 2023). These findings highlight the potential of navigation tasks to differentiate AD from other 
etiologies of cognitive decline. 
Complementary physiological measures such as EEG and eye-tracking may provide additional insight 
into how spatial navigation is supported or altered in preclinical AD. While behavioral tasks capture 
overall performance, EEG and gaze metrics can reveal early changes in visual exploration, attentional 
allocation, and neural processing. Evidence from later disease stages supports this possibility. For 
instance, Plaza-Rozales et al. (2023) showed that individuals with amnestic MCI who later progressed to 
AD dementia displayed both impaired navigation learning and altered gaze patterns, together with reduced 
occipital responses and disrupted beta-band connectivity during a virtual navigation task (see figure2-C). 
However, such approaches have not yet been applied to biomarker-positive cognitively healthy 
individuals, leaving their potential contribution to the characterization of preclinical navigation changes 
unexplored. 
Women experience a higher incidence of AD than men, a difference that may in part reflect the 
neuroendocrine changes occurring across the menopausal transition (Coughlan et al., 2023). 
Perimenopausal period constitutes a critical window of emerging vulnerability to AD-related brain 
changes where early intervention may be beneficial (Mervosh et Devi, 2025; Henderson & Rocca, 2012). 
One study suggests that spatial navigation is modulated by hormonal status. Navigation strategy use 
appears sensitive to endocrine aging, with estrogen fluctuations influencing the balance between place- 
and response-based strategies (Yu, 2023). Investigating spatial navigation performance during menopause 
may thus provide valuable insight into how hormonal transitions shape cognitive aging and early AD 
vulnerability. 
 
Epidemiological data on spatial navigation in healthy populations remain scarce, limiting our 
understanding of interindividual differences, including potential sex effects. Sea Hero Quest, a mobile 
game played by over 3.7 million individuals worldwide, has begun to address this gap, revealing 
influences of age, gender, and cultural background on navigation performance  (Coutrot et al., 2018 ; 
Gilles et al., 2024). These large-scale data enable the creation of population benchmarks, which can inform 
sensitive, easy-to-administer navigation tests and help detect early changes in genetically at-risk groups, 
such as APOE*ε4 carriers (Coughlan et al., 2019; Lim et al., 2023). Overall, these findings underscore 
the promise of spatial navigation measures for early AD assessment, with potential clinical impact for 
patients and families.


## Page 16


GPS-based studies in cognitively unimpaired individuals with AD biomarkers have yielded promising 
results, particularly for identifying preclinical participants through driving behavior. These approaches are 
already valuable but extending them to include navigation-focused features could provide additional 
insights into spatial navigation strategies. For example, Ghosh et al. (2022) applied machine learning to a 
range of complementary navigation-related features and successfully distinguished AD patients from 
cognitively healthy controls. It would be interesting to explore whether such metrics are sensitive at the 
preclinical stage, particularly for differentiating cognitively healthy individuals with AD biomarkers from 
those without. 
 
Early tau pathology affects subcortical nuclei, including the locus coeruleus (LC), one of the earliest 
regions involved in AD, which may contribute to subtle spatial navigation impairments at the preclinical 
stage (Braak & Del Tredici, 2015). By supporting contextual updating and flexible transitions between 
egocentric and allocentric navigation strategies, LC-related circuits play a role in spatial learning (Poe et 
al., 2020; Grella & Donaldson, 2024; Kaufman et al., 2018; Zhong & Moffat, 2018). Early LC 
degeneration may therefore disrupt navigation independently of hippocampal atrophy or overt cognitive 
decline. Future work in humans could explore whether combining high-resolution imaging of the locus 
coeruleus with controlled navigation paradigms helps clarify links between LC integrity, navigation-
related neural activity, and behavior. 
 
Conclusion  
Spatial navigation is a promising cognitive domain for early detection of AD pathology, including at 
preclinical stages when individuals remain asymptomatic but already show biomarker evidence of 
pathology. The neural circuits supporting navigation, especially the entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, and 
retrosplenial cortex, are among the first affected by AD pathology, providing a strong biological rationale 
for assessing navigation deficits as early markers. 
At present, tasks assessing path integration, wayfinding, and cognitive mapping appear particularly 
promising for detecting subtle changes in preclinical AD but are still far from implementation in daily 
practice. Performance on these tasks correlates with plasma and CSF biomarkers, with p-tau showing the 
strongest and most reproducible associations across studies, whereas amyloid biomarkers appear less 
consistently related to navigation performance. 
While more studies are needed, spatial navigation assessment, particularly when combined with AD 
biomarkers, may transform preclinical screening, guide individualized care, and ultimately mitigate the 
impact of AD on patients, families, and healthcare systems. By moving beyond traditional memory-based 
assessments, spatial navigation offers a promising avenue to detect the earliest cognitive signatures of AD 
and clinical translation.
