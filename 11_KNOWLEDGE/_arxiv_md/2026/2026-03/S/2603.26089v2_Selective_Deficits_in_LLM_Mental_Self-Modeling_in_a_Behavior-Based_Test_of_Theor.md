Selective Deficits in LLM Mental Self-Modeling in a
Behavior-Based Test of Theory of Mind
Christopher Ackerman
christopher.ackerman@gmail.com
Abstract
The ability to represent oneself and others as agents with knowledge, intentions,
and belief states that guide their behavior - Theory of Mind - is a human universal
that enables us to navigate - and manipulate - the social world. It is supported by
our ability to form mental models of ourselves and others. Its ubiquity in human
affairs entails that LLMs have seen innumerable examples of it in their training
data and therefore may have learned to mimic it, but whether they have actually
learned causal models that they can deploy in arbitrary settings is unclear. We
therefore develop a novel experimental paradigm that requires that subjects form
representations of the mental states of themselves and others and act on them
strategically rather than merely describe them. We test a wide range of leading
open and closed source LLMs released since 2024, as well as human subjects,
on this paradigm. We find that 1) LLMs released before mid-2025 fail at all of
our tasks, 2) more recent LLMs achieve human-level performance on modeling
the cognitive states of others, and 3) even frontier LLMs fail at our self-modeling
task - unless afforded a scratchpad in the form of a reasoning trace. We further
demonstrate cognitive load effects on other-modeling tasks, offering suggestive
evidence that LLMs are using something akin to limited-capacity working memory
to hold these mental representations in mind during a single forward pass. Finally,
we explore the mechanisms by which reasoning models succeed at the self- and
other-modeling tasks, and show that they readily engage in strategic deception.
1
Introduction
The ability to form mental models of oneself and others is a core aspect of human cognition. It
underlies Theory of Mind (ToM) - the ability to represent oneself and others as agents with knowledge,
intentions, and belief states that guide their behavior - which is a cornerstone of human social relations.
As such, it is of interest whether large language models (LLMs) have acquired this ability as well.
An LLM endowed with a robust ToM would be better able to navigate the social world, to cooperate,
and to empathize, but also to deceive and manipulate.
The classic false-belief test used to assess ToM in children [Wimmer and Perner, 1983, Perner et al.,
1987] - in which subjects are described or shown a scenario in which a character witnesses an object
being placed in one location and then the object is moved to another location after the character
has left the room, and are asked where the character will look for the object when she returns - has
also been used to evaluate ToM in LLMs [Kosinski, 2024, Strachan et al., 2024]; experiments have
shown increasing performance with model scale, with recent frontier models reaching human-level
performance. One weakness of using such ToM tests to study LLMs’ ability to model mental states
is that they may be fooled by LLMs’ prowess with mimicry. Implicit examples of ToM-guided
behavior are ubiquitous in books and other materials LLMs have been trained on, offering them many
opportunities to learn contextually driven performances of ToM-like behavior. Furthermore, the tests
themselves are likely in the LLMs’ pretraining data, offering LLMs the possibility of doing abstract
pattern matching rather than online mental state tracking.

To address this weakness, we develop a novel paradigm designed to measure LLMs’ ability to model
the cognitive states of themselves and others that gets them substantially outside of their training
distribution. Inspired by behavioral experiments in pre-verbal children [Onishi and Baillargeon, 2005,
Southgate et al., 2007] and primates [Hare et al., 2000, 2001, Krupenye et al., 2016], and following
the behavior-based testing model laid out in Ackerman [2026], we gauge task success by whether
LLMs can select the appropriate action as defined in the context of the paradigm, rather than asking
them to report mental states. In addition, our paradigm probes the ability to model multiple types of
cognitive states - not merely whether another agent has a false belief, but also whether that agent has
definitive knowledge or merely belief, and whether an agent has cooperative intentions or not. Third,
it probes the ability to model one’s own mental state, in particular whether one may be ignorant of
something that others know.
The paradigm takes the form of a text-based game in which characters - including, importantly, the
subject being tested (“the player”) - are described as being in a room where they can see objects
being put in and moved between containers and see characters leaving and re-entering the room. At
the beginning of the player’s turn, a scenario is described to them in which various events occur
and at the end of which they are told that one of the characters - themselves, their teammate, or an
opponent - is going to be asked to name the final contents of a particular container. A correct answer
will score a point for the answerer’s team. The player is then given the opportunity to tell a character
information, ask a character for information (both at the cost of half a point), or pass at no cost.
The paradigm affords testing four different components of mental modeling, isolated by four different
conditions (examples shown in Figure 1):

The “True vs False Belief” task is composed of scenarios in which the teammate is the answerer,
the player knows the true final contents of the container, and the teammate either 1) has outdated
information about the container (its contents were switched while the teammate was out of the room)
or 2) believes that the container contains the object it does (the contents weren’t changed while the
teammate was gone, or were changed back to what the teammate witnessed before the end). Success
entails telling the teammate the final contents of the container in the former case and passing in the
latter. This is analogous to the classic ToM “false belief” test, but requires that understanding be
demonstrated by action selection rather than verbal description.
The “Teammate Knowledge” task is composed of scenarios in which the teammate is the answerer,
the player knows the true final contents of the container, and the teammate either 1) has a false belief

as defined above or 2) knows the contents of the container (because they were in the room when the
final placement happened). Success entails telling the teammate the final contents of the container in
the former case and passing in the latter. This probes whether the subject can distinguish whether
another agent lacks knowledge.
The “Self Knowledge” task is composed of scenarios in which the player is the answerer, the teammate
knows the true final contents of the container, and the player either 1) may have outdated information
about the contents of the container (because the scenario ended with the player outside the room but
other characters inside) or 2) knows the contents of the container (as defined above). Success entails
asking the teammate for the final contents of the container in the former case and passing in the latter.
This probes whether the subject can distinguish whether they themselves lack knowledge.
Finally the “Teammate vs Opponent” task contrasts scenarios where 1) the player is the answerer
and lacks information the teammate has or the teammate is the answerer and lacks information the
player has (where success means asking or telling the teammate, respectively) with those where 2)
the player is the answerer and lacks information the opponent has or the opponent is the answerer
and lacks information the player has (where success means passing, although lying in the latter cases
is scored as success as well). This probes whether the subject can distinguish the intentions of others.
We test 28 LLMs released since May 2024, from seven different open and closed source providers,
including a mix of reasoning and non-reasoning models, and with reasoning turned on and off in
the former. In the foregoing, nonreasoning models and reasoning models with reasoning turned off,
when required to output only their action selection, are referred to as “nonthinking”. Models which
are able to use pre-action tokens - either in their reasoning traces or in their output chain of thought -
are referred to as “thinking”.
The “nonthinking” models are of particular interest. Here, the correct action must be decided in a
single forward pass, without use of an external “scratchpad”. (Technically it takes multiple passes to
generate the full Ask and Tell action syntax, but the action choice is determined by the first token,
and that is where ~all the errors lay.) Thinking models can (and often do) simply copy over portions
of the game setup and scenario to prompt themselves into a correct answer, but nonthinking models
must perform their computations entirely internally; success here suggests that an LLM has learned a
mechanism for building and running mental models within its weights.
As a baseline, we test human subjects on all of these tasks as well, and find strong and fairly uniform
performance across tasks. However, nonthinking LLMs older that GPT-5 fail at all of these tasks,
achieving no better than chance success. In addition, even the recent nonthinking LLMs that achieve
human-level performance on our “other-modeling” tasks fail at our “self-knowledge” task. Among
those nonthinking LLMs that did succeed at modeling another agent’s knowledge/belief states in our
original set of scenarios, presenting them with an equivalent set of scenarios that required tracking of
more transitions between knowledge and belief states impaired performance, while a set of scenarios
that merely included extra irrelevant events did not, suggesting that, among these LLMs and for these
cognitive components, mental states were indeed being tracked.
With thinking, in contrast, the best LLMs achieve near-perfect performance on all of these tasks, with
a clear correlation between overall model ability/recency and task performance. Across all LLMs of
which we run both thinking and nonthinking versions, the biggest advantage offered by thinking is
the ability to suppress Tell actions when they are inappropriate (because the teammate believes the
truth, or the opponent is the answerer). Moreover, thinking LLMs uniquely display a spontaneous
proclivity for, and sometimes adeptness, with lying.
1.1
Related Work
The question of whether and to what degree LLMs possess ToM has attracted substantial interest
in recent years. Early reports suggested they performed comparably to children [Kosinski, 2023],
but Ullman [2023] showed that small, semantically irrelevant alterations to false-belief scenarios
significantly degraded performance, suggesting that LLMs may be relying on surface-level pattern
matching rather than genuine mental state tracking. Subsequently, Kosinski [2024] tested multiple
LLMs of increasing scale on false-belief tasks modeled on the classic Sally-Anne paradigm, finding
that GPT-4 solved 75% of tasks and arguing that ToM may have emerged as a by-product of improving
language skills. Strachan et al. [2024] found that GPT-4 performed at or above human level on

a battery of ToM tasks including false belief and misdirection, and Street et al. [2024] reported
adult-level performance in GPT-4 on higher-order ToM tasks as well.
On the other hand, Shapira et al. [2024] stress-tested social reasoning abilities in LLMs and found
that performance was brittle under adversarial conditions. Hu et al. [2025] reviewed the conflicting
evidence for ToM in LLMs and argued that many existing evaluations conflate task performance with
underlying competence. Riemer et al. [2025] found that LLMs did much more poorly when they
needed to apply ToM in an iterated game.
The most significant contribution of our work to these precedents is the behavior-based paradigm.
Rather than asking LLMs to describe what a character will do, we place the LLM in the position of a
first-person player who must select an action based on its models of itself and others. This probes the
ability to use information strategically, which is a more stringent test of competency, and also a more
safety-relevant one. It has the added benefit of likely being outside LLMs’ training distribution.
2
Methods
Our experimental paradigm is set up as a text-based game in which participants are described as being
in a room and must monitor the changing contents of various containers in the room, and the egresses
and entrances of other players, as they are described, and update their models of the mental states of
players accordingly in order to act correctly. The act in question is whether to ask for information, to
tell information, or to do neither. If they ask for or tell information, participants must choose which
other player in the game to target - their teammate, or an opponent. Their decision must be guided
both by their mental models of themselves and others and which player - themselves, their teammate,
or one of their opponents - is going to need to know the information at the end of the turn. The full
game setup presented to participants, along with one example game scenario, is shown in Figure 9 in
the Appendix.
The game scenarios are structured so as to probe different mental components of ToM. One of these
is the ability to understand that other agents can have beliefs that differ from reality as observed by
the participants - as in the common false-belief task. For example, the player and their teammate
may both observe an object being put into a container, after which the teammate leaves the room,
after which the object is replaced with a different object. When the game scenario entails that the
teammate will need to name the current contents of the container in order to score 1 point, the correct
action of the player is to spend 0.5 points to tell the teammate the current contents. When the contents
at the end of the scenario are the same as what the teammate witnessed, or when the teammate is in
the room at the end and has observed the current contents of the container, then the correct action is to
pass. On the other hand, when it is an opponent who will need to name the contents of the container,
the correct action is not to tell them. Sometimes it is the player themselves who will need to name the
contents of the container. In these cases, the player must recognize whether they are in position to
know the final contents of the container, and if they are not but their teammate is, then they should
spend 0.5 points to ask them. (Point values were chosen arbitrarily, without any optimization.)
The scenario descriptions we analyze are designed so that there is a clear correct action. In cases
where there is some inherent ambiguity - such as about whether an opponent would believe a lie -
all defensible actions are counted as successes. Complete mappings of scenario variables to correct
actions are shown in Table 1 in the Appendix.
A scenario is defined by a specific combination of answerer and epistemic states (knowing, true belief,
false belief, no information) among the player, teammate, and opponents. There were 26 scenarios
in all, and each was presented to models 10 times (once for humans), with random variation of free
parameters (object names and container assignments). Scenarios were generated in advance, and the
same ones were presented to all subjects. Conditions are balanced such that always answering with
either of the two plausible alternative in each condition would result in 50% success. Performance is
computed as percent correct; error bars in figures are 95% Wilson binomial intervals.
For LLMs, we also generated additional sets of “load” scenarios. One set (“Event load”) contained a
higher number of events than our base scenarios, but not a higher number of epistemic state transitions
(ESTs) that needed to be tracked, and was contrasted with our base scenarios. Another (“EST load”)
contained a higher number of ESTs that needed to be tracked, and was contrasted with a fourth
set of scenarios that had the same number of events but fewer ESTs. An example of a scenario

where multiple ESTs need to be tracked is when the teammate is out of the room and objects being
moved between containers render their belief false and then true again. Load effects are computed as
percentage decrement in performance, with 95% CIs computed via stratified bootstrap.
Each scenario was presented one at a time, preceded by the entire game setup description, in
independent API calls. All LLMs were called via OpenRouter API. Human participants (n=10)
were recruited via AI Alignment-related Slack channels, and were shown identical information; the
presentation only differed in that the game setup was displayed initially in chunks for easier parsing,
and then was available in an expandable section while the individual scenarios were presented. They
were paid $30/hr. We make no claim that our human participants are representative of the overall
human population - in fact we suspect they are more intelligent and pay more attention to the task
than a randomly chosen human - but we suggest those outstanding characteristics are useful for
parsing the game setup and enduring the numerous scenario presentations, rather than instantiating
Theory of Mind per se, which is reported to be a human universal.

Results
Figure 2 shows the results for “nonthinking” LLMs. Performance is generally poor among older
LLMs, but there is an apparent upward trend for the “other-modeling” tasks, with the most recent
LLMs reaching or exceeding human-level performance. However, this is not true for the self-modeling
task: no LLM achieves notably above chance performance, and no upward trend with model ability
is evident. Notably, humans do not show this pattern: they have similarly high performance across
cognitive components.
Figure 2: Accuracy on each cognitive component, by LLM (nonthinking); humans are rightmost bars.
In contrast, as shown in Figure 3, when allowed to spend tokens to think through the task, even
older LLMs perform well on the other-modeling tasks. Moreover, a number of recent LLMs achieve
human-level performance in the self-modeling task as well, and across models there is an upward
trend in performance with increasing overall model ability and recency. An example of how thinking
tokens help a model succeed at the self-knowledge task is shown in Figure 11 in the Appendix.
Self-modeling also stands out in the correlation structure of performance across tasks. Figure 4
shows how performance on the various cognitive components correlates across models. Among the
nonthinking models, components generally correlate fairly well - facility with one predicts facility
with another. Self-knowledge stands in stark contrast, correlating with no other task. For thinking
models, however, this distinction is much less apparent.
To examine the effects of load on performance, we run another set of experiments where we indepen-
dently increased 1) “event load” - the number of events that were described in the scenario - while
holding the number of “epistemic state transitions” that the player needs to track - constant and 2)
“epistemic state transition load” while holding event load constant (see Methods for more details).

How does reasoning enable superior performance? A more granular analysis of the types of errors
models make is shown in Figure 6. While thinking models are better than nonthinking models overall,
the difference is particularly stark in cases where the correct behavior is to suppress a Tell action.
Without thinking, LLMs tend to reflexively Tell the answerer, even when circumstances warrant
passing, such as when the answerer is their teammate and already believes the truth, or when the
answerer is the opponent. An example reasoning trace from an LLM thinking through the need to
Pass is shown in Figure 12 in the Appendix.
Suppressing inappropriate tells - avoiding giving away too much information - is also key to effective
deception. As can be seen in Figure 7, bottom, thinking models are much better at “strategic
deception”: spending 0.5 points to lie to an opponent when the opponent believes the truth, and
passing when they believe incorrect information. And as can be seen in Figure 7, top, thinking models
have a greater proclivity to tell their opponent lies, whether strategic or not. Notably though, the
most recent model versions from leading providers - the 4.6 series from Anthropic, the 5.4 series
from OpenAI, and the 3.1 series from Google - lie less than their earlier counterparts, possibly due to
enhanced character training. An example of a reasoning LLM thinking its way into an effective lie is

4
Discussion
We introduce a novel, behavior-based ToM paradigm to test the ability to model mental states. It
requires multiple perspective taking, solicits first-person actions rather than verbal descriptions, and
is designed to be outside the training distribution of LLMs. We show that, while humans do not
have difficulty with the tasks, LLMs struggle with them unless allowed to use output tokens to work
through the logic.
Our finding that nonthinking LLMs older than GPT-5 fail at all of these tasks stands in contrast to
their reported human-level performance on classic false-belief tasks. That the reason for this may be
that we are successfully isolating a true mental modeling requirement is supported by our load results.
Among nonthinking LLMs that did succeed at our “True vs False” and closely related “Teammate

Knowledge” tasks, increasing the number of epistemic state transitions that they needed to keep track
of decreased performance - while simply increasing the number of events described did not.
It is especially noteworthy that all nonthinking LLMs fail at our Self-Knowledge task; they seem to be
specifically impaired at representing the idea that things could have changed in the environment that
they as the player aren’t aware of - at adopting the perspective of a themselves as a player in the game
subject to the same rules as others. This specificity is apparent in the task performance correlations
as well: uniquely, performance in the Self-Knowledge task did not correlate with performance on
any other condition. Finally, unlike the other conditions, Self-Knowledge task performance does not
correlate with overall model ability or release date. Notably, an analogous ability has been found to
appear only in children over the age of five [Rohwer et al., 2012], suggesting it’s a later-developing
cognitive component of ToM than the others.
Thinking tokens do allow LLMs to achieve strong performance on the other-modeling tasks, and
some LLMs even achieve human-level performance on the self-modeling task with them as well. The
fact that they do not show the load effects in the self-modeling task that both they and the nonthinking
LLMs show in other-modeling tasks offers suggestive but not conclusive evidence that they are not
relying on online mental state tracking to succeed at that task.
Nonthinking models also show less adeptness with lying than thinking ones. Their lower propensity
for lying may reflect the additional inferential steps required to realize that lying could advance
their ends rather than a deficiency in ToM itself. The fact that even when they do choose to lie only
the most advanced LLMs are able to do so strategically via suppressing gratuitous Tell actions is
suggestive. Strategic lying provides evidence that these LLMs are using causal models for inference,
whereas the majority of nonthinking LLMs’ failure to lie strategically, as with their failure to suppress
unnecessary Tells in the True vs False task, suggests that they may be doing pattern-matching to
memorized stimulus-response mappings.
The reasoning traces that thinking models produce can be quite long, and often involve reiteration
of the facts presented in the scenario and the game rules, and sometimes step through descriptions
of updates to the mental states of themselves and the other players. Having this additional text in
context seems to be critical for allowing the models to determine correct behavior. It may be that this
is simply due to the generated text eventually triggering the right memorized mappings. However,
our finding that taking the first- rather than second-person perspective is associated with success in
the self-modeling task suggests that there may be some real self-modeling being triggered as well.
We look forward to more detailed analysis of these traces in the future.
Given the fluency with which even older, nonthinking LLMs can tell stories or describe situations that
seem to portray characters making inferences about the beliefs and intentions of others, our results
may seem surprising. One potential explanation is that our paradigm, which involves a fairly detailed
setup and eventful scenarios, is too complex or unclear. We believe that four pieces of evidence argue
against this explanation: 1) event load did not impair these models’ performance, 2) some LLMs
did succeed at most of the tasks, 3) some LLMs with thinking succeeded at all of the tasks, and 4)
our human subjects succeeded at all of the tasks. We believe that the best remaining explanation for
the discrepancy is that our paradigm is exposing real deficiencies in the abilities of LLMs to create
mental models. As Ackerman [2026] found in the context of testing LLM metacognition, requiring
strategic use of internal models by soliciting arbitrary, out-of-distribution behaviors can reveal that
apparent LLM success in cognitive modeling tasks in fact often relies on memorized cues.
Limitations and future research. We present this as a preliminary step towards behavior-based
evaluation of mental-state modeling abilities in LLMs, not as the final word. It would be of interest
to evaluate a wider array of ToM tasks, such as from established benchmarks [Chen et al., 2024],
using this framework. The use of a diverse array of game setups will also help establish the generality
of these findings. We do not claim that LLMs are inherently incapable of accurately representing
their own knowledge states without chain-of-thought, so it will be worthwhile to test new LLMs in
our paradigm as they are released. A further characterization of the strategies that chain-of-thought
affords and the representations underlying them will shed light on what reasoning LLMs can and
can’t do in practice. Finally, it would be of interest to identify the internal mechanisms underlying
LLM performance; Zhu et al. [2024] offers some evidence that probes can identify belief states.
