Abstract.
We investigate the spatial profile of the curvature perturbation generated in
multi-waterfall hybrid inflation models, which are known to produce various topological de-
fects. Using the lattice simulation code STOchastic LAttice Simulation, based on the stochas-
tic formalism of inflation, we analyse six cases by varying the number of waterfall fields n and
the functional form of the inflaton potential (“Quadratic” and “Cubic” cases). Our statistical
analysis shows that the probability density functions (PDFs) and power spectra are broadly
consistent with the so-called stochastic-δN algorithm.
The “Cubic” case also exhibits a
characteristic upper bound in the PDF, as discovered in our previous work, that suppresses
primordial black hole formation while potentially affecting halo formation. Furthermore, we
employ the Euler characteristic as a topological diagnostic tool to identify the structures
of the waterfall fields as well as the curvature perturbation. We find that the topological
defects, such as domain walls (n = 1), cosmic strings (n = 2), and monopoles (n = 3), are
reconnected during inflation into finer structures by the stochastic noise, making their corre-
lation lengths much smaller than the Hubble scale at the critical point of the waterfall phase
transition counterintuitively. The Euler characteristic also implies global structures of the
curvature perturbation for n = 1, though we do not conclude if they are due to the domain
wall, because neither the strings (n = 2) nor monopoles (n = 3) leave such structures. The
global structures of the curvature perturbation will provide a novel probe for the physics of
the early universe.

Introduction
The inflationary paradigm provides a well-established mechanism for solving the horizon
and flatness problems and for generating the primordial perturbations that later grew into
the observed large-scale structure [1–6]. While much attention has traditionally focused on
the nearly scale-invariant, small-amplitude perturbations observed in the cosmic microwave
background (CMB) [7], there is growing interest in inflationary scenarios that produce large
curvature perturbations on small scales, since such perturbations can potentially collapse to
form primordial black hole (PBHs) [8–10] (see also Refs. [11–14] for several reviews), offer a
complementary probe of early Universe physics, and motivate detailed studies of mechanisms
that can amplify curvature perturbations.
Though a single real scalar field is often supposed to drive inflation in a minimal setup,
multiple fields can be relevant in general. Hybrid inflation [15] serves as a representative
example in which multiple scalar fields play essential roles in both driving and terminating
inflation. In this model, the so-called inflaton field governs the slow-roll expansion along a
nearly flat potential valley until it reaches a critical point where the effective mass of the
auxiliary waterfall fields becomes negative. This tachyonic instability marks the onset of the
waterfall phase, during which the waterfall field rapidly rolls down its potential and inflation
comes to an end. Since the dynamics of the waterfall phase depend sensitively on the field
fluctuations at the critical point, the system is intrinsically non-perturbative, often leading
to a significant enhancement of curvature perturbations. Such enhancements can naturally
produce PBHs, particularly in the mild-waterfall regime, where the waterfall stage lasts for
several e-folds [16–26].

## Page 3


For such regimes, stochastic formalism provides a useful complementary description. In
this approach, the dynamics of long-wavelength (IR) modes are effectively described by clas-
sical stochastic equations, which are derived by integrating out the short-wavelength (UV)
modes that continually cross the Hubble horizon [27–36]. The UV modes act as stochastic
noise sourcing the IR fields, leading to a Langevin-type equation for the coarse-grained infla-
ton fields. This formalism captures the effect of quantum fluctuations during inflation and
provides a non-perturbative framework to study the evolution of curvature perturbations on
superHubble scales. In conjunction with the δN approach [37–43], the formalism can calcu-
late the conserved curvature perturbation ζ non-perturbatively as a fluctuation δN in the
stochastic first passage e-folding number N to a certain end surface, known as the stochastic-
δN formalism [44–46]. It enables us to quantitatively estimate the power spectrum of the
curvature perturbation in mild-waterfall hybrid inflation [47, 48].
As a further development, we proposed a real-space lattice simulation of inflation
based on the stochastic formalism with Mizuguchi, dubbed STOchastic LAttice Simula-
tion (STOLAS) [49]. STOLAS is the C++ code that can directly produce three-dimensional
maps of the curvature perturbation. This approach allows us to examine, in a single consis-
tent setup, both the probability distribution of large curvature fluctuations and their spatial
correlations. One main purpose of this work is to clarify the previous stochastic-δN formalism
by comparing its power spectrum with a simple Fourier transform of the lattice result.
The topological configuration of the curvature perturbation is also an interesting subject,
as hybrid inflation is known to form topological defects, such as the domain wall, cosmic
string, and monopole, in general. While these defects are ultimately formed near the end
of inflation, their seeds can be generated gradually during the waterfall transition as field
fluctuations grow. Such inhomogeneous field configurations may influence the structure of
the curvature perturbation.
STOLAS with the δN formalism, a non-perturbative lattice
simulation of the curvature perturbation, can capture this effect, as the other main topic of
this paper.
The paper is organised as follows. In Sec. 2, we review the scheme of STOLAS. Section 3
is devoted to a review of the hybrid inflation scenario and its key features. In Sec. 4, we
present the statistical properties of the curvature perturbation obtained within STOLAS. In
Sec. 5, we investigate the topological feature of the field configuration of the waterfall fields
and the curvature perturbation. Section 6 is devoted to conclusions.

Topological defect
Hybrid inflation is known to be accompanied by topological defects in general [15], as the
waterfall dynamics spontaneously break the global O(n) (Z2 for n = 1) symmetry.
The
models with n = 1, n = 2, and n = 3 lead to the domain wall, cosmic string, and monopole,
respectively.5 These defects correspond to the ψr ∼0 regions, which are not allowed to roll
down toward the potential minimum due to topology. On the other hand, a small ψr is na¨ıvely
expected to result in a large δN. In this section, we investigate whether the topological defect
could also leave an imprint on the large-scale structure of the curvature perturbation.

Conclusions
In this paper, we investigated the spatial profile of the curvature perturbation generated by a
mild-waterfall-type hybrid inflation. Given that this model is known to produce topological
defects, we performed simulations using STOchastic LAttice Simulation (STOLAS), a lattice
simulation code based on the stochastic formalism of inflation, to determine the topological
configuration of the curvature perturbation that would be imprinted by defects. We examined
six cases (“Quadratic” n = 1, 2, 3, and 15, and “Cubic” n = 1 and 2) by varying the number
of waterfall fields n and the functional form of the inflaton potential.

In Sec. 4, we analysed the statistical properties of the curvature perturbation. Our
results for the probability density function (PDF) at δN ≳1 appeared to contradict previous
research [54]. This discrepancy arises because our results are averaged at each lattice point
and thus coarse-grained at the lattice scale, while our previous computation corresponds to
the minimal scale, i.e., the Hubble scale at the end of inflation. We expect that increasing
the resolution will allow STOLAS to reproduce the findings of [54], which leaves for future
work.
For the “Cubic” case, Ref. [54] previously pointed out the existence of the upper
bound in the PDF. We observed the same upper bound also in STOLAS, and it results in a
bunch of regions with ζ around the corresponding maximum value. In such configurations,
regions exceeding δN > 1 do not exist; therefore, primordial black hole (PBH) are seldom
formed.
These structures may affect halo formation, making future N-body simulations
an intriguing direction for research. It would also be interesting to investigate the scalar-
induced gravitational wave (see, e.g., Refs. [61–66]) from such configurations of the curvature
perturbation.
The power spectrum was derived through a direct Fourier transform of the curvature
perturbation. Our results are well fitted using the analytical formula derived in Ref. [48]
based on the stochastic-δN algorithm. Except for deviations caused by slow-roll effects, the
algorithm is consistent with the STOLAS results. While the peak of the power spectrum is
roughly proportional to 1/n in the “Quadratic” case as known in the literature [51, 52, 54],
we observed that the “Cubic” case does not follow this relation because the amplitude of the
power spectrum is mainly determined by the upper bound which does not depend on the
waterfall number n.
In Sec. 5, we employed the Euler characteristic to investigate the topological properties
of the waterfall fields and curvature perturbation. We found it consistent with the interpre-
tation that topological defects emerge according to their respective symmetries at around the
critical point, and then reconnect into finer and finer structures due to the stochastic noise,

Computation of Euler characteristic in Mathematica
We here summarise the specification in calculation of the Euler characteristic with Mathe-

by counting the number of vertices V , edges E, and faces F with χ = V −E + F. A single
cube (case (a) in Fig. 7) returns an Euler characteristic of χ = 8 −12 −6 = 2. An object
containing one hole yields χ = 0, while an object with two holes yields χ = −2. This cor-
responds to the general relation χ = 2 −2g, where g is the genus (number of independent
holes). When two cubes share one full edge, Mathematica returns χ = 3, due to the resulting
counts: V = 14, E = 23, F = 12, which satisfy χ = V −E + F = 3. Even when the
cubes share only one vertex, the Euler characteristic computed is again χ = 3. The value of
