ABSTRACT
Detecting and responding to cyber attacks is increasingly difficult as high-volume, complex net-
work traffic allows threats to remain concealed. While Intrusion Detection Systems (IDSs) identify
anomalous behavior, Attack Graphs (AGs) serve as the primary threat model for analyzing attacker
strategies and informing any response. Despite the conceptual connection being recognized in early
research, the field of AG and IDS integration lacks a common structure.
This paper presents the first systematic analysis of AG-IDS integration, reviewing a 73 comprehen-
sive works in literature. We introduce a novel taxonomy revealing that current research is dominated
by specialized, single-purpose integrations—such as using AGs to filter IDS false positives or using
IDS alerts to prune AGs. Our analysis highlights a critical gap: the absence of a unifying framework
that treats IDSs and AGs as a cohesive, integrated system.
To address this gap, we propose a formal AG-IDS lifecycle. This framework establishes a contin-
uous feedback loop where IDSs refine the accuracy of AG models, and those updated models, in
turn, enhance IDS detection capabilities. We provide a proof-of-concept implementation demon-
strating the practical advantages of this lifecycle for threat detection and incident response. Finally,
we conclude by elaborating on significant opportunities for future development within the AG-IDS
domain.

and Attack Graphs Integration

Introduction
Attack detection and response are two essential phases for securing computer networks [92, 122, 19]. Detection in-
volves monitoring system states, identifying anomalous activities, and assessing their impact on the network. In this
context, Intrusion Detection System (IDS) are the state-of-the-art tools for identifying and alerting anomalous behav-
ior [57, 65, 51, 121]. On the other hand, responding to potential cyber risks in the network involves reacting to detected
incidents, defining mitigation strategies, and updating response plans. Effective response relies on threat modeling to
anticipate potential attacks [132]. Attack Graph (AG) [55, 95] supports response by modeling possible attackers’
strategies in a graph-based structure that enables the analysis of attack vectors and the planning of countermeasures in

distributed computer networks [94, 58, 16]. The potential to use these techniques in combination is widely recognized
in the literature [31, 87, 88, 85, 105]. IDSs can identify individual anomalies but lack the ability to correlate them
across nodes, whereas AG captures the relationships among vulnerabilities and attack steps, enabling the reconstruc-
tion and early detection of complex, multi-stage intrusions [21, 98]. With this motivation, different research efforts
combined these techniques to correlate alerts [118], learn attacker strategies [90], or plan optimal countermeasure in
complex networks [114, 97]. For instance, AGs often suffer from scalability issues due to the combinatorial number of
strategies an attacker may exploit in a network [107, 95, 134]. In this case, research focused on addressing such issues
by leveraging intrusion alerts [143, 52, 45, 79]. Similarly, IDSs must process increasing volumes of traffic, resulting
in high false positive rates [89]. Hence, research focused on addressing the problem of false positive reduction by
leveraging AGs [50, 103, 21, 31]. Despite clear benefits, efforts to combine AGs and IDSs remain highly fragmented.
Existing approaches often tackle isolated problems – such as reducing IDS false positives or improving AG scalability
– without a unified vision for deployment in dynamic, real-world networks. Integration is further complicated by het-
erogeneous IDS technologies, diverse AG models, and inconsistent alert semantics. To address these gaps, this paper
provides the first comprehensive Systematization of Knowledge (SoK) on AG–IDS integration, categorizing existing
methods, and identifying opportunities for advancing adaptive and scalable solutions.
Beyond consolidating a fragmented field, our SoK critically evaluates the strengths and limitations of combining AG
and IDS and reveals how far their integration has truly been explored. To guide our systematization, we pose the
following research questions that enable identifying a novel taxonomy:
RQ1 – “How are AGs and IDSs coupled together in existing approaches?”
RQ2 – “To what end are the current approaches combining AGs and IDSs?”
Based on these questions, we systematized 73 papers, whose analysis resulted in the identification of four categories
of AG-IDS combination approaches, namely: (i) IDS-based AG generation approaches (AG|IDS): constructing
meaningful AG(s) from IDS output; (ii) AG-integrated IDS approaches (IDS[AG]): embedding AG to optimize
the IDS detection pipeline; (iii) AG-based IDS refinement approaches (IDS →AG): leveraging AG to validate or
enhance the IDS outputs; and (iv) hybrid approaches: combining any two of the above-mentioned categories.
Our systematization reveals that hybrid AG-IDS integration approaches offer promising avenues for enabling con-
tinuous, adaptive threat detection and response. However, most of the analyzed works typically assume detection
environments where integration occurs once and remains unchanged during the detection process. This assumption
fails to resemble real-world computer networks, where security threats continuously evolve, and network characteris-
tics change. To address this gap, we envision a novel AG-IDS lifecycle that supports continuous updates and iterative
refinement to support prompt responses and accurate detection. The term lifecycle aptly captures the ongoing, struc-
tured process through which these techniques evolve in response to emerging cybersecurity threats. We illustrate the
proposed lifecycle through a proof-of-concept, designed to elucidate the potential of its key stages and core function-
alities. In summary, we contribute:
• a comprehensive systematization of AG-IDS integration approaches, covering 73 papers;
• the definition of a new taxonomy based on integration methods and objectives;
• a novel AG-IDS lifecycle, showcasing its envisioned effectiveness experimentally; and
• the systematization of the current research gaps and opportunities.
The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 introduces the preliminary concepts and notations on
IDSs and AGs, as well as the related work. The systematization methodology and obtained taxonomy are presented
in Sections 3.1 and 3.2. Sections 3.3 to 3.6 delve into the core categorization of our systematization, presenting each
category of approaches. Sections 4 and 5 expand on the categorization, by envisioning an AG-IDS lifecycle, which is
implemented as a proof-of-concept. Section 6 lists the opportunities for future research directions that emerge from
our systematization. Finally, Section 7 provides the conclusions and outlooks.

2.1.1
Intrusion Detection Systems. An Intrusion Detection System (IDS) monitors computer networks, devices,
and data traffic to detect known malicious activities or policy violations [57]. Traditionally, IDSs are categorized by
their detection technique and monitoring scope.
Detection perspective
IDSs are either Signature-based Intrusion Detection System (SIDS) or Anomaly-based In-
trusion Detection System (AIDS) [71]. SIDSs rely on pattern-matching to identify known threats using signature
databases [49, 61], such as specific malware code sequences [54]. AIDSs detect anomalies modeling normal sys-
tem behavior and flagging significant deviations [14, 71], using statistical, knowledge-based, or data-driven methods.
Given the rise of Machine Learning (ML)-based approaches, we further classify IDSs into four types: (i) ML-based
AIDSs, (ii) ML-based SIDSs, (iii) non-ML AIDSs, and (iv) non-ML SIDSs.
Monitoring process
IDSs also differ by their placement and scope. Network-level Intrusion Detection Systems
(NIDSs) monitor network-wide traffic from strategic points – typically near firewalls – to detect external threats [9, 60].
Host-level Intrusion Detection Systems (HIDSs), installed on individual devices – e.g., laptops, routers –, monitor local
activity such as file access and device-specific traffic [72]. NIDSs offer broad visibility, while HIDSs provide fine-
grained monitoring; combining both yields comprehensive protection. Despite advances in targeted IDS—e.g., for
Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) [108, 76, 129, 26], they still suffer from false positives/negatives due to limited
host-level correlation [87], hindering holistic threat analysis.
2.1.2
Attack Graphs. An Attack Graph (AG) is a graph-based model representing potential attack paths an adversary
might follow by exploiting sequences of vulnerabilities to compromise a network. It serves as a comprehensive threat
model supporting tasks such as cyber risk assessment [137, 93], network hardening [29, 63], and threat detection [87,
96]. The core of AGs lies in the interconnection of vulnerability exploits, which define multi-step attacks [82]. Based
on the representation of the attacker strategies and their relationships, four main AG models are identified in the state
of the art taxonomy [55]:
• State-based AGs represents network state nodes indicating networked software configuration, attacker privi-
leges, network performance measures, etc.
• Vulnerability-based AGs focus on vulnerability exploits.
• Host-based AGs represent hosts and topological information of the network.
• Attack Scenario-based AGs represent attack plans and strategies composed of coordinated attacker actions.
Research primarily focuses on AG generation—i.e., computing possible attack paths [137] since it remains a key
challenge due to the combinatorial number of attack paths in the network size and vulnerabilities [64]. As a result,
applying AGs in dynamic, real-time, and heterogeneous environments remains an open research problem [95].
2.2
Related Work
With the growing interest in supporting detection and analysis for network security, different studies have reviewed the
integration of AG and IDS. From a modeling standpoint, recent works survey graph-based approaches, emphasizing
the potential of AGs for alert correlation [63, 13]. In particular, Almazrouei et al. [16] provide a comprehensive review
of AGs for vulnerability assessment in Internet of Things (IoT) networks, highlighting their role in intrusion detection.
Focusing on detection, Kovavcevic et al. [59] review techniques for attack scenario detection but lack coverage of
diverse AG models. Capobianco et al. [31] explore the use of AGs for intrusion detection, particularly in computing
attack surfaces, states, and actions. Overall, existing surveys remain fragmented, each addressing specific aspects of
the AG-IDS interplay without offering a unified systematization. Additionally, some works address multi-step attack
detection and its challenges – such as modeling, automation, dataset design, and reproducibility [87, 100] – but do not
fully explore the AG-IDS combination in this context, as this SoK aims to do.

3.1
Methodology
To systematize the literature, we defined a set of queries to be executed on widely used bibliographic search engines.
The queries incorporate the following keyword combinations (∧is AND, ∨is OR):
• ‘intrusion detection’ ∧(‘attacks graph’ ∨‘attack path’)
• (‘anomaly detection’ ∨‘alert’) ∧(‘attack graph’ ∨‘attack path’)
• ‘attack graph generation’ ∧(‘anomalies’ ∨‘alerts’)
• (‘attack mitigation’ ∨‘secure network’) ∧(‘attack graph’ ∨‘attack path’)
As far as bibliographic search engines are concerned, we leverage ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, Springer Link,
Science Direct/Scopus, and aggregators like Google Scholar and DBLP. For each search engine and query pair, we
reviewed the first three pages of results. Our focus on top-ranked results prioritizes high-impact work. The research
reached saturation after the first three pages of all digital libraries, where no additional relevant papers appeared
in any case. This first search process led us to 102 initial articles. For each result, the three equally contributing
authors inspect the title, abstract, and introduction to determine whether the paper addresses the topic of AG-IDS
integration. We classify each paper according to three disjoint circumstances: (i) Primary work, which proposes a
methodology for integrating AGs and IDSs, (ii) Secondary work, which surveys literature related to either AG, IDS,
or both, (iii) Unrelated work which does not pertain to either AG or IDS, and is thus excluded from our analysis.
Notably, secondary works identified in step (ii) served as valuable sources for discovering additional primary works.
We recursively explored their bibliographies to identify further relevant contributions. In this phase, we particularly
relied on comprehensive surveys such as [16, 63, 13, 59, 87], which we recognize as significant references in the AG
and IDS domains (see Section 2.2). While informative, these secondary works typically focus on only one of the two
topics – either AG or IDS – and often lack detailed schematics outlining integration aspects. To refine our selection of
primary works, we assessed each paper based on the following questions:
• “Are the IDS specifics – e.g., detection engine, scale, monitoring process – clearly described?”
• “Are the AG specifics – e.g., category, scale – clearly described?”
• “Is the integration purpose explicitly stated?”
Papers that failed to provide clear answers to all three questions were excluded from further analysis due to insuffi-
cient specificity. This selection process ultimately yielded 73 articles, which are examined in this SoK. Each of the
equally contributing authors deeply analyzed one of the categories (see Section 3.2). Iterative sessions were organized
for further discussions and presentations of the findings of each category. Finally, any disagreements regarding the
classification or inclusion of a paper were resolved through majority voting among the authors.
Disclaimer: The research methodology presented herein applies exclusively to the integration of AG and IDS.
Consequently, literature proposing novel advancements in standalone IDS—such as provenance-graph-based sys-
tems [32, 53, 128]—is considered outside the scope of this work. Furthermore, research focusing on generic graph-
based solutions (e.g., provenance graphs or Graph Neural Network (GNN) architectures [127, 36]) that do not align
with the specific AG definition (see Section 2.1.2) is similarly excluded from this study.
3.2
Taxonomy
Analyzing the set of papers identified using our search methodology, a clear categorization of AG-IDS combination
approaches naturally emerged, based on the scope of integration. More specifically, we classify these approaches
according to how AGs and IDSs are combined (RQ1) to achieve a particular objective (RQ2). Accordingly, we iden-
tify four main categories: a) IDS-based AG generation, denoted as AG|IDS; and, b) AG-integrated IDS, denoted as
IDS[AG]; c) AG-based IDS refinement, denoted as IDS →AG; d) Hybrid approaches, representing works that span
two categories. These categories reflect different strategies for combining AGs and IDSs, aiming to enhance attack de-
tection capabilities, address scalability challenges, or improve system reliability. Figure 1 illustrates the classification
of the surveyed papers according to this taxonomy. Subsequently, we provide a definition of each category.
AG|IDS
Approaches in the AG|IDS category use IDS outputs to generate AGs and compute potential attack
paths inferred from intrusion alerts. These methods support challenging tasks like alert correlation and vulnerability
analysis, with the generated AG providing useful support for both. Techniques employed to generate AG from IDS
alerts include data mining [138, 15], causal knowledge [10, 43], Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) techniques [28, 66],
and probability computation [108].

IDS[AG]
The IDS[AG] category encompasses approaches that incorporate AGs or their underlying knowledge
– expressed in any form – into the IDS inference pipeline. These methods aim to enhance the detection capabilities
or reliability of IDS systems. Techniques in this category integrate the AG’s network exposure knowledge – or a
relevant subportion of the AG – primarily for correlating IDS alerts with attack paths [73, 133, 28], refining IDS
detection parameters based on AG data [104, 11, 111], or leveraging the AG as a runtime detection module directly
[39, 130, 56]. The AG may be either predefined or dynamically generated during system execution or optimization.
IDS →AG
Approaches in the IDS →AG category utilize AGs to refine, analyze, or validate the predictions
made by IDS components. Common objectives include improving detection accuracy [78, 50], correlating intrusion
alerts for comprehensive analysis [112, 48, 76], and enabling adequate response based on alerts [35, 117, 27]. Sim-
ilarly to IDS[AG], here the AG may be either known a-priori or dynamically constructed during system execution.
Generally, the AG is assumed to be complete and reliable, serving as a basis for verifying or analyzing the output of
the IDS.
Hybrid approaches
As illustrated in Figure 1, the three categories are not entirely distinct. Different works propose
hybrid approaches that address multiple aspects of AG-IDS integration. Some methods generate AGs to represent sys-
tem states and use them to optimize the IDS detection pipeline or refine its output to catch the potential of AG|IDS,
IDS[AG], and IDS →AG. However, no existing work comprehensively integrates all three categories (see Fig-
ure 1). Such a gap highlights both the diversity of challenges tackled by each category – such as IDS detection accuracy
and AG scalability – and the complexity of designing multi-layered systems that integrate AGs and IDSs. It also un-
derscores the need for further investigation into the strengths and limitations of current approaches, paving the way
for future innovations in AG-IDS integration. Accordingly, in this paper, we propose to fill this gap by envisioning a
continuous AG-IDS integration lifecycle in Section 4 and implement it in Section 5 as a proof-of-concept.

3.3
IDS-based AG Generation (AG|IDS)
We classify as AG|IDS those approaches that leverage IDS outputs to construct AGs and represent potential attack
paths inferred from intrusion alerts. Our systematization identified 46 papers in this category. Figure 2 summarizes
the key statistical findings, while Table 1 depicts the detailed systematization for each paper.
RSQ1 Integration. Most approaches that generate AGs leverage integration with IDSs primarily for alert correlation.
IDSs typically produce an extensive number of alerts, many of which are inaccurate or redundant, making them
difficult to utilize for improving detection. Alert correlation mitigates this issue by converting noisy, low-level alerts
into structured, causally linked, high-level events. Once correlated, these alerts become suitable for constructing AGs
that represent complex, multi-step attacks. Common approaches for alert correlation employ data mining [69, 138, 15],
modeling of causal knowledge [10, 142, 43], CTI techniques [112, 28], and probability computation [108, 131].
Among these works, many integrate IDS into AG generation to support response. Such works identify active attack
locations in the network [101] and provide assessment for suitable mitigation actions [102, 115, 28]. A third relevant
purpose in this category deals with vulnerability analysis. In fact, different works generate AG from IDS to handle
missed detections through the analysis of network vulnerability dependencies [91, 46] and reveal the network security
situation [17, 120].
Finally, only a limited number of studies address real-time detection, probably due to AG generation suffering from
scalability limitations, making it hard to adjust for real-time tasks. Among them, Holgado et al. [46] design algo-
rithmic approaches to generate AGs efficiently and leverage them for prompt detection, while Ahmadian Ramaki
and Rasoolzadegan [10] design a three-phase alert correlation framework, which processes the generated alerts in real
time.
RSQ2 IDS Type. Most approaches in this category (69.5%) rely on NIDS, as their operational model aligns closely
with the structural characteristics of AGs. For example, many works leverage the IP addresses from NIDS alerts to
represent nodes of the AG and edges to represent possible attack steps between them [139, 86, 129, 43]. Surprisingly, a
large portion of the works in this category (24%) do not rely on strict assumptions about the monitoring process – i.e.,
NIDS or HIDS – of the IDS used [120, 80, 52, 140]. This flexibility stems from the ability to extract key information
– such as affected nodes, services, and alert timestamps – from virtually any IDS. Finally, only a small number of
studies employ HIDS for AG generation [131, 28, 78]. These works posit that attack structures function as abstract

Concerning the detection perspective, the majority of works consider SIDS [125, 15, 45, 24] as signatures align well
with the formal structure of AGs, facilitating their usage for AG generation. For example, different works incorporate
probabilistic methods such as Markov Models [86, 20] or Bayesian Networks [81, 133], and may also use semantic
analysis via ontologies [106]. In contrast, AIDS [101, 74, 28, 102] are less frequently used, probably due to the
semantic gap between the AIDS output and the input requirements of an AG. For example, in AIDS, an anomaly
may be an abstract statistical deviation (e.g., unusual traffic) that hardly map to a specific AG node. Nonetheless,
these methods emphasize the topological structure of AGs, capturing asset and vulnerability dependencies. Here, alert
correlation supports topology-aware clustering and downstream ML tasks [74, 102].
Lastly, detection-agnostic techniques are rarer and apply alert aggregation to correlate events and identify relationships
indicative of a single attack [138, 108, 120], which then inform AG construction.
RSQ3 AG Type. The various models defined in the AG taxonomy [55] are considered with comparable frequency
for AG generation using IDS. This observation is significant as it suggests that existing approaches address multiple
perspectives of AG modeling. Nevertheless, a slightly greater proportion of works focus on generating attack scenario-
based [10, 18, 15, 129, 69, 90, 44] and state-based [120, 74, 131, 84, 46, 43] AGs. These models are natural choices: the
former emerge from alert correlation, while the latter facilitate mapping between IDS signatures and AG states. While
promising, these approaches overlook the complexity of the correlation algorithms they rely upon, thus hindering their
effectiveness in improving AG scalability.
Other works generate host-based AGs [68, 139, 70, 80, 101] as they focus on modeling the attacker’s behavior at
the host level. Differently, only a handful of works consider vulnerability-based AGs [43, 91] due to the difficulty
of extracting vulnerability information from IDS alerts. These approaches mainly address vulnerability analysis,
foreseeing the vulnerability information (e.g., CVSS metrics of the different CVEs) as an important integration of
IDS alerts. This disparity suggests that while alert correlation is well-explored [17, 86], vulnerability modeling and
prediction for scalable AG generation remain underexplored. Finally, recent efforts in attack path prediction [23, 144]
show promise and warrant further investigation, although inaccurate scalability assumptions may hinder real-world
applicability.
RSQ4 Threats. A notable observation is that AG generation is commonly used to assess cyber risks associated with
multi-step attacks. However, a significant portion of the literature relies on the DARPA2000 dataset [129, 90, 18,
74, 125, 15, 46], which contains only DDoS attack instances. This limits the applicability of the corresponding
approaches to non-DDoS scenarios. Moreover, DARPA2000 lacks network topology information, which hinders the
construction of host-based AGs. As a result, most models in this category define either attack scenario-based or state-
based AGs, further highlighting the scarcity of suitable datasets. To compensate, some studies rely on simulated or
custom datasets [101, 68, 120, 142], despite AG generation typically requiring inputs such as reachability graphs and
vulnerability inventories. This reliance on limited or synthetic data impairs the fair validation and comparison of AG
generation methods, ultimately affecting their reproducibility and applicability in real-world environments.
RSQ5 Application. Our analysis reveals that only half of the approaches (52%) generate AGs without specifying
a concrete application scenario [84, 142, 10, 90, 74, 46, 91]. These works are in line with AG modeling, which is
intended to be a general model by definition. Therefore, here generality is a strength of these approaches.
Surprisingly, the other half of the works consider scenario-specific information to generate AGs. For example, some
solutions focus on IoT [28, 17] and smart grids [133, 110, 112], mainly due to the availability of DDoS-related
datasets in these domains. The reasoning behind these application-specific works is that customizing AGs for a specific
application may produce better results due to the consideration of contextual implications of real-world deployments.
This specificity may be relevant for vulnerabilities and attack vectors that can vary significantly across domains.
Therefore, investigating context-specific characteristics of IDSs and vulnerabilities in greater depth, to build scenario-
specific AGs, represents a promising research direction.
RSQ6 ML. Most AG|IDS approaches do not employ ML for AG generation. Among those that do, two main ML
models are employed. Markov Chain (MC) are used to model probabilistic transitions between alerts [86, 46, 40]. MCs
are particularly well-suited for generating state-based AGs since the states of a MC can be easily mapped to the states of
an AG. On the other hand, Neural Networks (NNs) are particularly used for AG generation. Framing AG generation as
an attack path prediction problem has shown promise in improving scalability, where popular models include learning
from intrusion alerts to predict sequences of compromised devices, with Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) being

3.4
AG-integrated IDS (IDS[AG])
We classify as IDS[AG] those approaches that leverage AG as a model to support the detection process of IDS. Based
on our systematization, we identified 19 papers that fall into this category. Figure 3 summarizes the key statistical
findings, while Table 1 depicts the detailed systematization for each paper.
RSQ1 Integration. The foundational idea behind approaches in this category is to harness AG as a means to modify
how the IDS brings about its decision process, aiming at reducing false alarms and improving precision by leveraging
AG’s ability to comprehensively represent network exposure. This integration enhances the detection capabilities,
reliability, and transparency of the IDS. Our analysis reveals that the AGs can be incorporated into the IDS for:
• correlating IDS alerts with the AG to ensure the existence of an attack path and define an IDS that flags an
alarm only if an attack path is exploited (we refer to this sub-class with alert correlation) [133, 28, 73];
• modifying the detection rules or learning process that build the IDS, for instance, generating a new detection
rule from a specific attack path of the AG (we refer to this subclass as detection refinement) [111, 21, 67]; or
• leveraging the AG in real-time as a detection module – modeling multi-step intrusions – where AG states are
verified based on network/hosts information and alerts are raised only whenever an attack path is complete
(we refer to this sub-class as runtime detection) [10, 130, 39, 56].
Alert correlation approaches are the most popular solutions since establishing a mapping between IDS alerts and/or
signatures with the AG is relatively feasible in simple scenarios, and the mapping can be made efficiently. On the other
hand, detection refinement is underanalyzed, as it requires constructing a methodology to design detection techniques

from attack path instances, which may be cumbersome over a complex network. However, detection refinement
may also represent the most effective strategy if used properly; thus, we stress the need to conduct a more thorough
investigation. Finally, runtime detection systems are quite popular as they enable leveraging the AG as a detection
framework, but suffer scalability issues as they require large graph traversal and state updates.
RSQ2 IDS Type. NIDS represents the most commonly adopted detection model, appearing in more than 90% of
the reviewed works [27, 111, 37, 130, 133, 46]. These approaches differ depending on the AG used and on how
they combine its knowledge into the IDS. For example, NIDS-based runtime detection approaches leverage the AG
as a standalone detection tool and filter out unwanted IDS network states to raise alerts only when an attack path is
complete [130, 56, 27, 37]. Meanwhile, NIDS-based detection refinement systems modify the IDS inner principles
– e.g., detection rules –, matching one or more AG’s attack paths to a detection principle [67, 103]. The overly
reliance on NIDS can be attributed to several factors: (i) while AGs can correlate with both network-level (e.g.,
attacker lateral movements) and host-level (e.g., privilege escalation or account-to-account movements) steps, the most
common AG models are primarily built upon network connectivity and vulnerability chains. This network-centricity
makes correlation with NIDS more direct and straightforward for initial integration efforts compared to mapping the
intricate, host-specific events tracked by HIDS. (ii) AG-integrated IDS was initially proposed to enhance detection at
the network level, which naturally encouraged a focus on NIDS. And, (iii) most AG-integrated IDS approaches rely
on available off-the-shelf IDSs like Snort2 and Suricata3 due to their ease of use and flexibility.
As a consequence of (iii), the majority of AG-integrated IDS approaches rely on SIDSs to easily map alerts and their
signatures to the corresponding nodes or states of an AG. Several of these approaches correlate SIDS alerts to the AG
nodes to verify the state of attack progress and construct a runtime detection scheme, differing by the alert correlation
procedure and detection scheme used [136, 10, 133]. Meanwhile, AIDSs are mostly used in systems that leverage
ML to aggregate alerts and refine the detection or define it at runtime [111, 119], since ML is well-suited for both
correlating alerts – e.g., via clustering or classification – and constructing anomaly detection schemes over the classes
of aggregated alerts.
RSQ3 AG Type. Several different AG types [55] are considered for AG-integrated IDS. State-based [136, 10, 27,
111] and host-based AGs [104, 39, 133, 28] represent popular solutions since they naturally enable mapping with
IDS signatures and/or alerts. Similarly, attack scenario-based AGs are largely used in approaches relying on alert
correlation as they assume that similar alerts map to similar attack scenarios [103, 10, 67]. These approaches mostly
rely on runtime detection and refinement to quickly identify the activation of practical attack scenarios and block them.
Lastly, given the difficulty of mapping vulnerability information to IDS alerts and vice-versa, only a handful of works
falling in this category build on vulnerability-based AGs [37, 11].
A critical aspect of IDS[AG] models is their usage of AGs at runtime—for instance, to detect intrusions by verifying
the completion of attack paths or to map alerts over the states of the AG. This implies that current approaches assume
the feasibility of attack path computation at runtime. However, most are validated on simplified network topolo-
gies [28, 33, 11]. Among 19 analyzed papers, only three explicitly support AGs with over 100 nodes [111, 103, 136].
Given that real-world detection must operate over high-volume data streams, scalability remains a key challenge for
deploying IDS[AG] systems.
RSQ4 Threats. Many approaches are agnostic to specific threats [56, 130, 133], focusing instead on aggregating alerts
and evaluating detection performance at a network-wide level. This lack of specificity limits the applicability of these
approaches since, in practice, IDSs are typically tailored to specific threat models. Among the works that do specify
attack types, the most common are DDoS [46, 103], DoS [28], and multi-step attacks in general [67, 21, 104]. These
multi-step attacks benefit significantly from AG-based modeling, as early detection enables timely mitigation.
The datasets used for evaluation support these findings. Most studies rely on: (i) well-known DDoS and DoS datasets
such as DARPA2000 [103, 136, 11, 73, 46], (ii) custom-built datasets [56, 27, 111, 27, 133, 104], or (iii) simula-
tion-based environments [28, 21, 67, 39]. This reliance on narrow or synthetic datasets raises concerns about the
generalizability of current frameworks, as their effectiveness may not extend beyond the specific conditions under
which they were evaluated.

sider [56, 130, 74, 46]. The root cause for such a trend is to be found on the AG notion being fundamentally designed
as a general threat model, where generality is a key theoretical strength. However, in the context of operational inte-
gration with IDSs, this generality can become a practical constraint: AG that lack domain-specific, real-time context

AG-based IDS Refinement (IDS →AG)

We identified 29 papers that fall into this category. Figure 4 summarizes the key statistical findings, while Table 1
depicts the detailed systematization for each paper.
RSQ1 Integration. Integrating post-hoc checks of IDS alerts onto an AG is vital for transforming raw alerts into
actionable intelligence. This process facilitates detection refinement by validating and contextualizing suspicious
events against known attack paths, enables the creation of adequate responses by revealing the attacker’s stage

and objective in the attack chain, and ultimately enhances runtime detection by confirming that a series of iso-
lated alerts represents a true, ongoing multi-stage campaign.
The systematization in this category reveals that
one of the most straightforward applications of AGs in this context is the interpretation of the large volume of
alerts produced by IDSs. In particular, alert correlation plays a crucial role in accurately identifying multi-step at-
tacks [112, 76, 48, 104, 18, 43, 20, 110, 105, 52, 102], which often require the integration of information from
different sources. Moreover, alert correlation enables network administrators to reconstruct attack paths or understand
the broader attack behavior [48, 104, 11, 125, 101, 126], thereby facilitating the deployment of appropriate mitigation
and response strategies. Importantly, alerts themselves serve as a valuable source of information that can be leveraged
for the detection refinement, thus improving detection accuracy and reducing false positives [76, 118, 18, 126, 135].
Another prominent use of AG in the context of IDS refinement is the enhancement of intrusion detection reliability.
Some approaches introduce techniques that leverage AGs based on causal relations, not only improving detection
accuracy but also enabling the prediction of future attacker behaviors [50].
RSQ2 IDS Type. The majority of papers in this category – i.e., 73% – adopt SIDS, mainly leveraging Snort [105,
112, 48], RealSecure [135, 11] and Amilyzer [117]. The large adoption of SIDS can be attributed to their ease of
implementation, fast detection, and transparent actionable alerts. Therefore, it is relatively simple to interface IDS
alerts to AG post-hoc to determine if the alert fits into any pre-calculated attack path or attack scenario. These post-
hoc checks on alerts enable actionable intelligence, including detection refinement, adequate response for the raised
alerts, and runtime detection, as elaborated earlier. On the other hand, only a limited number of approaches adopt
AIDSs [27, 50, 101, 102]. This mainly happens due to the challenges of integrating anomaly-based detection with
the formal model of AGs. Meanwhile, all of them leverage ML for the alert correlation, detection refinement, or even
runtime detection.
Lastly, from the monitoring perspective, the vast majority of contributions – i.e., 90% – of this category, target NIDSs.
The reasons behind such an imbalance are similar to the ones expressed for the IDS[AG] category in Section 3.4
(RSQ2).
RSQ3 AG Type. The majority of the works in this category adopt attack scenario-based AGs [135, 76, 50, 119, 18,
110, 112, 78, 52, 77]. Such a finding is mainly attributed to the alert correlation methodologies that these works
propose, for which the AG maps raised IDS alerts to known attack scenarios. Additionally, such a mapping enables
fast and efficient detection refinement [101, 20] and response [114, 113, 105]. For a similar reasoning, state-based
AGs are highly adopted [38, 118, 48, 35, 27, 125, 20, 102, 114]. Analyzing IDS alerts with vulnerability-based AGs
has also been quite well-adopted. The preparation of an adequate response and detection refinement benefits from an
extensive vulnerability-based AG. Lastly, host-based AGs [104, 105, 101] are less adopted than other types of AG for
this category primarily because of their construction, collection, and maintenance complexity.
RSQ4 Threats. AGs are particularly effective for modeling DDoS [101, 135, 11, 125, 126, 76] and multi-step at-
tacks [50, 48, 76, 112, 18], as they capture the sequential and distributed nature of such threats. Consequently, many
studies focus on these attack types. Furthermore, a significant number of studies are agnostic to the specific attack
type [35, 37, 118, 38], building systems on top of general-purpose IDSs without addressing potential compatibility
issues. Commonly used datasets include DARPA2000 [135, 11, 125, 126, 76, 18, 77], which provides communication
traces but lacks detailed network topology and vulnerability data. This raises concerns about the representativeness
of such a dataset for evaluating AG-based IDS refinement. Many also rely on simulations or custom datasets, further
limiting the reliability and reproducibility of their findings. Notably, the literature lacks a systematic analysis of how
IDS outputs can be semantically prepared for AG-based processing, which is a critical gap for practical deployment.
RSQ5 Application. A considerable amount of IDS →AG approaches are applied in critical infrastructure domains,
such as Cyber Physical System (CPS) [117, 43, 105, 102], industrial control systems [50], smart grids [101, 110, 112],
and Advanced Metering Infrastructure (AMI) [27], where the volume of alerts is particularly high. Other applications
include cloud computing [76, 33, 114] and Software-Defined Networks (SDN) [37, 78]. However, many studies do not
specify the application domain [35, 48, 118, 38, 135, 126, 119, 104, 11, 18, 125, 20, 77, 113], limiting the contextual
relevance of their findings.
RSQ6 ML. Only a small subset of the 29 reviewed papers incorporate ML into IDS →AG approaches. Among
these, NNs and Artificial Immune System (AIS) are the most common. Bendiab et al. [27] classify network traffic
using ResNet50, Mobilenet, and SOINN, while other works apply Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) on multi-device
time series for attack detection [50], propose a hybrid GCN-LSTM model for anomaly detection [101, 102], or use
AIS-based techniques like negative and clonal selection [78, 77, 76]. These approaches primarily use ML to enhance
IDS performance, rather than to support the integration with AGs. This highlights a promising research direction:

3.6
Hybrid Approaches
A key finding of our systematization is that the majority of the studied approaches fall into one of the categories
proposed in the taxonomy. Nonetheless, a smaller portion of existing works propose hybrid approaches that span more
than one of the defined categories. These approaches aim to either design effective detection or develop comprehensive
frameworks by combining the strengths of different integration paradigms (e.g., to provide real-time detection and
response [27]).
In the following, we refer to X + Y approaches as those that simultaneously adopt principles from both category X
and Y —e.g., AG|IDS + IDS[AG] considers both AG generation (AG|IDS) and the AG-based IDS (IDS[AG]).
We analyze each hybrid category, focusing on the specific advantages and limitations of hybridization. Let us note that
specific answers to research subquestions (RSQ1-RSQ6) are included in the previous analyses (Sections 3.3 to 3.5)
and are thus are ignored here to avoid redundancy.
3.6.1
AG|IDS + IDS[AG]. The collection of hybrid approaches combining AG|IDS with IDS[AG] primarily fo-
cuses on the dynamic evaluation of known vulnerabilities and system entry points [10, 73]. In these approaches, AGs
are constructed using AG|IDS principles – i.e., from IDS alerts – and are updated whenever an alarm is triggered
and linked to a previously unknown vulnerability [46, 133]. This enables dynamic verification and extension of the
system’s vulnerability set, reducing the efforts of costly expert-driven analysis. Furthermore, AG|IDS + IDS[AG]
approaches also update the detection scheme in response to changes in the AG [28]. This ensures that newly discov-
ered vulnerabilities – identified through IDS alerts – are incorporated into future iterations of the intrusion detection
process, preventing them from being overlooked or forgotten.
It is worth noticing that approaches in this category rely mostly on static SIDS, suggesting opportunities for ML-
enhanced and adaptive correlation models, such as GNN or Reinforcement Learning. Existing temporal models like
MC and Bayesian Networks are outdated, opening the door to modern probabilistic and sequence-learning methods for
multi-step attack detection. Another gap is that most evaluations depend on old or simulated datasets due to the diffi-
culty of retrieving datasets for both detection and AG generation. This highlights the need for realistic benchmarking,
especially related to resource-constrained environments.
3.6.2
AG|IDS + IDS→AG. Similar to the previous category, the collection of hybrid approaches combining
AG|IDS with IDS →AG primarily focuses on the dynamic evaluation of known vulnerabilities and system en-
try points [43, 78, 101]. Reasonably, they are more than the ones in AG|IDS + IDS[AG] (almost twice), as AGs
are more wired towards improving the detection accuracy by design, rather than optimizing the detection mechanism
itself. Most of AG|IDS + IDS→AG approaches focus on updating the alert correlation schemes used to verify the
alerts generated by the IDS [102, 52, 112, 20]. As such, these systems function as static or dynamic verification tools,
ensuring that the alert validation process remains aligned with known vulnerabilities in the networks.
A potential drawback of these approaches is that they are agnostic to the IDS optimization process, possibly causing
susceptibility to IDS obsolescence [141, 22]. In particular, the IDS used to update the AG may fail to detect exploits
that arise from the combination of multiple, previously unknown vulnerabilities. To mitigate the obsolescence risks, it
is essential to update not only the alert verification and correlation mechanisms but also the IDS itself whenever a new
vulnerability is discovered.
3.6.3
IDS[AG] + IDS→AG. The collection of hybrid approaches combining IDS[AG] and IDS →AG aims to
provide comprehensive detection and AG-informed responses to detected anomalies. Many of these works verify

whether the detection outcomes produced by the IDS, when informed by AG, satisfy the constraints defined by the
AG itself [104, 37, 66]. When the AG model is introduced during the IDS optimization phase, it is essential to ensure
that information provided by AG is not lost or overwritten during the learning process. This challenge becomes
even more pronounced when the underlying detection mechanism is ML-based, as safely incorporating structured
knowledge into learning models is non-trivial [119, 27, 66]. An interesting aspect is that this issue resembles the
challenges of neuro-symbolic systems, where injected knowledge may or may not be retained effectively by the
model [41, 83, 5, 2, 3]. This indicates that hybrid AG-IDS pipelines would benefit from methodologies explicitly
designed to preserve, reason over, and update symbolic knowledge as part of the detection process. Moreover, the
lack of guarantees regarding the persistence of injected knowledge suggests that current hybrid architectures may
only partially exploit the AG model, limiting their potential for coherent multi-step attack reasoning. Addressing
this gap calls for research into robust knowledge integration strategies, where the AG functions not merely as a
post-processing layer or auxiliary input, but as a structural prior guiding the entire decision pipeline.
In summary, the current body of research demonstrates several hybrid approaches, each aiming either at dynamic AG
refinement or at improving IDS reliability. These works address hybridization from only one of these perspectives.
Therefore, they do not exploit the full potential of coordinated AG-IDS interaction. None of the surveyed approaches
implements a continuous AG-IDS integration loop combining all three integration classes proposed in this work (a
gap clearly reflected by the empty central cluster in Figure 1). Nevertheless, existing approaches form a valuable
foundation for the development of a complete AG–IDS lifecycle. Within such a lifecycle, hybrid strategies may
enable dynamic optimization and continuous updating of both the AGs and the IDSs used to detect the exploitation of
known vulnerabilities. To mitigate obsolescence when new vulnerabilities arise, it is crucial to update not only alert
verification and correlation mechanisms but also the IDS itself.
ü Systematization Takeaways:
• While various two-way hybridizations exist, current research lacks a unified framework that merges all
three integration classes (AG|IDS, IDS[AG], IDS →AG) into a continuous, self-updating lifecycle.
• Hybrid models that focus solely on updating alert correlation schemes remain vulnerable to new ex-
ploits. To truly mitigate obsolescence, the IDS must be dynamically optimized alongside the AG.
• In ML-based IDSs, retaining injected AG knowledge is non-trivial. Future pipelines must treat AG not
merely as auxiliary inputs, but as structural priors to guarantee coherent multi-step attack reasoning.
• The field relies heavily on outdated static/temporal models and simulated datasets. Advancing hybrid
integration requires modern techniques and realistic benchmarking.

4
Closing AG-IDS Integration Loop
Our systematization reveals that existing AG-IDS integration approaches primarily seek to mitigate the inherent limita-
tions of standalone AGs and IDSs. These methods predominantly operate within static detection environments, where
the integration is performed as a one-time configuration. Such a paradigm overlooks dynamic context changes and the
necessity for continuous refinement of detection and response mechanisms. Conversely, while the identified handful
of hybrid approaches represent a valid attempt to address these issues, they only partially alleviate the constraints of
static integration. This is because they focus on either the AG or IDS side in isolation, lacking a cohesive, bidirectional
perspective. Consequently, they fail to facilitate the continuous adaptation of the integrated AG-IDS mechanism itself,
typically introducing hybridization to only a single component.
Real-world environments require security mechanisms capable of adapting to emerging threats, evolving vulnerabili-
ties, and fluid system configurations. This dynamism requires a shift toward leveraging a multitude of up-to-date IDSs
and AGs to enable robust detection and response. Building on our systematization and to meet the above require-
ment, we propose a continuously integrated AG-IDS lifecycle that transcends semi-static, one-time, or unidirectional
integration paradigms. This lifecycle orchestrates diverse AG-IDS methodologies across multiple stages, enabling the
iterative refinement of both AGs and IDSs in tandem. Rather than isolating specific integration strategies – such as
IDS[AG], AG|IDS, IDS →AG or hybrid models – our vision unifies these models within a cohesive, feedback-
driven framework engineered for sustained adaptability and operational resilience.
The proposed lifecycle serves as a proof-of-concept of our vision and is structured as shown in Figure 5. The core
objective is to enable the continuous update of the IDS model and detection process (see Add function to the IDS pool
in Figure 5), as well as AG generation and analysis (see the Add function to the AG pool in Figure 5). By establishing
a complete feedback loop (represented by the information flow, logical ports, and actions in Figure 5), the lifecycle
aims to fulfill the requirements for continuous protection against novel threats and vulnerabilities. Specifically, the
lifecycle introduces feedback loops that: (i) define new detection schemes based on updated AGs to identify emerging
or modified attacks (represented by the output of the IDS[AG] module), (ii) analyze IDS outputs to uncover novel
vulnerability paths (represented by the output of the IDS →AG module), and (iii) define or update AGs after un-
covering new vulnerability paths (represented by the output of AG—IDS module). The envisioned lifecycle performs
integration iteratively, selecting AGs and IDSs from a shared pool – continuously adapted based on real-time alerts
and vulnerabilities – and updating a vulnerability database that records system knowledge in a context-aware manner.
At each iteration, the system selects one or more optimized IDSs (the output of the sample action) to guide the
generation of custom AGs, which is then added to the AG pool. This process incorporates both known vulnerabilities
from the Vulnerability Database and IDS predictions outputted from the sampled orange IDS cloud, thereby embedding
AG|IDS techniques into the lifecycle. Conversely, the system refines IDS models by integrating them with one or
more AGs outputted from the sampled violet AG clouds and returns the enhanced detectors to the IDS pool, effectively
embedding IDS[AG] techniques into the lifecycle. Similarly, the system can utilize any AG from the pool, including
those derived from IDS alerts, to validate resulting IDSs using updated AGs via IDS →AG strategies.
To illustrate these dynamics, we provide a running example that maps the lifecycle to the logical ports, pools, and
actions in Figure 5. Consider a production network where a previously unknown Remote Code Execution (RCE)
vulnerability exists in a common logging library. Initially, the Vulnerability Database contains no record of this flaw,
and the IDS Pool consists of standard NIDS or HIDS. The cycle begins when an attacker exploits this zero-day flaw to
establish a reverse shell. While the specific exploit signature is unknown, a sampled NIDS from the IDS Pool identifies
anomalous outbound traffic. This alert is fed through the IDS[AG] module. By combining this real-time alert with
current AG models (via an AND logical port), the system generates a new AG from the AG—IDS that includes paths
updated from the vulnerability database via IDS →AG for the compromised web server as a verified stepping
stone. This new graph is then committed to the AG Pool via the Add action. With the threat landscape updated, the
lifecycle moves to refine the detection layer. The system performs a Sample action on the AG Pools to retrieve the
newly updated AG. Simultaneously, a more specialized NIDS is sampled from the IDS Pool. These components are
integrated through an AND port into the IDS[AG] module. This results in a context-aware AG-IDS sensor specifically
tuned to monitor the lateral movement paths identified in the updated attack graph. As the attacker attempts to move
from the web server to the database, this refined AG-IDS sensor detects the specific lateral movement payload. This
detection event, potentially combined with other sensor outputs via an OR port, is fed into the IDS →AG engine.
This process closes the loop in two ways: first, the refined detection model is returned to the IDS Pool via the Add
function to ensure permanent protection; second, the specific exploit characteristics and verified attack paths are used
to Add a new entry to the Vulnerability Database and generate updated AGs. Through this iterative reinforcement, the
lifecycle transforms a single anomalous alert into a hardened, context-aware defense posture.

AG-IDS Lifecycle Proof-of-Concept
To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed AG-IDS integration lifecycle, we define a proof-of-concept based on the
CIC-IDS2017 dataset [116]. We selected the CIC-IDS2017 dataset because it provides publicly available, labeled data
with diverse attack types. Its structural metadata is essential for deriving attack paths and aligning network events with
AG. Its wide adoption in the IDS community ensures our evaluation remains comparable to existing NIDS baselines.
It includes both benign traffic and a wide range of cyberattacks in the form of packet capture files (PCAPs) and
corresponding flow-based CSV files generated using the CICFlowMeter tool4. The dataset includes the generation of
naturalistic background traffic through the B-Profile system [42], which models human-like behavior in network usage
across common protocols. Collected data comprises simulations of 25 users interacting with the network during real
enterprise activities. The dataset spans five days and comprehensive setting configurations, including heterogeneous
hosts (i.e., routers, firewalls, switches, Windows, Ubuntu, and macOS operating systems), attack diversity, based on
real-world threat intelligence, and an extensive feature set, with over 80 flow-based features extracted from traffic.
Our analysis focuses on the three core components of AG-IDS integration, examined across successive iterations of
the simulated lifecycle. We here emphasize that this proof-of-concept serves as a demonstrative implementation of
the proposed lifecycle, clarifying its key stages and functionalities, while a formal prototype and in-depth evaluation
remain open future research directions (see Section 6). Finally, we make the source code of our lifecycle proof-of-
concept publicly available5.

Experimental Setup
For the IDS, we consider a multi-class detection module which aims to predict if a given packet is benign or malicious,
while predicting the attack category as well. Due to limitations of existing rule sets in the Emerging Threats6 (which
will later be used for AG generation and IDS signatures), we limit out evaluation to FTP Patator and DoS attack
classes of the CIC-IDS 2017 dataset. Since we focus on the above-mentioned attack classes, we consider the data
samples of CIC-IDS coming from the Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday split and remove the packet traces that are
neither benign nor belong to one of the attack classes that we considered. Throughout our experiments, we consider
a Decision Tree (DT) classifier and – if not mentioned otherwise – we set its maximum depth to 20, the minimum
number of samples required to split an internal node to two, the minimum number of samples required to be at a leaf
node to one, and the optimization criterion to the Gini impurity. We train the DT classifier on 60% of the dataset
and test its performance on the remaining 40% using a random split – except for the experiments of Figures 10
and 18 – and select the 20 most relevant features according to the ANOVA F-value7. Concerning the AG used, if
not mentioned otherwise, we use the graph constructed from the Emerging Threats rule set and set the probability of
adding a random attack path p = 0, which correspond to leveraging a very reliable AG. To ensure a fair evaluation,
we measure the performance of the trained DT-based IDS using accuracy, F1-score, and False Positive Rate (FPR).
For each experiment, we compute the performance gain by measuring the difference in accuracy/F1/FPR between the
standard IDS and its refined counterpart, and plot this delta over the barplot of each setting. An improvement in IDS
performance is given by a positive delta value for accuracy and F1-score and a negative delta for FPR.

To evaluate the effectiveness of the IDS[AG] component within the proposed lifecycle, we compare a standard DT-
based IDS with its IDS[AG]-enhanced counterpart. Without loss of generality, we consider a multi-class IDS module
that classifies each packet as benign or malicious and also identifies the attack category. Due to limitations in the
Emerging Threats rule set, we focus on the FTP Patator and DDoS attack classes from the CIC-IDS2017 dataset. The
IDS[AG] integration is enabled by converting the presence of an attack path in the AG – continuously generated as
described in Section 5.2 – into a binary feature added to the DT training set. Specifically, for each packet observed
during training, if its source and destination correspond to nodes connected by an attack path in the AG, the feature is
set to one; otherwise, it is set to zero. This allows the IDS model to incorporate knowledge of whether an attack path
exists along the packet’s route. To reflect the dynamic nature of the lifecycle, we also assess how the quality of the AG
and the availability of data affect detection performance.
Figure 8 shows the IDS[AG] performance as the AG quality is degraded by randomly adding non-existent attack
paths with probability p. A value of p = 0 corresponds to a perfectly reliable AG containing only real attack paths,
while p = 0.2 represents a highly connected AG where most paths do not reflect actual threats (e.g., because some
vulnerabilities cannot be exploited). Across all p values, IDS[AG] consistently outperforms the baseline, improving
detection accuracy and F1-score by over 1%, and reducing the FPR by a similar margin. As expected, lower p – i.e.,
more reliable AGs – yields greater performance gains. These results confirm that the iterative AG refinement central
to the proposed lifecycle enhances IDS detection. In early iterations (higher p), the AG may be incomplete, yielding
modest improvements. However, as the AG is refined in later iterations (lower p), the detection gains become more
substantial. Figure 9 shows similar trends when varying the AG used in IDS[AG], considering: (i) the AG derived
from vulnerabilities in the dataset’s service descriptions (Scrape), (ii) the AG built from Emerging Threats signatures
(ET), and (iii) three combinations of Scrape and ET or its subset (Sub(ET)). The ET AG is the optimal AG to be used
for this task – as it is constructed directly from effective IDS signatures – and results in the highest performance boost,
further confirming that iterative AG refinement improves attack detection.

5.4
AG-based IDS Refinement (IDS →AG)
We evaluate the effectiveness of the IDS →AG component in the proposed lifecycle, using the same experimental
settings as in Section 5.3. We extend the DT-based IDS module with an IDS →AG refinement layer that leverages
prior knowledge from the AG constructed in Section 5.2. Specifically, the DT is trained on the CIC-IDS2017 dataset
following standard ML-based IDS procedures. During inference, if the model predicts an attack, we check whether
the packet’s source and destination are part of an existing attack path in the AG. If such a path exists, the prediction
is retained; otherwise, it is flipped to benign. This refinement assumes that if no viable attack path exists in the AG,
the IDS prediction is likely a false positive, as no threat could feasibly exploit that flow. Conversely, we do not flip
benign predictions to attacks, since the presence of an attack path only indicates a potential strategy, not an active
threat. Therefore, the IDS→AG strategy is designed to correct false positives—i.e., benign samples misclassified as
attack-based on structural threat knowledge encoded in the AG.

Opportunities
Our systematization highlights promising areas for advancement: achieving dataset standardization, addressing model
heterogeneity, and enhancing integration strategies throughout the system lifecycle. In this perspective, the proposed
systematic AG-IDS lifecycle provides a clear roadmap of opportunities to fully integrate these technologies from a
comprehensive, end-to-end perspective. Building on these insights, we here outline more in detail the promising
research directions to advance adaptive and scalable AG-IDS systems.
AG-IDS system deployment: The proof-of-concept in Section 5 showcases the effectiveness of the proposed lifecy-
cle. However, its thorough development and deployment still represent an open research opportunity for the future.
Indeed, to deploy the proposed lifecycle, it is required to define flexible frameworks capable of dealing with the
management of both AGs and IDSs over various network scenarios, independently of their types. Moreover, such a
framework should enable multiple levels of integration, while also being flexible to allow defining more sophisticated
combination approaches. Existing Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) or cyber threat intelligence
platforms, such as Microsoft XDR 8, MITRE ATT&CK Mapping 9, or GraphWeaver 10, can be a starting base for
eventually building an AG-IDS system.
Multi-source integration: Our systematization reveals that current integration approaches typically rely on single
AG and IDS sources, limiting contextual coverage and detection diversity. Additionally, real-world environments – for
instance, security operations centers – monitor and respond to different sources. In this context, future research should
explore and support integration across multiple AGs and IDSs following a similar roadmap to existing solutions, e.g.,
Elastic Stack 11 or Security Onion 12.

critical. Furthermore, the lack of open-source implementations hinders reproducibility. This necessitates creating a
standardized benchmarking platform for fair comparison, which will incentivize further research in the field.
Machine Learning: The systematization highlights three compelling opportunities for advancing the use of ML in
AG-IDS integration systems: (i) integrating advanced ML models – e.g., GNNs [4], CNNs [123] and LSTMs [62] –
which have demonstrated encouraging performance for detection and response tasks; (ii) implementing continuous
retraining to proactively address concept drift and challenges posed by evolving traffic; (iii) increasing attention to
the constraints and requirements for real-world deployment; and, (iv) leveraging emerging tools like LangChain 13
for developing modular agentic AG and IDS integration. Regarding (i), moving beyond simplistic ML solutions
allows models to exploit critical contextual information (e.g., network topology and temporal dynamics), significantly
boosting accuracy. With respect to (ii), continuous learning must replace the static train-and-deploy paradigm to
maintain model performance against dynamic traffic and data distribution shifts. Concerning (iii), models must be
developed with real-world constraints in mind to ensure practical deployment. Finally, (iv) offers a novel path for
automating complex investigation steps through custom agentic systems.
Enhanced CTI: In the context of threat intelligence, the AG-IDS integration is worth investigating for the automation
of Tactics, Techniques and Procedures (TTPs) mapping [12]. Specifically, the raw alerts generated by the IDSs are
correlated and mapped to MITRE ATT&CK semantics on TTPs by leveraging the logical structure of AGs. In another
note, the AG-IDS integration system can serve as a validation engine. In particular, the identified attack path or attack
scenario is confirmed by a sequence of IDS alerts, which can be provided as feedback for threat intelligence platforms.
Neuro-Symbolic integration for AG-IDS: Neuro-symbolic (NeSy) AI combines symbolic reasoning, which handles
structured knowledge and logical constraints, with neural learning, which excels at pattern recognition and general-
ization from data [34, 75]. In the AG–IDS integration context, NeSy can enable systems that reason over AGs while
learning from IDS alerts and network traffic, creating adaptive and interpretable detection pipelines. This dual capabil-
ity is particularly valuable for correlating alerts with feasible attack paths and anticipating complex intrusion strategies.
A concrete example is dynamic alert prioritization: a NeSy-based system could use AG constraints (e.g., prerequisite
vulnerabilities and reachable states) to rank IDS alerts by their likelihood of forming a valid attack path, while neural
components learn patterns of alert sequences that historically led to successful compromises. This would reduce false
positives and help SOC operators focus on the most critical threats.
7
Conclusions
AGs and IDSs are fundamental to network security, underpinning both detection and response. While each has evolved
independently and in partial integration, research on their combined use remains fragmented, limiting a unified under-
standing of their full potential. This paper provides a comprehensive systematization of AG-IDS integration, catego-
rizing existing approaches into three main scopes: IDS-based AG generation, AG-integrated IDS, and AG-based IDS
refinement. Our analysis reveals that no current solution addresses all three dimensions cohesively, leaving a gap for
holistic integration. To bridge this gap, we propose a dynamic AG-IDS lifecycle that enables continuous adaptation
and iterative refinement of detection and response mechanisms. We further provide an experimental proof-of-concept
of this envisioned lifecycle, empirically demonstrating its advantages in improving scalability, detection accuracy,
and contextual risk assessment. This vision offers a pathway to overcome persistent challenges such as scalability,
high false positive rates, and limited situational awareness. Moreover, it establishes a foundation for future research,
highlighting opportunities in benchmarking, dataset development, and real-world deployment.
