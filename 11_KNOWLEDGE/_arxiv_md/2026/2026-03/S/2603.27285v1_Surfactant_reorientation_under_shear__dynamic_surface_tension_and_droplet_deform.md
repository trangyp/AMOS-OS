Surfactant reorientation under shear: dynamic surface tension and droplet
deformation
Alexandra J. Hardy, Abdallah Daddi-Moussa-Ider, and Elsen Tjhung
School of Mathematics and Statistics, The Open University, Walton Hall, Milton Keynes MK7 6AA,
United Kingdom
(*Electronic mail: elsen.tjhung@open.ac.uk)
(*Electronic mail: admi2@open.ac.uk)
(Dated: March 31, 2026)
We study the deformation of a surfactant-covered droplet under shear flow using a phase-field model that explicitly
accounts for both the surfactant concentration and its polarization, representing the average molecular orientation.
We first consider a flat interface and show that an imposed tangential shear causes the surfactant polarization to
tilt away from the interface normal. This reorientation reduces the ability of surfactants to lower the interfacial
free energy, leading to an increase in the effective surface tension and demonstrating that surface tension can be
dynamically modified by shear. We then examine droplet deformation under shear in both weakly and strongly
confined geometries. In the weak-confinement regime, numerical results recover the linear Taylor scaling at small
capillary numbers, while at larger capillary numbers they are accurately described by a modified Maffettone–Minale
phenomenological model. The presence of surfactants enhances deformation through a reduction in the effective
surface tension. In the strong-confinement regime, wall effects further increase droplet deformation, with results
qualitatively captured by including the Shapira–Haber correction.
Overall, our findings show that surfactant
reorientation under flow provides a microscopic mechanism for shear-dependent surface tension and has significant
implications for droplet deformation in confined multiphase flows.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Surfactants are well known for their ability to lower the
surface tension between two immiscible fluids1,2. Because of
this property, they are widely used across many industrial
applications,
including detergents,
emulsification,
food
processing, pharmaceuticals, and enhanced oil recovery3–7.
In particular, the addition of surfactants gives rise to a
wide range of interfacial phenomena8–10, including enhanced
droplet deformation under an imposed shear flow.
Surfactants
consist
of
a
hydrophilic
head
and
a
hydrophobic tail and they preferentially align perpendicular
to the interface. However under shear flow, this orientation
can be modified, leading to a coupling between flow and
interfacial properties.
Using a phase-field model that
explicitly incorporates both surfactant concentration and
orientation11,12, we demonstrate that an imposed tangential
shear induces a tilt of the surfactant polarization (or
average orientation) away from the interface normal. This
reorientation reduces the ability of surfactants to lower the
interfacial free energy, resulting in an increase in the effective
surface tension.
As a consequence, the surface tension
becomes a dynamic and local quantity that depends not only
on surfactant concentration but also on the local shear rate.
Shear-dependent surface tension can directly influence
droplet deformation.
In pure systems,
i.e.,
without
surfactants, several theoretical models have been developed
to describe droplet behavior under shear. This is quantified
by the capillary number, which measures the ratio of viscous
to interfacial forces. For sufficiently small capillary numbers,
Taylor’s classical theory predicts a linear scaling between
droplet deformation and the capillary number13.
For
larger deformations, phenomenological models such as the
Maffettone–Minale (MM) model have been developed14,15.
Extensions incorporating wall confinement effects have been
proposed by Shapira16 and Vananroye17. Numerical studies
have confirmed these behaviors and revealed additional
features, including overshoot, oscillatory relaxation, and
eventual breakup at high capillary numbers18–23.
In the presence of surfactants, the droplet dynamics
become more complex due to surfactant redistribution and
the resulting Marangoni stresses24,25.
Previous studies
generally report enhanced deformation due to reduced
surface tension26–28, with additional effects arising from
non-uniform surfactant distributions29,30.
However, most
existing models treat surfactants as scalar concentration
fields and neglect their molecular orientation. As a result,
they do not capture the coupling between flow, surfactant
orientation, and interfacial mechanics.
To address this limitation, we employ a phase-field model
that explicitly incorporates surfactant polarization (average
orientation) and its coupling to the interface11,12. Within
this framework, we first show that shear flow dynamically
modifies the effective surface tension through surfactant
reorientation.
We then investigate droplet deformation
under shear, both in the absence and presence of surfactants,
and in regimes of weak and strong confinement. In the weak-
confinement regime, we recover the classical Taylor scaling
at small capillary numbers and show that deformation
at larger capillary numbers is accurately captured by a
modified Maffettone–Minale theory.
Surfactants enhance
deformation by reducing the effective surface tension, which
can be incorporated via an effective capillary number
accounting for shear-dependent interfacial properties.
In
the strong-confinement regime, wall effects further increase
deformation, and the combined influence of confinement
and surfactants is described by including the Shapira–Haber
correction.

II.
PHASE FIELD MODEL
We consider a two-phase fluid system (e.g., oil and
water) containing surfactant molecules.
Surfactants have
a hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic tail, causing them
to preferentially adsorb at the interface between two fluid
phases, typically aligning perpendicular to the interface.
Within a continuum phase-field description, the two fluid
phases are described by a single scalar order parameter
ϕ(r, t), which takes values approximately +1 in the oil
phase and −1 in the water phase. The interface between
the two fluid phases is diffuse, so that ϕ varies smoothly
between these two values across a finite interfacial region
of thickness ξ.
The surfactant molecules are represented
by two additional fields: the local surfactant concentration
c(r, t) and the average molecular orientation p(r, t), referred
to as the polarization field.
The thermodynamic behaviour of the system is described

coupling between the two fluid phases, the surfactant

VI.
CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we have investigated the interplay between
surfactant orientation, interfacial mechanics, and droplet
deformation under shear flow.
Using a phase-field model
that explicitly incorporates both surfactant concentration
and polarization/average orientation, we have shown that
the anisotropic nature of surfactant molecules leads to
a fundamentally new effect:
a shear-dependent surface
tension.
For a flat interface, we demonstrated that an imposed
tangential shear induces a tilt of the surfactant polarization
relative to the interface normal.
Because the surfactant
orientation couples directly to the interfacial gradient in
the free energy, this reorientation reduces the ability of
surfactants to lower the interfacial energy.
As a result,
the effective surface tension increases with shear rate and
becomes a dynamic quantity depending on both surfactant
concentration and flow. Our perturbative analysis yields an
explicit expression for this shear-dependent surface tension,
which shows good agreement with numerical simulations.
We then investigate the effects of surfactants for droplet
deformation under shear beyond the linear Taylor regime.
In the weak-confinement regime, droplet deformation is
accurately described by a modified MM theory.
In the
presence of surfactants, the deformation is enhanced due
to the reduction in effective surface tension. It is important
to note, however, that although shear flow can in principle
strongly modify the effective surface tension, this effect
becomes significant only at very large shear rates, ˙γ ≫
Γ−1
r .
In this regime, the droplet is typically unstable
and undergoes breakup into smaller droplets.
A detailed
investigation of droplet breakup therefore lies beyond the
scope of the present work and will be addressed in future
studies.
In the strong-confinement regime, where wall effects
become
significant,
droplet
deformation
is
further
enhanced.
We
find
that
the
combined
effects
of
confinement and surfactants can be captured qualitatively
by incorporating the Shapira–Haber correction into the
theoretical framework. Overall, our numerical results show
qualitative agreement with these theoretical predictions.
In related systems, vesicles subjected to shear flow in
confined geometries exhibit dynamical modes such as tank-
treading and tumbling45,46.
The key distinction from
our system is that vesicles are enclosed by an elastic
membrane, whereas here the interface is fluid. Consequently,
the velocity field remains continuous across the interface;
moreover, under the assumption of equal viscosities inside
and outside the droplet, such tank-treading and tumbling
dynamics are not observed.
The present work highlights the importance of surfactant
orientation in determining interfacial properties under
flow.
By identifying a microscopic mechanism for shear-
dependent surface tension and demonstrating its impact
on droplet deformation in both unbounded and confined
geometries, this study provides a more complete description
of surfactant-covered multiphase flows.
These findings
may have implications for the control of emulsions and

microfluidic systems, where both confinement and flow-
induced effects play a crucial role. Finally, the modelling
framework developed here can be readily extended to study
active emulsions32.
