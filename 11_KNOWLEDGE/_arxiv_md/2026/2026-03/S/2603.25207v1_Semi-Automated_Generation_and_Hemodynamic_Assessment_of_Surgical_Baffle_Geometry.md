Abstract
Patient-specific computational modeling has emerged as a powerful tool for surgical planning
in complex congenital heart disease. One promising application is complex biventricular repair,
which often requires construction of a custom intraventricular baffle to establish a physiologic
left ventricle-to-aorta outflow pathway.
In current practice, baffle geometry is designed and
shaped intraoperatively and preoperative planning remains largely manual, limiting the ability
to generate anatomically conformal, watertight models suitable for quantitative hemodynamic
assessment. In this work, we present a semi-automated computational framework for the design
and assessment of patient-specific intraventricular baffles. The method constructs an explicit
VSD-to-aorta flow pathway, preserves native right ventricular geometry, and reshapes only the
baffle region using section-wise area constraints along a physiologically aligned centerline. The
resulting geometry is integrated into a closed, multi-labeled domain for computational fluid
dynamics (CFD) analysis.
We retrospectively applied this framework to four patients with
double outlet right ventricle (DORV) who previously underwent biventricular repair. For each
case, a patient-specific baffle was generated and its hemodynamic performance was evaluated
using CFD. Predicted pressure gradients across the reconstructed outflow were within clinically
acceptable ranges and comparable to the patients’ postoperative echocardiographs. This ap-
proach enables quantitative, pre-operative design and evaluation of candidate baffle geometries
and provides a reproducible method for generating simulation-ready models.
By combining
physiologically constrained geometric design with CFD-based assessment, the framework rep-
resents a step toward computational, patient-specific decision support for biventricular flow
restoration in a complex heterogeneous patient population.

Introduction
Congenital heart defects (CHDs) are the most common type of birth defect, affecting roughly 40,000
births annually in the United States. They encompass a broad and highly variable spectrum of
structural abnormalities [1]. Many patients with severe defects have only one ventricle effectively
contributing to cardiac output and therefore require surgical intervention to restore adequate cir-
culation. Historically, these highly heterogeneous patients were often palliated to a single-ventricle
physiology [2]. However, this strategy is associated with substantial long-term morbidity and mor-
tality. Therefore, when anatomically feasible, several advanced centers offer complex biventricular
repair as an alternative strategy that restores two-ventricle physiology [3, 4].
In parallel, computational modeling has emerged as a powerful tool for generating patient-specific
predictions across a range of clinical applications [5, 6, 7, 8, 9], and supporting clinical decision
making, with particular impact in pediatric cardiology [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 16]. By improving
understanding of complex cardiovascular flow patterns and integrating multimodal imaging data,
these models provide a promising framework for patient-specific planning and comparison of surgical
strategies [13, 14]. These advantages are particularly relevant in congenital anatomies requiring
complex biventricular repair, where surgical reconstruction must be tailored to highly patient-specific
morphology and substantial anatomical heterogeneity can make preoperative planning difficult.
One particularly challenging defect is double outlet right ventricle (DORV), in which both the aorta
and pulmonary artery arise predominantly from the right ventricle [17]. DORV is also characterized
by the presence of a ventricular septal defect (VSD), a hole between the ventricles that results in
mixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood. Successful repair often requires the construction of
an intraventricular tunnel that redirects left ventricular blood flow through the VSD to the aorta
while avoiding flow obstruction [18]. This is typically achieved using a surgically created intracardiac
baffle, an internal patch or conduit that redirects blood flow within the heart [19, 20]. However,
the relationship of the VSD to the great arteries varies considerably across patients, making the
feasibility and design of the baffle highly anatomy-dependent [21].
Currently, baffles for DORV patients are designed and constructed intraoperatively on the arrested
heart, requiring surgeons to shape and adjust the patch in real time while the heart is unpressurized
[18].
This limits the ability to evaluate alternative configurations or quantitatively assess their
hemodynamic performance preoperatively. Furthermore, the anatomical heterogeneity of DORV,
variability in baffle design, and high rates of reoperation, further motivate the need for patient-
specific computational tools to support surgical decision making.
To address these challenges, patient-specific surgical planning requires (i) a three-dimensional (3D)
anatomical model derived from imaging data, (ii) a computational method for generating a patch
that optimizes a defined objective, and (iii) a framework to evaluate the design using computational
fluid dynamics (CFD). Patient-specific cardiac models can now be routinely reconstructed from CT
or MRI, with recent machine learning approaches enabling rapid whole-heart segmentation and re-
ducing reliance on manual workflows [22, 23, 24]. These models are used clinically for visualization,
3D printing, and surgical planning in complex congenital heart disease, including more recent virtual
reality-based environments [25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. Major advances toward preoperative planning have
been made through interactive visualization tools that allow clinicians to inspect patient-specific
anatomy and define candidate baffle pathways within reconstructed models [30]. In some cases,
these geometries are subsequently flattened to support operative template preparation, a has been
demonstrated for branch pulmonary artery repair [31]. Other computational studies have examined
the effects of idealized intraventricular tunnel geometries on pressure gradients, yet these rely on

simplified or manually constructed conduits rather than automated patient-specific design workflows
[32]. Thus, existing methods do not fully address key computational design requirements, includ-
ing outflow tract area optimization, watertight integration with native anatomy, and CFD-based
hemodynamic assessment.
This paper proposes a novel computational pipeline for generating patient-specific intraventricular
baffles that redirect flow from the left ventricle to the aorta. Using systolic-phase cardiac computed
tomograph (CT) images, the method produces a 3D baffle that integrates directly with the patient’s
anatomical model for CFD analysis and surgical planning.
The resulting baffle forms a tunnel
from the ventricular septal defect (VSD) to the aortic valve while maintaining sufficient cross-
sectional area along its length. We retrospectively applied this framework to four DORV patients
who had previously undergone biventricular repair at our institution and compared the predicted
pressure gradients from the virtual baffles with postoperative catheterization measurements from
the surgically constructed pathways. These results support the feasibility of the method and its
potential use in preoperative planning.

Methods
We present a computational method to generate and evaluate patient-specific intracardiac baffles
that restore biventricular flow in congenital heart disease (CHD) patients. While this study focuses
on DORV with VSDs in varied anatomical locations, this methodology is broadly applicable to other
complex CHDs involving intracardiac flow rerouting. For each patient we performed the following
steps: (1) reconstruction of a patient-specific 3D model from preoperative systolic CT imaging, (2)
algorithmic generation of a closed intraventricular baffle surface, and (3) hemodynamic analysis of
the reconstructed outflow pathway. An overview of this pipeline is illustrated in Fig 1.

2.2
Computational Domain Preparation
This section details the workflow used to transform medical imaging data into segmentations and
subsequently into a three-dimensional, multi-labeled mesh with an initial, unoptimized baffle.
2.2.1
Semi-Automated Machine Learning Segmentation
For each of the four patients, the end-systolic phase from the preoperative CTA was extracted
and segmented into seven anatomic structures. The systolic phase was selected for segmentation
and subsequent baffle construction to capture ventricular geometry during peak ejection and better
reflect the anatomy relevant to outflow reconstruction. The cardiac blood pools of the left ventricle
(LV), right ventricle (RV), left atrium (LA), and right atrium (RA) were segmented to define the
luminal portion of the heart, including trabeculations. The myocardial wall (myo) was segmented
separately to distinguish tissue from blood pool. The pulmonary artery (PA) included the main
pulmonary trunk and the proximal right and left branch PAs, while the aortic root (ao) consisted
of the ascending aorta with the arch and branch vessels excluded.
The images were initially segmented using a U-Net–based deep learning framework. Convolutional
neural networks have been well established for semantic segmentation objectives in a supervised
setting with U-Net-based models remaining highly competitive [34], especially in low-data regimes.
We employed the self-configuring nnU-Net framework [35], which automates preprocessing, training
configuration, and hyperparameter selection. The model was trained on the ImageCHD dataset
(67 training, 5 validation, 34 test cases) [36] and its performance was compared with a baseline
U-Net implementation from SDF4CHD [24]. Training used default nnU-Net settings with DA5 data
augmentation scheme, which applies extensive spatial and intensity transformations during training.
The resulting segmentations, along with the corresponding CT images, were then imported into 3D
Slicer for manual refinement. The first column of Figure 6 illustrates the final seven-part segmenta-
tion for all four patients, with each row corresponding to a different patient. Mislabeled regions were
corrected and unlabeled voxels were assigned to the appropriate anatomical class as needed, with
particular attention to the ventricular septal interface and aortic valve annulus. All segmentations
were reviewed and validated by a pediatric cardiologist with subspecialty training in cardiovascular
imaging (Maskatia) to ensure accuracy and methodological consistency across patients.
2.2.2
3D Model Generation and Construction of a Unified, Capped Flow Domain
The finalized multi-label segmentations were interpreted as three-dimensional image grids in which
each voxel was assigned an integer label, ModelFaceID, corresponding to a specific anatomical struc-
ture. For each structure, a binary mask was generated by isolating voxels belonging to that label. To
improve geometric and topological consistency, each binary mask underwent morphological closing
and median filtering. Polygonal mesh, Mk, was extracted using the marching cubes algorithm, post-
processed to remove topological artifacts (e.g., non-manifold edges), and geometrically smoothed [37].
For each subdomain mesh Mk, all triangular faces were assigned ModelFaceID = k with mapping:
LV =1 , RV = 2, LA = 3, RA = 4, myo = 5, ao = 6, PA = 7.
For each patient, we constructed a singular, manifold mesh, MAoBiV, to represent the continuous
blood pool of the LV, RV, and aorta by taking the union of the three segmented regions and fol-
lowing the same pipeline of filtering and surface extraction. This process automatically removed

Explicit boundary surfaces corresponding to the aortic valve annulus and the VSD were generated by

non-self-intersecting cutting loops.

2.2.3
Definition of the baffle boundary and placeholder baffle surface
To determine the baffle attachment boundary, a congenital cardiac surgeon (Ma) interactively defined
the intended suture line on the ventricular mesh using the BafflePlanner module in the SlicerHeart
extension for 3D Slicer [38, 30]. The suture line was specified by placing 12 −15 discrete points
in three-dimensional space, which delineated a clinically feasible suture path along the endocar-
dial surface of McappedRV. The resulting coordinate set served as both a visual guide and as the
computational input for the following automated contour generation.
This sparse point set was converted into a densely sampled polyline by first projecting the points onto
their two-dimensional principal component plane, where a greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm was
used to establish a non-self-intersecting ordering. A parametric spline interpolant was then fit to the
ordered points to generate a smooth 3D curve approximating the intended suture path. The spline
was subsequently resampled at N = 600 equidistant parametric intervals, and each sampled point
was projected back onto the surface of McappedRV using a closest-point projection. This produced
the final dense, closed polyline defining the baffle boundary, Bbaffle.
McappedRV was partitioned along the closed curve Bbaffle, and the region containing both the VSD
inlet and aortic outlet was retained. To obtain a watertight domain for subsequent steps, this opening
was capped using SimVascular, creating an initial surface that served as a temporary placeholder.
The boundary of this cap precisely defines the perimeter where the final baffle will be attached. The
geometry was then fully remeshed in SimVascular to provide the geometric foundation for the baffle
construction detailed in the following section. The steps from surgeon selected points to the creation
of a placeholder baffle are depicted in Figure 3.

2.3
Patient-Specific Baffle Surface Construction
With the boundary fixed, the remaining task was to determine the extrusion depth of the baffle into
the RV. Starting from the initial placeholder surface, we constructed the final baffle surface Sbaffle,
and the integrated multi-surface domain Mfinal. The aim was to create a patient-specific intraven-
tricular tunnel that achieved a prescribed target cross-sectional area, Atarget, while maintaining a

smooth, non-self-intersecting flow pathway. This was performed by enforcing area constraints along

2.3.1
Determining Atarget
We aimed to produce a VSD-to-aorta tunnel that maintains a physiologically appropriate cross-
sectional area throughout. We defined Atarget on a patient-specific basis to ensure that the resulting
outflow geometry was consistent with normative values for body size. The native aortic annulus
diameter (dpt) was measured directly from imaging.
The Body Surface Area (BSA)-normalized
reference annulus diameter at a z-score of 0 (dz0) was obtained from established pediatric cardiol-
ogy nomograms. The corresponding cross-sectional area was computed assuming a circular orifice.
Normative values were derived using the Boston Children’s Hospital BSA-aortic annulus z-score
calculator [39, 40], which is derived from measurements in normal children collected over approxi-
mately 12 years and follows established allometric adjustment methods. Anthropometric regressions
(height, weight, BMI) use Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and World Health Organi-
zation reference datasets, and the echocardiographic z-score regressions are periodically updated as
new normative models become available. Patient-specific parameters for the four DORV cases are

2.3.2
PCHIP-based centerline construction
We then defined a centerline along the intended VSD-to-aorta flow pathway to parameterize cross-
sectional measurements. VMTK centerlines are derived from the Voronoi medial axis and follow
minimal-action paths that maximize the inscribed radius [41]. This formulation is well suited for
tubular vessels where the medial path coincides with the physiologic flow direction. In the intraven-
tricular cavity, however, the lumen is wide and non-axisymmetric, and the VSD and aortic outlet
may differ substantially in size. The resulting VMTK centerline would therefore follow the geomet-
ric medial path rather than the intended VSD-to-aorta trajectory and enter the inlet and outlet
with poorly aligned tangents. Therefore, a custom centerline was constructed to enforce a smooth,
physiologically aligned path with controlled endpoint tangents and stable normals for cross-sectional
area evaluation.

ramps. The inlet and outlet centroids pin and pout were computed from the cap polydata, and the

of face normals. Let the inlet and outlet radii be denoted by rin and rout. Two interior ramp points

2.3.5
Convexity-enforced shape profile
The raw amplitudes {αj} obtained from the cross-sectional optimization ensured that each recon-
structed loop individually satisfied the target area constraint.
However, directly applying these
amplitudes produced high-frequency variations along the centerline, leading to local inward cur-
vature between adjacent sections and a non-smooth baffle surface. These geometric irregularities
created abrupt changes in cross-sectional area and surface normal direction, which are undesirable
for smooth ventricular outflow. They are also mechanically unfavorable because surgically implanted
baffles used in congenital cardiac reconstruction are commonly fabricated from autologous or bovine
pericardium [43, 44]. Pericardial tissue behaves as a thin, tension-dominated membrane and is thus
susceptible to stress concentrations and material failure at sharp corners or abrupt bends [45, 46, 47].

Results
The automated baffle generation and simulation workflow was applied on the pre-operateive image
data of four DORV patients who had previously undergone biventricular repair with an intracardiac
LV–Ao baffle. The resulting geometries, including the generated baffle surface Sbaffle and recon-
structed flow pathway Mfinal, were evaluated through hemodynamic simulations and compared
with post-operative clinical measurements.
3.1
Final baffle surfaces and simulation-ready meshes
The workflow was successfully applied to all four DORV patient cases, producing high-fidelity,
patient-specific surface models and simulation-ready computational meshes for each case. Across the
cohort, the constructed baffles formed smooth, continuous surfaces that were free of self-intersections
and integrate seamlessly with the native anatomy at the attachment boundary. In addition to meet-
ing the prescribed geometric constraints, the resulting domains were watertight and topologically
consistent, enabling robust downstream meshing and hemodynamic simulation. Figure 6 highlights
this end-to-end pipeline as applied to each of the four DORV patients. For each case, it summarizes
the steps taken to produce Mfinal.
3.2
Simulation results and comparison to clinical measurements
Steady-state CFD simulations were performed on all final baffle models to analyze the post-operative
flow and pressure environment. For each of the four DORV patients, the converged solution at the fi-
nal timestep was visualized using streamline trajectories seeded at the VSD inlet. Across all patients,
the streamlines demonstrated coherent inlet-to-outlet transport through the intraventricular tunnel.
Flow entering from the VSD was redirected toward the aortic outlet, with streamline trajectories
that followed the baffle geometry and no evidence of large-scale flow reversal. These streamlines
can be visualized in the first two rows of the figure below. These patterns suggest that the baffle
geometry successfully guides ventricular flow toward the systemic outlet.
We also evaluated the pressure field along the baffle pathway by reporting the converged steady-state
pressures of the inlet and outlet. Specifically, Pinlet and Poutlet were computed as the converged,
area-averaged static pressures on the VSD inlet and aortic outlet faces, respectively. These values
represent mean face pressures for the converged solution rather than pulsatile peak gradients. Lo-
calized pressure variations were observed along the baffle surface due to curvature and local flow
acceleration; however, the inlet and outlet pressures provide a global characterization of the pressure
environment associated with baffle-guided flow. Across patients, the resulting pressure drops ranged
between 2 −5 mmHg. These relatively small pressure drops suggest that the baffle pathway intro-
duced only a modest additional resistance to the ventricular outflow. Pressure gradients of several
millimeters of mercury are commonly considered hemodynamically acceptable [20, 53].

Discussion
Computational modeling has emerged as an essential tool for patient-specific analysis of congenital
heart defects, with applications spanning electrophysiology, electromechanics, solid mechanics, and
fluid dynamics [54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59]. These approaches have increasingly been translated into
clinical workflows to inform treatment planning and evaluate surgical strategies [60, 61, 13]. The
complex anatomic and physiologic conditions associated with biventricular repair provide an ideal
application for preoperative computational modeling.
Machine learning-based segmentation methods were successfully applied to accelerate generation of
patient-specific 3D models for surgical planning. The nnU-Net-based pipeline reduced total seg-
mentation time to roughly 1–2 hours per case compared to approximately 4–8 hours of conventional
manual segmentation [62]. The most significant remaining limitations were in anatomically challeng-
ing regions, particularly near VSD margins and aortic valve annulus, where reduced image contrast
and spatial resolution can limit accuracy [63]. These regions were therefore refined through expert
congenital cardiac imaging review. As additional cases are incorporated into an expanded dataset,
model performance is expected to continue to improve, further reducing the need for manual editing.
An important innovation of this workflow is that the user-defined baffle boundary is constrained
to remain coincident with the underlying anatomical mesh, enabling robust generation of a closed,
watertight geometry suitable for CFD analysis. In contrast to purely visualization-based design ap-
proaches, in which drawn curves may not remain fully attached to the surface, this mesh-conforming
formulation preserves geometric consistency during tunnel construction. In the current implemen-
tation, the boundary was defined interactively by a congenital cardiac surgeon, who selects control
points along the intended patch suture line in 3D Slicer. Although this approach enables incorpo-
ration of surgical intent, the resulting boundary remains user-dependent and directly influences the
reconstructed tunnel geometry and predicted hemodynamics. Future work will focus on automated
boundary selection based on geometric and hemodynamic optimization criteria, with the goal of
identifying baffle paths that minimize pressure gradients while reducing inter-user variability.
To define the flow pathway, a custom centerline was constructed rather than using standard VMTK
centerline extraction. VMTK methods assume a tubular lumen with comparable inlet and outlet size
and generate a medial path that maximizes the inscribed radius [64]. In the intraventricular tunnel,
however, the geometry is non-tubular and the inlet and outlet may differ in size and orientation. In
the proposed approach, the centerline was defined using prescribed tangential segments at the inlet
and outlet, which were then connected using a Piecewise Cubic Hermite Interpolating Polynomial
(PCHIP). Although other interpolants could be applied, PCHIP was selected because it provided a
shape-preserving interpolation, avoiding the overshoot commonly observed with cubic splines and
producing stable cross-sectional normals near the boundaries [42].
When applying PCHIP, the
lengths of the tangential transition regions at the inlet and outlet were set to one quarter of the
local effective radius. Future work will optimize this parameter by estimating the transition lengths
directly from the extent of the anatomically fixed regions adjacent to each boundary, thereby allowing
the tangential segments to better reflect the curvature constraints imposed by the native geometry.
An initial, placeholder baffle was first generated, after which the PCHIP-based centerline defined
cross-sectional planes spanning the combined ventricular domain. The resulting cross-sectional con-
tours were modified by reshaping only the baffle segment of each loop using a parabolic displacement
profile. Enforcing the target area independently at each section, however, produces locally irregular
cross-sections with geometric depressions and abrupt transitions between adjacent slices. Such fea-
tures are inconsistent with the smooth, low-curvature configurations typically assumed by autologous
16


## Page 17


or bovine pericardial patches under physiological loading [43, 44]. To address this, an upper convex
envelope smoothing procedure was applied to the section-wise deformation amplitudes to generate a
globally smooth outer envelope while preserving the prescribed lumen area. This approach removes
local concavities and yields a monotonic, gently varying tunnel profile that better reflects expected
surgical patch geometry and promotes more streamlined flow.
The final baffle surface was constructed using a thin-plate spline (TPS) formulation that interpolated
the prescribed boundary curve and the smoothed cross-sectional profiles. TPS was selected because
it produces a minimum-bending-energy surface with global C2 continuity, making it well suited for
representing smoothly varying surgical patches while exactly satisfying boundary constraints [48, 30].
Alternate surface construction methods were considered. Lofting between cross-sections is widely
used for vascular reconstruction from ordered contours aligned along a centerline [65, 61]. However,
lofted surfaces are sensitive to contour correspondence and can exhibit slice-to-slice irregularities
when applied to non-tubular geometries, particularly when contours are anisotropic [66]. Overall,
TPS offers a robust balance between global smoothness and boundary conformity.
Although the proposed area-controlled profile combined with TPS produces a geometrically smooth
and hemodynamically favorable surface, the optimal patch configuration is material-dependent and
influenced by patch stiffness, fixation treatment, and compliance mismatch with the surrounding
tissue [67, 68, 69]. Prior studies have demonstrated that suture-line constraints, axial pre-stretch,
and differential compliance between patch material and native myocardium affect the implanted
geometry and resulting stress distribution [31]. These effects are not explicitly represented in the
present methodology, which treats the baffle as a purely geometric construct.
Future work will
incorporate material-informed constraints to better capture intraoperative deformation and postop-
erative morphology. In particular, coupling the geometric optimization with simplified structural
models of patch mechanics and suture-line anchoring may improve prediction of the final implanted
baffle shape.
Hemodynamic performance of the designed baffle was evaluated using SimVascular with steady-state
CFD. Under steady-flow, rigid-wall assumptions, this framework provided an efficient first-order
assessment of parabolic flow routing and the global pressure associated with directing VSD inflow
toward the aortic outlet. Across patients, the converged inlet-to-outlet pressure differences were
minimal, between 2 −5 mmHg, and well within accepted clinical ranges. These predicted gradients
were consistently lower than the echocardiographic gradients measured for the implanted surgical
baffles, by 3.04 to 9.88 mmHg, further supporting the potential use of this modeling framework to
preoperatively identify baffle geometries with favorable hemodynamic performance. Despite this,
there are localized regions with elevated pressure values that future studies could further evaluate
and analyze.
Furthermore, cardiac blood flow occurs within a deforming ventricular domain. Rigid-wall models
are unable to fully capture ventricular flow because ventricular contraction significantly influence
intraventricular hemodynamics over the cardiac cycle. [70, 71]. Despite this limitation, the proposed
methodology produced physiologically reasonable flow pathways and pressure gradients across a
spectrum of DORV anatomies suggesting that the geometric design framework is robust to underlying
anatomical variability.
Future work will incorporate FSI or coupled electromechanical and flow
simulations with patient-specific ventricular kinematics and patch mechanics to better approximate
the implanted baffle kinematics and hemodynamics through the outflow tract with cardiac wall
motion. Prior work on multiscale, multiphysics whole-heart modeling supports the feasibility of
such approaches [72].
17


## Page 18


Although this study focused on biventricular planning in DORV, the proposed framework can be
applied to other forms of intraventricular baffling and biventricular outflow reconstruction. The
workflow is designed to generate a patient-specific baffle geometry for biventricular repair and eval-
uate resulting hemodynamic performance. Beyond DORV, these methods are intended to support
surgical planning for biventricular conversion in borderline single-ventricle (SV) patients, a popula-
tion characterized by significant anatomic and physiologic heterogeneity [73]. In this cohort, small
variations in ventricular size, atrioventricular valve morphology, ventricular septal defect location,
and outflow tract alignment can significantly influence the feasibility of biventricular repair and
risk of post-operative obstruction.
Patient-specific computational modeling provides a quantita-
tive platform to evaluate candidate biventricular pathways, characterize hemodynamic trade-offs,
and identify anatomically feasible configurations prior to intervention. We plan to use this frame-
work to enable systematic comparison of repair strategies and support more objective, data-driven
decision-making in these complex surgeries.

Conclusions
We presented a semi-automated computational framework for patient-specific design and evaluation
of intraventricular baffles for biventricular repair.
The method combines anatomical reconstruc-
tion, surgeon-guided boundary selection, centerline-based parameterization, and constrained surface
generation to produce watertight, CFD-ready models.
By preserving right ventricular geometry
and reshaping only the baffle region, the framework constructs physiologically aligned VSD-to-aorta
pathways that satisfy cross-sectional area targets along the tunnel. The final surface therefore not
only spans the defect boundary smoothly but also respects a functional area constraint not addressed
by simple boundary-based interpolation. Applied retrospectively to four DORV cases, this workflow
generated anatomically conformal baffles with physiologically reasonable flow patterns and minimal
pressure gradients between 2 – 5 mmHg, which were lower than the corresponding gradients mea-
sured by echocardiography after surgery. These results support the feasibility of the method as a
preoperative planning tool that may help surgeons evaluate and optimize tunnel geometry before
intervention with the potential to improve hemodynamic outcomes.
