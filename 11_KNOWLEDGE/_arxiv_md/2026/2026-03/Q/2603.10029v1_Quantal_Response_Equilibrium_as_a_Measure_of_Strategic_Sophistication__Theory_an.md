Abstract
Theory of Mind benchmarks for large language
models typically produce aggregate scores without
theoretical grounding, making it unclear whether
high performance reflects strategic reasoning or
surface-level heuristics. We introduce a game-
theoretic evaluation framework grounded in quan-
tal response equilibrium (QRE). We derive closed-
form equilibria for four strategic games, each tar-
geting a distinct cognitive capability. We estimate
QRE rationality parameters λ that place model
behavior on a continuous scale calibrated against
human data (λhuman ∈[1.0, 2.5]), and establish
finite-sample convergence bounds via martingale
concentration. Validation across 1,855 games with
seven frontier models (plus four expansion models)
confirms predictions: bluff rates converge to within
4% of equilibrium, λ estimates range from 0.05
to 1.10 across games and models with substantial
cross-model variation, and capability profiles differ
across cognitive axes. Robustness analyses reveal
high sensitivity to prompt framing and version in-
stability in QRE rankings, highlighting the need
for standardized protocols.

INTRODUCTION
Theory of Mind (ToM), the capacity to attribute mental
states to others and use these attributions to predict behavior,
is central to strategic interaction [Premack and Woodruff,
1978, Wellman, 2014]. Originally characterized through
false-belief paradigms in developmental psychology [Wim-
mer and Perner, 1983, Baron-Cohen et al., 1985], ToM
underpins cooperation, deception detection, and multi-agent
coordination [Apperly and Butterfill, 2009].
As large language models are increasingly deployed in set-
tings requiring strategic interaction, from negotiation to
competitive games, understanding their capacity for ToM-
relevant reasoning requires rigorous evaluation tools. Yet
existing approaches lack theoretical grounding, making it
difficult to interpret what high performance actually mea-
sures.
Current ToM benchmarks have notable limitations. Vignette-
based tasks like the Sally-Anne test are susceptible to train-
ing data contamination and evaluate recognition rather than
strategic reasoning [Kosinski, 2023, Ullman, 2023, Shapira
et al., 2024]. Aggregate “ToM scores” conflate dissocia-
ble capabilities, obscuring meaningful differences between
models [Chen et al., 2024, Kim et al., 2023]. And without
formal equilibrium analysis, high scores may reflect surface
heuristics rather than actual belief modeling.
We address these limitations by developing a methodology
for quantifying bounded rationality in AI agents under strate-
gic uncertainty, combining formal equilibrium characteriza-
tions, bounded rationality estimation via quantal response
equilibrium with calibrated uncertainty, and convergence
guarantees that validate our measurements. No prior ToM
evaluation framework has combined game-specific equilib-
rium derivations with per-axis QRE estimation and finite-
sample convergence bounds.
Our contributions span game theory, AI evaluation, and cog-
nitive science. We define four strategic games, each with
formal game-theoretic characterization measuring a distinct
ToM-relevant capability, and derive explicit closed-form
equilibria enabling quantitative predictions (Section 3). We
formalize bounded rationality using quantal response equi-
librium [McKelvey and Palfrey, 1995, Goeree et al., 2016],
estimating rationality parameters λ on a scale validated
against human experimental data (Section 3.5). We prove
that our per-axis ELO rating system converges in expecta-
tion to true capability ratings under the Bradley-Terry model
[Bradley and Terry, 1952], with explicit sample complexity
bounds via Azuma-Hoeffding concentration (Section 3.6).
We validate these predictions across 1,855 games with seven
frontier LLMs (with expansion studies extending to four

Game-theoretic grounding serves ToM evaluation in specific
ways. If models approach equilibria during play, this pro-
vides evidence that games elicit strategic reasoning rather
than arbitrary heuristics. We observe convergence during
play rather than from round 1, suggesting online belief up-
dating, a core component of functional ToM. The quan-
tal response framework provides a continuous measure
of strategic sophistication via λ, which ranges from ran-
dom play (λ →0) to perfect Nash equilibrium behavior
(λ →∞). Human experimental data provides calibration:
λhuman ∈[1.0, 2.5] across diverse game types [Goeree et al.,
2016, Camerer et al., 2004]. Formal bounds on required sam-
ple sizes ensure adequate experimental power and enable
controlled comparison across settings.
Following behavioral economics [Camerer et al., 2004], we
adopt a functional definition of ToM (Definition 1): an agent
exhibits functional ToM if its behavior is consistent with
maintaining and updating models of others’ mental states.
This mechanism-agnostic definition enables measurement
without resolving debates about machine cognition, analo-
gous to revealed preference theory measuring utility without
assuming its existence. We do not claim that LLMs possess
genuine ToM in any phenomenological sense, nor that game
performance generalizes to unstructured social interaction.
Our claims are restricted to behavioral signatures in struc-
tured game contexts with well-defined equilibria.

RELATED WORK
The study of ToM originated with Premack and Woodruff’s
[1978] investigation of chimpanzee cognition and was sub-
sequently formalized through false-belief paradigms [Wim-
mer and Perner, 1983, Baron-Cohen et al., 1985]. Devel-
opmental research established ToM as a multi-component
capacity emerging between ages 3-5 [Wellman, 2014], with
distinct components for belief attribution, desire reason-
ing, and intention inference [Apperly and Butterfill, 2009].
Computational models share the insight that ToM can be
characterized functionally through observable behavior.
Early LLM ToM evaluation applied classic developmen-
tal tasks. Kosinski [2023] reported GPT-4 passing Sally-
Anne tests at levels comparable to 7-year-olds, sparking de-
bate about LLM social cognition. However, Ullman [2023]
demonstrated that minor task variations caused performance
collapse, suggesting pattern matching rather than actual rea-
soning. Shapira et al. [2024] systematically varied task pa-
rameters and found high brittleness, while Sap et al. [2022]
documented systematic failures in social reasoning requiring
mental state attribution.
ToMBench [Chen et al., 2024] expanded evaluation breadth
with diverse scenarios, while FANToM [Kim et al., 2023] in-
troduced adversarial robustness testing. These benchmarks
evaluate comprehension of mental states but not their use
in strategic interaction. Our work requires dynamic, multi-
round strategic interaction where success depends on on-
going belief updating; formal equilibrium predictions then
provide a validity check.
Recent work has highlighted the sensitivity of LLM evalua-
tion to surface-level prompt variations. Sclar et al. [2024]
demonstrate that minor formatting changes (separator to-
kens, instruction ordering) can cause accuracy swings ex-
ceeding 76 points on standard benchmarks, while Mizrahi
et al. [2024] show that non-semantic perturbations systemat-
ically inflate or deflate scores. These findings raise validity
concerns for any evaluation framework, including game-
theoretic approaches: observed strategic behavior may re-
flect prompt-activated heuristics rather than reasoning about
payoff structure. We directly address this concern through
prompt sensitivity analysis (Table 10, Appendix).
In game-theoretic LLM evaluation, SOTOPIA [Zhou et al.,
2024] introduced LLM-vs-LLM social evaluation but relies
on LLM-as-judge scoring, introducing circularity concerns.
Gandhi et al. [2023] and Lorè and Heydari [2023] examined
LLM behavior in specific games, finding varying degrees
of strategic sophistication. Other work examines LLM be-
havior in deception and negotiation games but conflates
multiple skills without formal decomposition.
Several recent benchmarks target LLM strategic reasoning.
GTBench [Duan et al., 2024] evaluates across 10 game tasks
but does not estimate bounded-rationality parameters or
provide convergence guarantees. WGSR-Bench [Wu et al.,
2025] and LLMsPark [Chen et al., 2025] share the goal of
principled strategic evaluation but focus on aggregate per-
formance rather than parameter estimation with uncertainty
quantification. Most closely related, Jia et al. [2025] ap-
ply truncated QRE (TQRE) to evaluate 22 LLMs across 13
single-shot abstract games. Our framework differs in using
multi-round natural-language games requiring within-game
belief updating (harder to solve via cached heuristics), per-
axis capability profiles revealing dissociations invisible to
aggregate measures, finite-sample convergence bounds, and
Bayesian posterior inference with principled uncertainty
intervals.

models bounded rationality by assuming agents choose
actions with probabilities proportional to expected utili-
ties. Unlike Nash equilibrium, QRE accommodates sys-
tematic deviations from optimal play while maintaining
game-theoretic structure. Goeree et al. [2016] provide a
detailed treatment of QRE theory and applications. The ra-

GAME-THEORETIC FRAMEWORK
Definition 1 (Functional ToM). An agent exhibits functional
Theory of Mind if its behavior is consistent with: (a) main-
taining models of other agents’ epistemic and motivational
states, (b) updating these models based on observations,
and (c) using these models to inform decisions.
This definition is mechanism-agnostic, analogous to behav-
ioral economics measuring “as-if” rationality without as-
suming perfect utility maximization. We decompose ToM-
relevant capabilities into four axes, each measured by a
dedicated game grounded in established theory (Table 1).
Epistemic State Modeling (ESM) captures the capacity to
model what another agent knows or believes, particularly
when this differs from one’s own knowledge. Text-Dixit
measures calibration accuracy in predicting how confidently
a partner will identify a target from a clue [Gneiting and
Raftery, 2007]. Recursive Strategic Reasoning (RSR) in-
volves nested belief modeling. The Strategic Claim game
instantiates a signaling game [Crawford and Sobel, 1982]
requiring calibrated deception and detection based on op-
ponent modeling. Shared Conceptual Grounding (SCG) in-
volves convergence on shared meaning through mutual mod-
eling. Say the Same Thing requires predicting focal points
[Schelling, 1960] based on mutual salience [Nagel, 1995].
Relational State Modeling (RSM) tracks evolving relation-
ship dynamics. Repeated PD with Promises requires model-
ing trust and commitment credibility over time, grounded in
cooperation theory [Axelrod and Hamilton, 1981, Embrey
et al., 2018] and the folk theorem literature [Fudenberg and
Maskin, 1986].
All games satisfy five design principles: procedural genera-
tion resists memorization; observable signals support belief
updating; coherent incentive structures create strategic trade-
offs; each game admits equilibrium characterization; and
private information is sampled independently, preserving
role symmetry.

subgame perfect equilibrium (SPE), the unique prediction
for the finitely repeated PD with commonly known upper
bound Tmax = 15 is mutual defection in every round, by

despite the rational prediction.
3.3
GAME 3: SAY THE SAME THING (SCG AXIS)
Say the Same Thing is a pure coordination game where play-
ers start with distinct words and simultaneously select new
words each round, aiming for exact match within 20 rounds.
Payoff decreases with convergence time (1 −t/20). Under
Schelling’s focal point theory [Schelling, 1960], efficient
play requires mutual salience estimation (formal specifica-
tion and equilibrium characterization in Appendix).
3.4
GAME 4: TEXT-DIXIT (ESM AXIS)
Text-Dixit is a signaling game where a storyteller observes a
target among 6 surreal scenes, provides a clue, and predicts
the guesser’s confidence. The guesser selects a scene and re-
ports confidence p. Scoring rewards calibration accuracy via
quadratic loss: ESM score = 100(1 −(ˆp/100 −p/100)2).
Under proper scoring rules [Gneiting and Raftery, 2007],
the storyteller’s optimal prediction equals the guesser’s true
expected confidence, requiring accurate epistemic state mod-
eling of the partner’s inference process (formal specification
and equilibrium in Appendix).

METHODOLOGY
4.1
MODELS
We evaluate seven frontier LLMs spanning four providers:
GPT-5-mini and GPT-4o-mini1 (OpenAI), Claude Haiku 4.5
(Anthropic), DeepSeek V3 (DeepSeek), Kimi K2 Thinking
(Moonshot), and Gemini 2.5/2.0 Flash2 (Google). The en-
semble includes reasoning, efficient, and open-weight archi-
tectures (full specifications in Table 4, Appendix). All evalu-
ations use temperature T = 1.0 so that mixed-strategy play,
essential for game-theoretic equilibria, is not suppressed.
4.2
EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
From seven models we construct 28 pairings (21 cross-
model and 7 self-play). Each pairing plays 10–18 replica-
tions per game type with pre-sampled conditions that ensure
identical setups across comparisons. The full experiment
comprises 1,855 games across five game types (Strategic
Claim, Repeated PD, STST, Text-Dixit, and sealed-bid auc-
tion control).
Before running experiments, we generate 150 condition sets
per game type: RPD horizon lengths, STST starting word
pairs, Strategic Claim value sequences, and Text-Dixit scene
collections. This pre-sampling ensures reproducibility and
enables paired statistical tests that control for condition-
specific variance.
For ELO computation, all models initialize at Ra = 1500
on each axis. Games are processed in randomized order with
update parameter K = 32. We compute 95% confidence
intervals via non-parametric bootstrap: game outcomes are
resampled with replacement within each model pair, ELO
ratings are recomputed for each of 1000 bootstrap samples,
and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles define the CI. Boot-
strap standard deviations range from 9 to 80 points (median
40) across model-axis combinations, with higher SDs for
models with fewer pairings.
Our power analysis applies Theorem 4. Detecting a 50-point
ELO difference from per-pairing win rates at α = 0.05
requires n ≥356 games (Hoeffding bound). Our design
uses 10–18 replications per pairing, achieving model-level
precision through aggregation across 28 pairings (1,855
total games, 100+ per model). Bootstrap standard deviations
range from 9 to 80 ELO points (median 40), confirming that
rating differences exceeding 50 points are reliably detected
at the model level.

DISCUSSION
This work contributes a methodology for estimating
bounded rationality in AI agents under strategic uncertainty.
Game-specific equilibrium characterizations yield quantita-
tive predictions that support formal hypothesis testing. QRE
parameter inference places rationality estimates on a scale
calibrated against decades of human behavioral data, and
convergence guarantees with explicit finite-sample bounds
ensure that capability measurements have quantifiable pre-
cision.
Our Bayesian λ estimates fall well below human baselines
(λhuman ∈[1.0, 2.5]; Goeree et al., 2016, Camerer et al.,
2004). In Strategic Claim, values range from 0.05 to 0.61,
while in RPD only Kimi K2 exhibits measurable rationality
(λ = 1.10). Kimi K2 is the sole chain-of-thought model
in our ensemble, suggesting that sustaining cooperation in
iterated games may require the explicit multi-step reason-
ing that “thinking” architectures provide. This gap likely
reflects an identifiability challenge rather than an absence of
strategic reasoning: when agents play near equilibrium, as
our convergence data confirms, utility differences between
actions shrink, making λ poorly identified by the logit model
[Wright and Leyton-Brown, 2017]. Despite the low abso-


## Page 8


lute magnitudes, cross-model variation is informative: a
12-fold range in SC λ separates the most and least strategic
models. Moreover, λ rankings diverge substantially from
raw behavioral statistics. The Spearman rank correlation be-
tween λ and aggregate conditional bluff rate is ρ = −0.29
(p = 0.53, n = 7), indicating that the two measures capture
fundamentally different constructs. For instance, Claude
Haiku bluffs most frequently (β = 0.73) yet has the low-
est λ (0.05), reflecting near-random bluffing; GPT-4o-mini
bluffs least (β = 0.11) yet has the highest λ (0.61), indicat-
ing that its rare deviations from equilibrium are strategically
structured. This dissociation—between how often a model
deviates from equilibrium and how rationally it does so—is
precisely what QRE is designed to measure and what raw
behavioral metrics cannot capture.
Model expansion studies (Table 12, Appendix) reveal that
QRE rankings are temporally unstable across model ver-
sions. DeepSeek V3.2 shows dramatically improved strate-
gic sophistication over V3 (ˆλ = 0.7 vs. 0.2; β = 0.288
vs. 0.523), while Kimi K2.5 shifts in the opposite direction
relative to K2 (ˆλ = 0.6 vs. 0.8). Both successor versions
converge to βR10 = 0 by round 10, suggesting improved
equilibrium play, but the non-monotonic λ trajectories cau-
tion against treating any single evaluation snapshot as a
stable capability assessment. Extension to two new model
families (Grok 4.1 Fast, MiniMax M2.5) confirms that the
framework generalizes beyond the original seven models,
with observed bluff rates (β = 0.47–0.50) and λ estimates
falling within the range of the original ensemble.
The SCG axis (Say the Same Thing) warrants separate dis-
cussion. Under exact-match scoring, all model pairs con-
verge to focal points with comparable facility, producing
zero ELO variance. Focal point coordination, it appears, is
trivially solved by current frontier LLMs, consistent with
their strong semantic capabilities. Shared conceptual ground-
ing, at least as measured by lexical convergence, does not
differentiate models at this scale. Future work could increase
difficulty via embedding-distance scoring, domain-restricted
vocabularies, or cross-lingual pairings. We report three in-
formative axes rather than force differentiation where none
exists.
Characteristic failure modes differ across games and mod-
els. In Strategic Claim, common failures include over-
bluffing (bluff rate > 0.5 persisting past round 5) and under-
challenging (threshold ≥6, effectively never challenging).
In RPD, defection cascades following a single defection and
end-game defection spikes occur even in otherwise coop-
erative pairs. In STST, semantic drift (distance increasing
between rounds) and cycling between two candidate words
are the primary non-convergence patterns. In Text-Dixit,
systematic overconfidence (ˆp > p + 20) is the dominant
calibration failure. These failure modes are not uniformly
distributed: DeepSeek V3 exhibits the highest over-bluffing
rate in SC, while Claude Haiku shows the strongest end-
game defection in RPD.
Several limitations bear on interpretation. Our games are
structured strategic interactions; whether results generalize
to unstructured social settings remains open. Equilibrium
convergence is consistent with ToM but also with simpler
mechanisms such as fictitious play or heuristic adaptation;
distinguishing these requires interventional experiments
(e.g., mid-game opponent strategy switches) beyond the
scope of this work. QRE λ and LLM sampling temperature
T both modulate action stochasticity; our temperature abla-
tion (Table 9, Appendix) shows stable behavioral patterns
across T ∈{0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0}, supporting λ as primarily
reflecting strategic rationality, though the confound cannot
be fully resolved without access to token-level logits. All
robustness experiments use self-play; cross-model interac-
tions may produce different dynamics. Prompt framing also
matters: our ablation (Table 10, Appendix) shows that re-
placing the game-theoretic narrative with formal or minimal
descriptions eliminates bluffing entirely (β = 0.59 →0.00
for Claude Haiku), a binary sensitivity paralleling Ullman’s
[2023] finding that minor task variations cause ToM per-
formance collapse [Sclar et al., 2024]. Model capabilities
evolve rapidly, so this framework should be viewed as
methodology for ongoing evaluation rather than a static
assessment. This study evaluates publicly available LLM
APIs in game-playing scenarios with no human participants;
human calibration baselines are drawn entirely from pub-
lished behavioral economics literature.

CONCLUSION
We have presented GToM-Bench, a benchmark that grounds
Theory of Mind evaluation in game theory. The framework
pairs formal equilibrium analysis with QRE-based bounded
rationality estimation and convergence guarantees derived
from martingale concentration arguments. Four games target
distinct cognitive capabilities through procedurally gener-
ated strategic interactions that resist memorization while
remaining theoretically tractable.
Empirical validation across 2,035 games (1,855 primary
plus 180 in robustness studies) with eleven models confirms
the theoretical predictions, and expansion studies extending
to four additional models demonstrate framework generaliz-
ability. Models converge toward Nash equilibria during play,
exhibiting the belief updating that characterizes functional
ToM. Bayesian rationality parameter estimates reveal het-
erogeneity across models and game types, with cross-model
variation providing diagnostic value even when absolute
magnitudes fall below human baselines. Version-stability
analysis reveals that QRE rankings shift across model gen-
erations, arguing for continuous re-evaluation rather than
static capability claims. Prompt sensitivity analysis further
shows that strategic behavior is binary-activated by narrative
framing, a methodological caution for the field.

C
GAME SPECIFICATIONS
Strategic Claim runs for 10 rounds. Each player receives a private value drawn uniformly from {1, ..., 6} and may claim
any value from their true value up to 6. The opposing player sets a challenge threshold from 1 to 7. Payoffs are structured
to create incentives for calibrated bluffing: honest claims yield the claimed value, successful bluffs yield the claim plus 2,
caught bluffs cost 4 points to the bluffer while rewarding the challenger 3, and false challenges cost the challenger 2 points.
Repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma uses hidden horizons drawn uniformly from 7 to 15 rounds (with commonly known upper
bound Tmax = 15). Stage payoffs follow the standard parameterization (T, R, P, S) = (5, 3, 1, 0). Players may exchange
natural language messages before each decision round, enabling cheap talk communication.


## Page 14


Say the Same Thing allows up to 20 rounds of simultaneous word selection. Players start with distinct words and must
converge on an exact match. Starting word pairs are stratified by semantic distance measured via cosine similarity in
embedding space.
Text-Dixit consists of 4 rounds with alternating storyteller and guesser roles. Each round presents 6 AI-generated surreal
scenes: one target and five decoys. The storyteller selects a clue of 2 to 4 words from a provided bank and predicts the
guesser’s confidence from 0 to 100 percent. Scoring rewards accurate calibration of the partner’s epistemic state.
D
HUMAN BASELINE LITERATURE
The behavioral economics literature provides extensive data on human λ estimates across game types. In signaling games,
estimates range from 1.5 to 2.5 [Crawford and Sobel, 1982]. Coordination games yield somewhat lower values of 1.0 to 2.0
[Nagel, 1995, Camerer et al., 2004]. Repeated games show higher rationality parameters between 2.0 and 3.0 [Embrey et al.,
2018]. Beauty contest and p-guessing games find that humans typically engage in 1.5 to 2.0 levels of recursive thinking
[Nagel, 1995].
Our Bayesian LLM estimates of λ = 0.05 to 0.61 (SC) and 0.01 to 1.10 (RPD) fall below these human ranges. This gap
is informative: it may reflect either genuinely lower strategic sophistication or, more likely, an identifiability limitation of
the QRE model when agents play sufficiently close to equilibrium that utility differences become small. The cross-model
variation remains diagnostically useful regardless, as it captures meaningful differences in strategic responsiveness even
when absolute λ magnitudes are attenuated.

To assess whether strategic behavior depends on prompt framing, we run SC self-play games with three prompt variants:

To verify that Bayesian λ estimates are robust to prior specification, we compare posterior means under three Gamma priors:

F.4
MODEL EXPANSION STUDIES
To assess framework generalizability beyond the original seven models and temporal stability of QRE rankings, we conduct
two expansion studies. B1 (New Families) tests two models from providers not in the original ensemble: Grok 4.1 Fast
(xAI) and MiniMax M2.5 (MiniMax), each playing 20 SC self-play games. B2 (Version Stability) tests successors of two
