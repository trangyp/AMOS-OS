Quantification of magnetic interactions in van der Waals heterostructures using
Lorentz transmission electron microscopy and electron holography

Magnetic van der Waals (vdW) materials are promising for memory and logic applications because
of their highly tunable magnetic properties and compatibility with vdW heterostructure devices.
However, in conventional plan-view measurements, coupling between magnetic textures in stacked
layers is difficult to resolve because the magnetic signal is integrated over the sample thickness. Here,
these interactions are quantified in Fe3GeTe2 (FGT)/graphite/FGT heterostructures using cross-
sectional Lorentz transmission electron microscopy and electron holography, enabling reconstruction
of the local magnetic field within and between the layers. Domain alignment weakens with increasing
FGT separation, yielding a dipolar coupling length scale of λ = 34 ± 4 nm for the cross-sectional
geometry studied here, corresponding to the average separation at which domain misalignment
first emerges. This length scale coincides with an approximately 50% reduction in the interlayer
magnetic field relative to bulk FGT. Surface effects result in canting of the magnetic moments away
from the easy axis up to ∼100 nm from a surface. Finally, the domain walls are narrow (∼9 nm),
while micromagnetic simulations reproduce the observed textures without invoking Dzyaloshinskii–
Moriya interaction. These results quantify the internal and stray fields in stacked vdW magnets
and guide the design of devices that require controllable coupling between magnetic textures.
INTRODUCTION
Magnetic van der Waals (vdW) materials have at-
tracted significant attention following recent reports of
intrinsic magnetism in bilayer Cr2Ge2Te6 [1], monolayer
CrI3 [2], and monolayer Fe3GeTe2 (FGT) [3]. Their mag-
netic properties can be tuned by layer thickness [1, 4],
electrical stimuli [3, 5–8], pressure [9], and strain [10],
making them promising candidates for spintronic mem-
ory and logic devices, as well as for quantum information
applications [11–13].
Another advantage of vdW magnets is that they can be
assembled into heterostructures without lattice-matching
constraints [14]. This enables studies of interlayer cou-
pling between vdW crystals, including magnetic interac-
tions between distinct vdW magnets [15], twist-angle ef-
fects in homobilayers [16, 17], and proximity phenomena
such as proximity-induced spin-orbit coupling [18].
In
addition, magnetic vdW heterostructures are promising
for spintronic applications. Relevant device concepts in-
clude vdW-based spin-orbit torque random-access mem-
ory [19], magnetic tunnel junctions [20], and spin valves
[21]. In such devices, ferromagnetic layers are separated
by non-magnetic spacers. Understanding the interactions
between ferromagnetic layers, the resulting magnetic do-
main structures, and the effects of interfaces and surfaces
on the local magnetization is therefore important for the
development of new spintronic applications.
However, in vdW heterostructures imaged using con-
ventional magnetic imaging methods in the plan-view ge-
ometry, it is difficult to determine which magnetic layer
gives rise to a given signal, because the measured signal is
integrated through the thickness of the heterostructure.
To address this challenge, we prepare cross-sectional
lamellae of FGT/graphite/FGT heterostructures, allow-
ing the vertical spacing between the FGT layers to be
controlled using a graphite spacer.
FGT is a metallic
vdW ferromagnet with a bulk Curie temperature (Tc) of
∼220 K and strong perpendicular magnetic anisotropy
[22, 23]. Heterostructures composed of bulk FGT layers
(thickness ≳50 nm along the crystal c-axis) separated
by graphite spacers ranging from monolayer graphene to
110 nm-thick graphite were investigated.
Using
Lorentz
transmission
electron
microscopy
(LTEM) and off-axis electron holography (OAEH), we
visualize the magnetic domain structure in each FGT
layer, as well as the internal and stray magnetic fields.
The cross-sectional geometry enables direct visualization
of dipolar coupling and domain alignment as a function
of FGT separation. Domain alignment decreases with in-
creasing layer separation, yet interactions persist even at
a separation of 110 nm. From one heterostructure, we de-
termine a dipolar coupling length scale of λ = 34±4 nm,
corresponding to the average FGT spacing at which do-
main misalignments are first observed. OAEH further re-
veals that this spacing corresponds to an approximately
50% reduction in the interlayer magnetic field relative to
the bulk FGT value, and that surface effects cause the
local magnetic moments to cant away from the magnetic
easy axis of FGT over distances of up to ∼100 nm from
the surface.

Surface effects
Surface-related effects on the magnetization of the
FGT layers are considered next.
Figures 4(c, d) show
dome-shaped regions near the FGT surfaces, between
magnetic domain walls, where |B⊥(x, y)| t is reduced
compared with the interior of the FGT. The striped
white arc in Fig. 4(c) highlights one such near-surface
region with reduced projected in-plane induction. These
regions extend up to ∼100 nm into the FGT and are
observed both at the outer surfaces of the vdW het-
erostructure and at the internal FGT/vacuum interface
in Fig. 4(c). However, they are less pronounced at the
internal FGT/vacuum interface in Fig. 4(d), where the
two FGT layers are in closer proximity.
This surface effect may arise from several factors, in-
cluding (1) demagnetizing fields near the specimen sur-
face, (2) magnetization canting toward the electron-beam
direction, which reduces the measured projected in-plane
component, and/or (3) a reduced local magnetic moment
near the FGT surface.
To investigate this further, the expected specimen
magnetization was reconstructed using a model-based it-
erative reconstruction approach with measured magnetic
phase images as input [41]. The reconstructed magneti-
zation maps are shown in Fig. S7. From these magnetiza-
tion maps, the |B⊥(x, y)| t distribution was computed, as
shown in Fig. 4(g, h). Importantly, the effects of demag-
netizing fields are not included in the model-based itera-
tive reconstruction, making it possible to assess whether
the observed near-surface reduction can be explained
by demagnetization alone. Line profiles extracted from
these reconstructions (Fig. 4(i, j)) closely match those ob-
tained directly from the experimental data (Fig. 4(e, f)).
In particular, the maximum magnetic field values within
the vacuum spacer, indicated by the horizontal green and
red dashed lines in Fig. 4(j), are ∼105 and ∼160 mT, re-
spectively, in good agreement with the values extracted
from the experimental data. Nevertheless, the reduced
|B⊥(x, y)| t near the free surfaces persists, indicating that
this surface effect cannot be explained by demagnetiza-
tion alone.
Because the reduced |B⊥| t near the free FGT surfaces
persists after model-based reconstruction, the diminished
projected in-plane induction is interpreted as magnetiza-
tion canting relative to the electron-beam direction. This
effect is less pronounced when the interlayer gap is small
(Fig. 4(d)), consistent with dipolar coupling to the adja-
cent FGT layer constraining the magnetization near the
internal interface. Confirming this interpretation would
require a technique such as vector field electron hologra-
phy [42].

In summary, OAEH quantifies the magnetic field and
interlayer dipolar coupling in magnetic vdW heterostruc-
tures. In the interior of FGT, projected in-plane induc-
tion values of ∼130–220 mT are extracted. Domain align-

roughly 50% relative to the bulk value, which occurs at

dipolar-field-driven canting despite the strong perpendic-

Magnetic domain walls in FGT
The domain wall topology in the FGT crystals is in-
vestigated in this section. The nature of domain walls in
FGT–particularly whether they are N´eel- or Bloch-type–
remains under debate. In LTEM studies of vdW magnets
imaged in plan view, domain wall contrast that appears
only upon tilting the sample is often taken as evidence
for N´eel-type walls [24–26]. However, in principle, tilt-
induced domain wall contrast is not a unique signature of
N´eel character. Similar behavior can, for instance, arise
from narrow Bloch-type walls [27] or from Bloch-type
walls in multilayer structures with alternating chirality
in adjacent layers [28, 29].
A closely related question concerns the role of DMI in
FGT. Because bulk FGT is centrosymmetric, it is not
expected to host an intrinsic bulk DMI. Nevertheless,
N´eel-type walls are often interpreted as evidence for an
effective DMI [18, 30, 44]. One possible origin is bulk de-
fects [31]. For example, Fe deficiency has been reported
to stabilize N´eel-type walls in Fe3−δGeTe2 with δ ∼0.3,
whereas crystals with δ ∼0 exhibit Bloch-type walls [24].
Another possible origin is surface effects, such as oxida-
tion, which have been reported to yield N´eel-type domain
walls [25, 30]. However, it remains an open question how
such surface effects influence the domain wall topology
throughout the thickness of vdW magnets.

Micromagnetic simulations
Finally, micromagnetic simulations were performed for
a sample with a geometry similar to that shown in Fig. 2.
The simulated structure (Fig. 7(a)) consists of two stripes
with a length of approximately 2.3 µm along the x-

The easy-axis anisotropy is oriented along the y-axis.
The stripe width and length are indicated in the figure.

Overall, domain-wall contrast is observed only upon
tilting the sample in plan-view LTEM imaging, consis-
tent with previous reports concluding that the domain-
wall topology is N´eel-type, and suggesting that N´eel-
type domain walls are favored in off-stoichiometric FGT.
However, OAEH data from cross-sectional lamellae show
that the domain walls are very narrow, in line with the
large magnetic anisotropy of FGT. Hence, it is possible
that the tilt-dependent LTEM contrast arises from the
small domain-wall width. Finally, micromagnetic simu-

that DMI is not required to reproduce the domain struc-
ture observed in our samples.
These results highlight
that additional experiments are needed to determine the
domain-wall character unambiguously in FGT. One pos-
sible approach would be to apply an in-plane magnetic
field during LTEM imaging of plan-view samples, since
Bloch- and N´eel-type walls are expected to respond dif-
ferently to in-plane fields [30].
CONCLUSIONS AND OUTLOOK
We have quantified magnetic interactions between
FGT layers in FGT/graphite/FGT vdW heterostruc-
tures with varying FGT separations by directly imaging
the domain structure and measuring the magnetic field
within and between the FGT layers.
A characteristic
dipolar-interaction length scale of λ = 34 ± 4 nm is ex-
tracted for the cross-sectional lamella geometry studied
here, corresponding to the average separation at which
the first misalignment between the layers is observed. In
addition, magnetic induction measurements show that a
separation on the order of λ corresponds to an approxi-
mately 50% reduction in the field within the spacer rel-
ative to that in the interior of the FGT. Together, these
results connect a directly observed characteristic coupling
length (λ) to a quantitative reduction in the local mag-
netic field within the vacuum spacer and demonstrate
that the effective interaction strength between vertically
stacked FGT layers can be tuned from strong to weak
coupling through the interlayer separation and sample
geometry.
Furthermore, surface effects lead to canting of the lo-
cal magnetic moments and reduce the projected in-plane
magnetic induction over length scales of up to ∼100 nm
from a free FGT surface. Signatures of partial flux clo-
sure near domain corners are also observed, indicating
dipolar-field-driven canting despite the strong perpen-
dicular anisotropy of FGT. These findings suggest that
surface effects can dominate the local magnetization in
flakes with thicknesses ≲100 nm.
Considering the magnetic domain walls, previous re-
ports have found that Fe-deficient FGT hosts N´eel-type
walls, whereas stoichiometric FGT hosts Bloch-type walls
[24]. Our crystals are Fe-rich (Fe3.31GeTe1.98) and show
tilt-dependent contrast in LTEM that is consistent with
prior reports of N´eel-type walls. This may indicate that
N´eel-type domain walls are favored in off-stoichiometric
FGT. Nevertheless, we emphasize that the OAEH data
show that the domain walls are very narrow (∼9 nm
wide), which could explain the tilt-dependent LTEM con-
trast without requiring N´eel-type domain walls.
Mi-
cromagnetic simulations show that DMI is not required
to reproduce the experimentally observed domain struc-
tures in the heterostructures and confirm that N´eel- and
Bloch-type walls produce very similar contrast in LTEM
and OAEH data. These results indicate that additional
methods, such as application of in plane magnetic fields
to plan-view samples [30], are required to determine the
domain-wall type unambiguously.
Overall, these results provide insight into interlayer
magnetic coupling in magnetic vdW heterostructures, as
well as into surface effects in device-relevant heterostruc-
tures for spintronic and quantum information applica-
tions.
We anticipate that electron holography studies
of cross-sectional specimens can be extended to probe
proximity interactions between vdW ferromagnets and
a wide range of other vdW materials, including materi-
als with heavy elements that induce interfacial spin-orbit
coupling, or superconductors, to investigate the interac-
tion between magnetic domains and flux vortices [50].

METHODS
Growth of bulk Fe3GeTe2 crystals
Bulk Fe3GeTe2 crystals were prepared by chemical vapor trans-
port (CVT) in sealed quartz ampoules using chlorine as the trans-
port agent. Fe (99.9%, −100 mesh, Strem, USA), Ge (99.999%,
−100 mesh, Wuhan Xinrong New Materials Co., China), and Te
(99.999%, −100 mesh, Wuhan Xinrong New Materials Co., China)
were mixed in a stoichiometric ratio corresponding to 15 g of
Fe3GeTe2. TeCl4 (99.9%, Strem, USA; 0.6 g) was added as the
transport reagent, and the mixture was sealed in a quartz ampoule
under high vacuum (< 1 × 10−3 Pa) using an oil diffusion pump
with a liquid-nitrogen cold trap.
The ampoule was placed in a muffle furnace and gradually heated
to 750 ◦C for 50 h.
The resulting polycrystalline Fe3GeTe2 was
then homogenized in the sealed ampoule and transferred to a two-
zone horizontal furnace. The growth zone was held at 800 ◦C and
the source zone at 700 ◦C for 2 days.
Subsequently, the growth
and source zones were set to 750 ◦C and 650 ◦C, respectively, for
14 days. Finally, the furnace was cooled to room temperature and
crystals were collected in an argon-filled glovebox.
vdW heteostructure assembly
Graphite and FGT flakes were obtained by mechanical exfolia-
tion of natural graphite (NaturGrafit GmbH, Germany) and bulk
Fe3GeTe2 crystals, respectively, onto Si substrates with 90 nm-
thick SiO2 using 3M Magic Scotch tape.
Heterostructures were
assembled using a dry-transfer method based on a polycarbonate
(PC)-coated polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamp mounted on a
glass slide for optical microscopy. Exfoliation and heterostructure
assembly were performed in a glovebox under an inert N2 atmo-
sphere.
FIB preparation
Cross-sectional TEM lamellae were prepared from as-assembled
FGT/graphite/FGT heterostructures on Si/SiO2 substrates using
Ga+ focused ion beam milling in an FEI Helios dual-beam FIB-
SEM operated at 30 kV. A carbon protection layer was deposited
before milling.
The final thinning on the FIB lift out grid was
performed at 2 kV to reduce surface damage.
Plan-view sample preparation
FGT flakes were obtained by mechanical exfoliation of bulk
Fe3GeTe2 crystals onto Si substrates with 90 nm-thick SiO2 using
3M Magic Scotch tape. Suitable flakes were identified by optical
microscopy. Exfoliation and optical inspection were performed in
an N2-filled glovebox. The flakes were then transferred to TEM
grids (Norcada Inc., Canada) outside the glovebox using cellulose
acetate butyrate as a polymer handle [52]. Finally, the polymer film
was dissolved in acetone, and the grids were rinsed in isopropanol
and dried in air.
Lorentz TEM and off-axis electron holography
LTEM and OAEH were performed in an image-CS-corrected
Thermo Fisher Scientific Titan 80-300 microscope equipped with
two electron biprisms and operated at 300 keV. A liquid-nitrogen-
cooled double-tilt specimen holder (Gatan model 636) was used to
vary the specimen temperature between 95 and 300 K. LTEM im-
ages and off-axis electron holograms were recorded under magnetic-
field-free conditions using a direct electron-counting camera (Gatan
K2 IS). Phase images were reconstructed from holograms using
standard fast Fourier transform algorithms (Holoworks 6 plugin in
Gatan DigitalMicrograph).
The electron phase shift contains both magnetic and electro-
static contributions.
To separate these, holograms of the same
region were acquired at 95 K and at room temperature (above Tc
of FGT), and the resulting room temperature phase map was sub-
tracted from the low temperature one. Magnetic contour and phase
shift maps were generated in DigitalMicrograph, and projected in-
plane induction maps were computed from the phase images using
Eq. 1 with a custom Python script.
