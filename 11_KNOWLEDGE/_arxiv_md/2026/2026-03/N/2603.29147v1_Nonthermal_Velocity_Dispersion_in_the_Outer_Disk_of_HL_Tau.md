ABSTRACT
Turbulence in protoplanetary disks plays a crucial role in the evolution of disk structures and the
planet formation process therein. However, the strength of the turbulence remains unclear in young,
embedded disks surrounded by infalling envelopes. In this paper, we present the first direct measure-
ment of the nonthermal velocity dispersion within the embedded disk around HL Tau, which possesses
a dusty disk with multiple rings and gap structures but is still associated with infalling gas flows
from an envelope. Using ALMA archival data of the H2CO emission, we measured the local line width
through a parametric model fitting that accounts for the contribution of Keplerian shear motion. After
subtracting the thermal component, the nonthermal velocity dispersion is ∼0.15 km s−1 on average
over radii of 80–180 au, and it slightly increases with radius. The estimated nonthermal motions cor-
respond to a turbulent mach number of M∼0.4 or a viscous α value of α ∼0.16, assuming that it is
entirely caused by turbulence and α∼M2. Our analysis also suggests that the H2CO emission traces
near the disk midplane (z ≲0.1R). Turbulence driven by the gravitational instability or infall from the
envelope most naturally explains the large nonthermal motions, considering the large disk mass and
associated infalling streamers. The strong turbulence measured in the outer disk, in contrast to the
vertically settled inner dusty disk, suggests a pronounced radial variation in the turbulence strength
and/or an anisotropic nature of the turbulence within the disk.
1. INTRODUCTION
Protoplanetary disks are expected to be turbulent due
to the magneto-rotational instability or hydrodynamic
instabilities (G. Lesur et al. 2023).
The strength of
turbulence is a crucial parameter in planet formation
models.
The evolution of solid particles within disks
depends on the turbulence strength (T. Birnstiel et al.
2016). Planetesimal formation via the streaming insta-
bility requires the concentration of dust grains that have
settled toward the disk midplane, whereas strong tur-
bulent mixing inhibits this process (J. Dr¸ażkowska &
C. P. Dullemond 2018). The turbulence in disks does
not only affect dynamics of solid particles, but also in-
fluence chemical processes within disks (K. Furuya & Y.
Aikawa 2014). Therefore, constraining strength of tur-
bulence and its driving mechanisms in disks is crucial

for understanding of disk evolution and planet forma-
tion processes.
Turbulence in protoplanetary disks is described by the
dimensionless parameter α in the context of the viscous
α-disk model (N. I. Shakura & R. A. Sunyaev 1973). In
this framework, the kinetic viscosity ν, as a product of
turbulence, is expressed with α as ν = αcsH, where cs
is the isothermal sound speed, and H is the gas pres-
sure scale height.
Although deriving α directly from
observations is difficult, there are a few ways to infer
α through a conversion from observed quantities (G. P.
Rosotti 2023).
Several studies have observationally investigated the
strength of turbulence on scales of ∼30–500 au in disks
in Class II sources (or T Tauri stars; tage∼1–5 Myr).
The most direct way is to measure the turbulent line
width with molecular line observations. Direct measure-
ments of nonthermal broadening of line emission result
in a typical upper limit of ∼5–10% of the isothermal
sound speed in four disks (e.g., A. M. Hughes et al. 2011;

The vertical thickness of the dusty disks has also been

in outer disks (M. Villenave et al. 2025; H. Jiang et al.
2025).
While these previous studies offer strong constraints
on the strength of turbulence in Class II disks, the turbu-
lence strength in even younger disks around Class 0 or I
protostars (tage≲0.1–0.5 Myr) remains unclear. Recent
discoveries of substructures in these young disks hint at
planet formation at this early evolutionary stage (e.g.,
P. D. Sheehan et al. 2020; N. Ohashi et al. 2023), em-
phasizing the importance of the disks around protostars
as the site of planet formation. Therefore, it is crucial
to measure turbulence in such protostellar disks.
HL Tau is a nearby protostar located in the Taurus
molecular cloud (d∼147 pc; P. A. B. Galli et al. 2018).
It has been considered as a Class I/II source, i.e., being
at a late accretion phase, and is still embedded in an in-
falling envelope (C. Mullin et al. 2024). This source also
exhibits a strong molecular jet and outflow (S. Cabrit
et al. 1996; M. Takami et al. 2007), and is associated
with anisotropic inflows connected to the disk on a scale
of ∼300 au, as seen in the HCO+ (3–2) emission (H.-
W. Yen et al. 2019a). HL Tau possesses a dusty disk
showing clear multiple gap and ring structures (ALMA.
Partnership et al. 2015). A previous study conducted by
C. Pinte et al. (2016) found that the dust scale height
is as small as Hd/R < 0.01 and estimated the strength
of turbulence within the dusty rings to be α∼3 × 10−4.
More recently, H. Yang et al. (2025) used the polar-
ized dust emission and derived similar small dust scale
heights at radii of ∼40–120 au.
In this work, we evaluate the strength of turbulence
in the outer disk (r ≳80 au) of HL Tau using ALMA
archival data of the H2CO line through measurements
of the nonthermal line broadening. The two methods
using the dust scale height and the nonthermal motion
are complementary because the dust emission is gen-
erally sensitive to the inner disk while the molecular
line can trace the outer portion of the disk. Measure-
ments of the nonthermal velocity dispersion in embed-
ded disks are challenging due to small gaseous disk size
and central stellar mass, and contamination of infalling
envelopes and molecular outflows.
However, the rela-
tively large disk with a moderate inclination angle of
i ∼47◦(ALMA. Partnership et al. 2015) and its large
protostellar mass make HL Tau a suitable target for the
first precise measurement of nonthermal velocity disper-
sions in embedded disks. Previous observations revealed
spatial and velocity structures of several molecular lines
in HL Tau (A. Garufi et al. 2021, 2022). In these obser-
vations, the H2CO line is found to isolate the disk from
the infalling envelope, while other molecular lines, such
as CO, HCO+ and CS, trace the envelope or outflow.
By utilizing the H2CO emission from the HL Tau disk,
we demonstrates the capability of measurements of the
nonthermal velocity dispersion toward protostellar disks
with a reasonable selection of the molecular tracer.
This paper is structured as follows. Section 2 presents
the data reduction process and observational data. Sec-
tion 3 describes the parametric model fitting approach,
and then Section 4 shows the results of the model fit-
ting.
Section 5 presents an analysis to estimate the
emission height. Section 6 discusses the interpretation
of the results, including potential sources of uncertain-
ties and possible physical mechanisms that can explain
the observed nonthermal velocity dispersions. Section 7
summarizes the conclusions of this paper. Additionally,
Appendix discusses the accuracy and robustness of the
model fitting approach.

2.2. Observational Results
Figure 1a presents moment 0 and 1 maps of the H2CO
emission.
The H2CO emission shows a clear velocity
gradient in the direction of the disk major axis (P.A.
of 138◦; ALMA. Partnership et al. 2015). This velocity
gradient and the disk-like morphology of the moment 0
map suggest that the H2CO emission primarily traces a
Keplerian disk. In a previous study, the moment 1 map
of the H2CO emission is indeed well fitted by a Keple-
rian disk model with a stellar mass of M∗= 2.1 M⊙
(A. Garufi et al. 2022).
The moment 0 map appears
to be a ring with the intensity peak around a radius of
∼100 au. This ring shape arises from the subtraction of
the optically thick dust continuum emission behind the
H2CO emission.
The velocity channel maps of the H2CO emission are
presented in Figure 2a. The channel maps show the typ-
ical butterfly pattern of a Keplerian disk. The H2CO
emission appears to follow a single isovelocity curve in
each velocity channel. When the emitting surface is lo-
cated at a high altitude from the disk midplane because
of the high optical depth or the vertical stratification
of molecules, the emission follows two isovelocity curves
tracing the upper and lower surfaces of the disk in each
velocity channel (C. Pinte et al. 2018). Hence, the H2CO
emission is expected to arise predominantly from layers
near the disk midplane. A more quantitative analysis
to investigate the emission height is presented in Sec-
tion 5. The H2CO emission exhibits negative intensities
around the disk center in velocity channels near the sys-
temic velocity of 7.24 km s−1, suggesting absorption by
the foreground gas colder than the optically thick dusty
disk. Nevertheless, the absorption is weak such that the
H2CO emission from outer radii of the disk is clearly
detected in these velocity channels.
Figure 1b shows a comparison between spatial distri-
butions of the 1.3 mm dust continuum emission at a
high angular resolution, presented by ALMA. Partner-
ship et al. (2015), and the peak intensity map of the
H2CO emission. The H2CO emission exhibits the high-
est signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) just outside the dusty disk
exhibiting substructures, and extends up to a radius of
∼200 au. Therefore, the H2CO emission mainly traces
outer regions compared to the dusty disk.
For comparison, the HCO+ emission is also presented
in Figure 1c.
The emission exhibits a clear velocity
gradient in the direction of the disk major axis, which
likely traces the rotational motion of the disk. In ad-
dition, faint blueshifted emission and more prominent
redshifted emission are elongated to the northeast and
southwest from the disk, respectively. As discussed in
detail in H.-W. Yen et al. (2019a), velocity structures of
these two components are consistent with infalling mo-
tion and they likely trace infalling gas flows toward the
disk. The HCO+ emission appears to be missing in the
southwest of the protostar, which is due to absorption
by the foreground envelope (H.-W. Yen et al. 2019a).
The absence of clear infalling streamers in the H2CO
emission suggests that it traces the Keplerian disk with

3. PARAMETRIC MODELING
The microscopic nonthermal gas motion can be de-
rived from the line widths of the observed spectra. The
total line width of the observed H2CO spectrum at a
given spatial coordinate is, however, dominated by Kep-
lerian shear motion due to the limited spatial resolution
of the data and small disk size. Hence, we employed a
parametric model fitting approach to measure the non-
thermal velocity dispersion taking into account the Ke-
plerian shear motion. In this method, we fitted velocity
channel maps of the H2CO emission with a parametric
disk model to constrain the physical parameters that
determine the velocity structures of the emission. The
local line width, including both thermal and nonthermal
broadening, was parameterized as a power-law function
of radius in the model assuming no vertical variations.
The temperature structure of the HL Tau disk has been
well characterized in previous studies (S. Okuzumi et al.
2016; H.-W. Yen et al. 2019a). Hence, we evaluated the
thermal component using the temperature profiles con-
strained in previous work. Then, the nonthermal veloc-
ity dispersion was derived by subtracting the thermal
component from the local line width that was deter-
mined by the parametric model fitting.

3.2. Radiative Transfer with Channel-based
Three-layer Approximation
In an inclined disk with finite thickness, gas located at
different radii can lie along a common LOS, leading to
velocity differences that contribute to line broadening.
This velocity shear due to the thickness of the disk is
more significant at smaller radii and can be negligible
when the radial extent of the disk is large (T. Paneque-
Carreño et al. 2024). However, given the small disk size
of HL Tau, the contribution of this velocity shear to the
line broadening needs to be considered.
Although the radiative transfer calculation is required
to account for the effects of disk thickness, solving the
full radiative transfer is computationally expensive. To
enable efficient exploration of a wide parameter space
while accounting for disk thickness, we introduced the
channel-based three-layer approximation, as illustrated
in Figure 3. First, the gas along a given LOS is divided
into cells, each of which corresponds to a distinct ve-
locity channel bin. Because the velocity ranges of these
cells do not overlap, they can be treated independently
in the radiative transfer calculation. The column den-
sity of each cell is then computed by integrating the
gas density along the LOS, approximating each cell by
a thin layer in every velocity channel. The gas temper-
ature of the layer is computed as the density-weighted
mean temperature. This procedure is applied separately
to gas located in front of and behind the dust layer. In
this framework, line broadening due to the disk thickness
is naturally incorporated, as velocity differences arising
from the vertical structure are mapped into distinct ve-
locity channels. Simultaneously, this approximation al-
lows us to solve the radiative transfer analytically by
considering two gas layers ahead and behind the geo-
metrically thin dust layer at each velocity channel.

3.3. Fitting Data
We fitted the observational data with the model us-
ing the Marcov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method.
The fitting was performed in two steps. First, we fitted
the continuum emission obtained from line-free channels
with a geometrically thin dusty layer model to deter-
mine the dust optical depth τd,c. The other parameters
of the dusty disk are fixed based on previous studies. We
adopted parameters for the dust surface density distri-
bution (Rc,d, γd) = (84.2 au, −0.2) from W. Kwon et al.
(2015), which used the same viscous disk model, with
correction for the updated distance. We used the dust
temperature profile Td(R) = 22.5(R/100 au)−0.57 K de-
rived in S. Okuzumi et al. (2016). Then, we fitted the
channel maps of the H2CO emission with the model de-
scribed above using the derived τd,c and the other pa-
rameters of the dust layer. We allowed the parameters of
the gas surface density and temperature profiles to vary
during the fitting to match the intensity of the model
and observed image cubes.
However, the derived gas
surface density and temperature profile would not be
very reliable because only a single rotational transition
is used in the fitting. Therefore, uncertainties of the gas
temperature and thermal line broadening were discussed
separately.
Several velocity channels were excluded from the fit-
ting procedure. The H2CO emission exhibits negative
intensities near the disk center in the velocity range
of 5.92–7.71 km s−1, as noted in Section 2.2.
These
channels were therefore excluded, as they are likely
affected by absorption from the foreground envelope.
In addition, a careful inspection of the observed spec-
tra indicates that channels between 8.86 km s−1 and
10.32 km s−1 are likely contaminated by emission asso-
ciated with an infalling streamer, which is discussed in
more detail in Section 4. Hence, these velocity chan-
nels were also excluded from the fitting. We note that
the line velocity dispersion can still be reasonably con-
strained despite the exclusion of some velocity channels,
since the spatial extent of the emission in individual
channel maps retains information on the line broadening
(S. Guilloteau et al. 2012; K. M. Flaherty et al. 2015; K.
Flaherty et al. 2020).
The MCMC sampling was performed using the
Python package emcee (D. Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013)
with 26 walkers and 2500 steps, with the first 2000 steps
discarded as burn-in. All fixed and best-fit parameters
with their prior ranges in the fitting for the H2CO emis-
sion are summarized in Table 1.

6. DISCUSSION
6.1. Uncertainties
The uncertainty of the nonthermal velocity dispersion
is estimated to be ∼30 m s−1 based on statistical errors
of ∆V and uncertainties in the gas temperature, which
are derived from a temperature range of the outer disk
of HL Tau reported in previous studies, as discussed
in Section 3.4. C. Carrasco-González et al. (2019) de-
rived a significantly higher dust temperature than the
temperature range suggested in other studies.
When
adopting their high dust temperature profile, thermal
velocities are calculated to be ∼0.14–0.17 km s−1 at
radii of 80–180 au, and consequently the nonthermal ve-
locity decreases to 0.10–0.14 km s−1. Nevertheless, the
nonthermal component of ∼0.2cs is still required, where
cs also increases to ∼0.5 km s−1 as the temperature
increases.
In addition to these uncertainties, another possible un-
certainty is the systemic error inherited from our mod-
eling approach. To examine the accuracy of the current
model fitting approach, we applied the method to mock
observational data generated using full radiative transfer
models with known turbulent velocities. We fitted the
mock data with and without channel masking, which re-
stricts the fit to only a subset of the velocity channels, to
assess the robustness of the fitting approach and impact
of the channel masking separately. In addition, we also
tested how the uv sampling and imaging with CLEAN
affect the fitting result with an ALMA simulation. De-
tails of these tests are presented in Appendix A, and the
results of measurements of the nonthermal velocity dis-
persions using the mock data are provided in Figure 11
in the appendix. In these experiments, which adopt the
same density structure of the disk, the nonthermal ve-
locity is recovered to within an accuracy of ∼20 m s−1
when vnth = 0.3–0.5 cs, regardless of the uv sampling
and channel masking. Hence, the measured nonthermal
velocity dispersion of 0.6 cs appears robust against these
uncertainties.
6.2. Origin of the large nonthermal velocity dispersion
Our analysis suggests large nonthermal velocity dis-
persions of vnth ∼0.6 cs near the midplane of the outer
disk of HL Tau. In this section, we discuss the physical
origin of the large nonthermal velocity dispersions.
6.2.1. Turbulence
Turbulence contributes to the line broadening, as we
attempted to measure it through this work.
Assum-
ing that the measured nonthermal velocity dispersion is
purely due to turbulence, we obtain a turbulent Mach
number M∼0.4 in the outer disk of HL Tau. This can
be further translated into the dimensionless parameter
α ∼0.16 via α ∼M2. Several mechanisms have been
proposed as the origin of turbulence in protoplanetary
disks (W. Lyra & O. M. Umurhan 2019; G. Lesur et al.
2023) such as the magnetorotaional instability (MRI;
S. A. Balbus & J. F. Hawley 1991), vertical share insta-
bility (VSI; R. P. Nelson et al. 2013; M.-K. Lin & A. N.
Youdin 2015; Y. Fukuhara et al. 2021) and the gravita-
tional instability (GI; K. M. Kratter & G. Lodato 2016).
Infall is also a possible driving source of turbulence, as
the HL Tau disk is associated with an infalling envelope
and streamers (G. Lesur et al. 2015; P. Hennebelle et al.
2017; A. Kuznetsova et al. 2022).
Local numerical simulations of MRI show that the
MRI-driven turbulence can be as strong as M ∼0.4
or α ∼0.1 at high disk altitudes (z/R > 0.2), but is
typically much smaller than 0.1 cs near the disk mid-
plane (z/R < 0.1) (S. Okuzumi & S. Hirose 2011; J. B.
Simon et al. 2018). The MRI can drive a strong turbu-
lence of M∼0.4 even near the disk midplane when the
magnetic field is strongly coupled with gas in a condi-
tion nearly ideal MHD (X.-N. Bai & J. M. Stone 2011;
S. Okuzumi & S. Hirose 2011; J. B. Simon et al. 2018).
However, such a condition is usually not expected in
the midplane of protoplanetary disks at a large radius
of ∼100 au because of the low ionization degree of the
disk gas. Similarly, numerical simulations suggest that
VSI drives turbulence of ∼0.1 km s−1, or M ∼0.3 at
most (M. Flock et al. 2020; G. Lesur et al. 2025), which
is smaller than the value suggested by this work. Hence,
it would be difficult to explain the observed large veloc-
ity dispersion with MRI or VSI turbulence.

mal velocity. Indeed, previous observational work sug-
gests that the HL Tau disk is subject to GI. The crite-

dius, given the radial dependence of typical temperature
and surface density profiles of protoplanetary disks, and
Keplerian angular velocity. Consequently, it is naturally
expected that a disk becomes GI unstable toward outer
radii. This tendency also aligns with the fact that tur-
bulence is as weak as α ≤10−4 in the inner disk of HL
Tau (C. Pinte et al. 2016; H. Yang et al. 2025), i.e., im-
plying that the disk is gravitationally stable in the inner
(low α) region and unstable in the outer (high α) region.
The possibility that the outer disk is gravitationally
unstable is further supported by the expectation that
the disk is in an increasing phase of disk mass.
The
mass infall rate from the envelope to the disk in HL Tau
has been estimated to be 2.2 × 10−6 M⊙yr−1 based on
an infalling arc structure on 1000 au scale, detected in
the C18O emission (H.-W. Yen et al. 2017, 2019b). The
mass accretion rate onto the protostar is estimated to
be 1.6 × 10−7 M⊙yr−1 based on a spectroscopy (R. J.
White & L. A. Hillenbrand 2004), which is smaller than
the mass infall rate by an order of magnitude. Hence,
the disk should grow on a timescale of 0.1 Myr to reach
a GI unstable regime, Mdisk/M∗∼0.1.

Gravitational instability is a self-regulating process:
the development of spiral arms and the subsequent gen-

stabilizes the disk. As a result, the instability (and tur-
bulence) is suppressed on a timescale of the local or-
bital period, and renewed mass supply is required for
the instability to grow again and maintain the turbu-
lence. For HL Tau, the orbital timescale at R = 100 au

absence of spiral structures in the outer disk.
6.2.2. Non-turbulence origin
One may wonder whether the disk wind contributes to
the line broadening rather than turbulence, as the bipo-
lar outflow has been observed in HL Tau (S. Cabrit et al.
1996; M. Takami et al. 2007). The velocity structures
of the H2CO emission mostly follow Keplerian rotation
of the disk and do not show any features of outflowing
gas motions, as seen in Section 2.2 and Section 4. The
base of the disk wind could contaminate and contribute
to the total line width. However, the base of the disk
wind is typically located at the FUV ionized surface at
z/H ≳5 (X.-N. Bai & J. M. Stone 2013; O. Gressel et al.
2015; G. Lesur et al. 2023), which is much higher than
the layer traced by the H2CO emission. Furthermore,
it is expected that the gas density at the wind base is
too low to contribute the line emission. Hence, it is un-
likely that the disk wind is responsible for the observed
nonthermal velocity dispersion.
Radial gas flows within the disk plane are naturally ex-
pected in the accretion disk, which could contribute to
the line broadening but is typically negligible (C. Pinte
et al. 2022). In the frame work of the viscous disk model
(N. I. Shakura & R. A. Sunyaev 1973), the radial accre-
tion velocity within the disk is expressed as

be significantly affected by these possible non-Keplerian
motions associated with the known gaps.

6.4. Comparison to Measurements in Class II Disks
Direct measurements of local, nonthermal motions us-
ing similar modeling approaches have been conducted
toward six Class II disks so far, among which four disks
show low turbulence with upper limits of ≲0.1cs and
two disks (DM Tau and IM Lup) exhibit strong tur-
bulence (K. Flaherty et al. 2020, 2024; T. Paneque-
Carreño et al. 2024; C. Hardiman et al. 2026).
For
the DM Tau disk, K. Flaherty et al. (2020) measured
turbulence of 0.25–0.33 cs based on the CO J = 2–1
emission. More recently, C. Hardiman et al. (2026) ana-
lyzed the CO J = 3–2 emission toward the same source
with a more comprehensive radiative transfer model, re-
porting a slightly higher value of 0.4 cs. For IM Lup,
turbulent velocities of 0.18–0.3 cs and 0.5–0.7 cs have
been reported with CO isotopologues (K. Flaherty et al.
2020) and the CN emission tracing a height of ∼2H (T.
Paneque-Carreño et al. 2024), respectively.
The tur-
bulent velocity measured by this work vnth = 0.6cs is
comparable to the latter measurement for IM Lup, al-
though the traced emitting heights are different. While
the origin of these turbulent motions detected in the
Class II disks are still under debate, the IM Lup disk
has been suggested to be under GI because of its large
disk mass and two symmetric spiral arms (S. M. An-
drews et al. 2018; T. C. Yoshida et al. 2025; T. Ueda
et al. 2024). Hence, the similar turbulent strengths in-
ferred for the two sources may be consistent with GI-

lence is generated by spiral density waves. Currently,
HL Tau is the only source associated with an infalling

would hint at an evolutionary trend of turbulence in
protoplanetary disks and also roles of infall in driving
turbulence.
6.5. Implication for Planet Formation
Given the multiple, settled dusty rings of the inner
disk of HL Tau, the strong nonthermal motions in the
outer disk suggests a large variation in the strength of
turbulence across the disk radii, and/or anisotropic na-
ture of turbulence. The rings and gaps of the HL Tau
disk could be explained by pressure bumps produced
by embedded planets (R. Dong et al. 2015), although
their origins are still under debate. High-density rings
trapped by the pressure bump can be suited sites for
planet formation (D. Carrera et al. 2021). Strong tur-
bulence in the outer disk suggests a high mass accre-
tion rate from the outer disk to the dusty rings possi-
bly pressure-trapped. With the mass accretion rate in
the outer disk estimated with Equation (15), about two
Jupiter masses would be fed to an outermost ring within
the local orbital timescale of ∼650 yr. The combination
of pressure bumps and strong turbulence in the outer
disk could foster the planet formation process within a
pressure-trapped ring by efficiently accumulating mass,
and the HL Tau disk could be a unique case to study
such a planet formation process.
7. CONCLUSIONS
We presented the first direct measurement of the non-
thermal velocity dispersion in an embedded disk asso-
ciated with an infalling envelope and streamers, using
ALMA archival data of the H2CO (31,2–21,1) line of HL
Tau. With the parametric model fitting approach, we
detected a nonnegligible nonthermal velocity dispersion
of ∼0.15 km s−1, or M ∼0.4 on average over radii of
80–180 au in the HL Tau disk with an increasing radial
trend. Turbulence driven by GI or infall most naturally
explains the large nonthermal velocity dispersion among
several possibilities including nonturbulent mechanisms.
Observations in molecular lines at high angular resolu-
tions would enable further investigation of potential spi-
ral structures in the outer disk, which are supposed to
be present if GI or infall induces strong turbulence via
spiral density waves. Regardless of its origin, the strong
turbulence in the outer disk, in contrast to weak tur-
bulence within the well settled dusty rings, could play
a significant role in the planet formation process by ef-
ficiently accumulating mass from the outer disk to the
dusty ring.

To examine the robustness of the parametric model-

to mock line data, which we generated through the full

The DSHARP opacity table was
