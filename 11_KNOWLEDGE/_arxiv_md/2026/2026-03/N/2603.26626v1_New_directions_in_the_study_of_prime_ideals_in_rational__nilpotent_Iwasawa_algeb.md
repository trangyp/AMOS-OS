Abstract
Let G be a nilpotent p-valuable (compact p-adic Lie) group. There is an ongoing investi-
gation into the prime ideals of its completed group algebra (Iwasawa algebra) Zp[[G]], and
there remains an open conjecture that they can all be proved to have a canonical standard
form. We very this conjecture for several new classes of nilpotent groups, including those
corresponding to the positive subalgebra of almost all classical and exceptional types,
curiously excluding those of type C.

of some GLn(Zp) consisting of upper-triangular matrices congruent to the identity mod p (if
p > 2) or mod 4 (if p = 2).

Now fix a finite extension of fields K/Qp, with ring of integers O, uniformiser π, and residue
field k = O/π.
There is an ongoing investigation into the prime ideals of OG := O[[G]],
especially when O = Zp, beginning with the paper [3], and advanced in several subsequent
articles, including [5], [2], [7], [9], [4] and [12].
We say that a prime ideal P of OG is standard if we can find a closed normal subgroup H of
G such that G∗:= G/H is p-valuable and the image of P under the surjection OG →OG∗is
centrally generated. We say that P is virtually standard if P ∩OU is standard for some open
subgroup U of G [9, Definition 1.1].
The main conjecture in the study of prime ideals in Iwasawa algebras (see e.g. [9, Conjecture
1.2]) roughly states that all prime ideals in OG are virtually standard. This is now known for
large classes of prime ideals in various groups, but in general it remains open.
To simplify the conjecture, fix a prime ideal P of OG and set
P † = ker(G →OG× →(OG/P)×),
a closed normal subgroup of G: then P contains the augmentation ideal ε = (P † −1)OG, so
we can pass to the quotient
P ∗= P/ε ⊆OG/ε ∼= OG∗,
where G∗= G/P †. Then P ∗is a prime ideal of OG∗, and now we have (P ∗)† = 1, so to prove
P is standard, we only need to prove that P ∗is centrally generated.
Note: The resulting group G∗may no longer be p-valuable, as it may no longer be torsion-free.
However, there is a finite normal subgroup F ∗≤G∗such that the quotient G∗/F ∗is p-valuable,
and P ∗∩OF ∗is the intersection of a G-orbit of prime ideals of OF ∗∼= O[F ∗], the (ordinary)
group algebra of F ∗. Hence it is considered relatively harmless to assume that F ∗= 1, or
equivalently to assume without loss of generality from the start that P † = 1.
We say that a prime ideal P of OG is faithful if P † = 1. Then setting Z = Z(G) as the centre
of G, then our main conjecture can be restated as follows.
Conjecture A. If P is a faithful prime ideal of OG, then P = (P ∩OZ)OG.
There are two natural cases to consider here.
1. If π ∈P, we may quotient out by (π) and pass to kG := k[[G]] ∼= OG/(π). This version
of Conjecture A was asked as [3, Question O], and has since been proven true for G
nilpotent [2, Theorem 8.4], and for G of the form Zd
p ⋊Zp [7, Theorem 1.4].
2. If π ̸∈P, we may invert π and pass to KG := K ⊗O OG = OG[ 1

(Warning: KG is not the same as K[[G]]. For example, if we take Zp[[x]] and invert p, we do
not get Qp[[x]].)

In this paper, we restrict to case 2, about which much less is known. We will also assume that
G is a nilpotent group, and we want to build on the results from [9] and try to prove Conjecture
A for all prime ideals, rather than just primitive ones.
In §3, we will recall a certain characteristic abelian subgroup A(G) of G, originally constructed
in [9, §3.5], containing the centre Z. When approaching the conjecture for general prime results,
the strongest result to date is:
Theorem. [9, Theorem B] Setting A := A(G), if P is a faithful prime ideal of KG, then
P = (P ∩KA)KG.
Of course, if A(G) = Z, then Conjecture A follows immediately from this theorem. There are
cases when this is true, for example when G has nilpotency class 2, but it is sadly false in the
vast majority of cases of interest. Indeed, whenever the second centre Z2(G) is abelian (which
is the case for all nilpotent groups arising as positive root subgroups) then A(G) is strictly
larger than the centre.
Moreover, the construction of A(G) relies on an iterative procedure (which can be of arbitrary
finite length), and so it can be time-consuming to compute.
In this note, we will define a smaller subgroup B(G) of G, with Z ⊆B(G) ⊆A(G). This
subgroup is much more explicitly constructed, and can often be computed more easily. Our
main result is very similar:
Theorem 1. (Theorem 3.10) Setting B := B(G), if P is a faithful prime ideal of KG, then
P = (P ∩KB)KG.
Needless to say, how much of an improvement this result is depends entirely on how much
smaller than A(G) the subgroup B(G) actually is. There are several cases when B(G) = A(G)
(including of course all the cases when A(G) = Z), so this result constitutes no improvement
in this setting.
However, in §4, we will explore a number of examples of groups G for which we can prove that
B(G) is a proper subgroup of A(G), and in several of them we even have that B(G) = Z. Using
our main theorem this is enough to prove Conjecture A for these groups, and in particular for
the majority of those groups that arise from Lie theory:
Theorem 2. (Corollary 4.9) If G is nilpotent and Lie(G) is the positive root subalgebra of
a split-semisimple Qp-Lie algebra, containing no component of type C. Then for any faithful
prime ideal P of KG, (P ∩KZ)KG = P.
It is curious that this approach fails when G is a positive root subgroup of type C, though we
still believe the conjecture to be true in this case.

Lemma 3.3. A(G) is a proper subgroup of G if and only if Z(G) ̸= Z2(G), and if G is nilpotent

Proof. Since A1 = CG(Z2(G)), it is clear that this is a proper subgroup of G if and only if

Lemma 3.6. If Z2(G) is abelian and Z2(G) ̸= Z(G), then Z2(G) ⊆A(G) and A(G) ̸= Z(G).

Since Z2(G) is abelian for many nilpotent groups of interest, we would like a stronger control

New Control Theorem: The subgroup B(G)

For all g ∈G : if (g, (g, H)) = 1 then (g, H) = 1.
(∗)

Lemma 3.7. S contains a unique maximal element, i.e. among all subgroups H of G satisfying

4.4
Obstacles to a complete proof
While the methods outlined in this paper allow us to establish our Conjecture A for a wide
range of new examples, they are still not sufficient to prove it in full generality, because there
are still many examples of nilpotent groups G such that B(G) ̸= Z(G), as demonstrated by
Corollary 4.11, and also the following result.
Lemma 4.13. Suppose G contains a closed, isolated, abelian normal subgroup H such that
G/H ∼= Zp. If G has nilpotency class at least 3 then B(G) = A(G) = H and B(G) ̸= Z(G).
Proof. We will show that H satisfies (∗), and thus H ⊆B(G) ⊆A(G) by definition. So since H
and A(G) are both closed and isolated by Lemma 3.8, it follows that A(G) = H or A(G) = G.
But G is nilpotent, so A(G) ̸= G by Lemma 3.3, thus A(G) = B(G) = H.
Suppose g ∈G and (g, (g, H)) = 1. If g ∈H then (g, H) = 1 since H is abelian, so assume
for contradiction that g /∈H. Then since G/H ∼= Zp, we know that g and H generate an open
normal subgroup U of G. Moreover, since (g, H) ⊆H, and g and H both commute with (g, H),
it follows that (U, (g, H)) = 1, i.e. (g, H) is central in U.
But (g, g) = 1 so this means that (g, U) is central in U by Lemma 2.1, and hence g ∈Z2(U).
But Z2(U) ⊆Z2(G) by the proof of [8, Lemma 4.9]. So g ∈Z2(G), and since (H, H) = 1 this
means that (U, H) ⊆Z(G), so H ⊆Z2(U) ⊆Z2(G), and hence U ⊆Z2(G). This means that
Z2(G) is an open normal subgroup of G, so since it is isolated this implies that Z2(G) = G,
contradicting the assumption that G has nilpotency class at least 3.
So B(G) = A(G) = H, and H ̸= Z(G) since G is non-abelian and G/H has rank 1.
To complete a proof of Conjecture A for nilpotent groups in general, it would be enough to
establish that it holds for groups of the form Zd
p ⋊Zp.
