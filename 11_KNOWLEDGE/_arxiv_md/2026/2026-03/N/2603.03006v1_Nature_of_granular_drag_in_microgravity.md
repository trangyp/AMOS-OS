Nature of granular drag in microgravity
Tivadar Pong´o,1, ∗Tianhui Liao,1, 2, ∗Jinchen Zhao,1 Valentin
Dichtl,3 Simeon V¨olkel,3 Ra´ul Cruz Hidalgo,4 and Kai Huang1, 3, †

The influence of gravity on the drag force acting on a projectile impacting granular media is
investigated experimentally via embedded inertial measurement unit (IMU) sensor and numerically
through discrete element method (DEM) simulations.
As gravity approaches zero, inertial drag
dominates, yielding qualitatively different scaling laws and cavity dynamics.
Analogous to fluid
dynamics, we define a dimensionless granular drag coefficient Cgd, which is found to stay largely at
a constant ∼1.2 in microgravity while an additional term inversely proportional to impact velocity
arises in the presence of gravity. The constant term can be understood from momentum transfer
along the penetration direction while the additional term suggests the influence of internal stress
built-up due to gravity. Similar discrepancy is also found for the initial peak of the drag force. This
analogy provides novel insights into the nature of granular drag in microgravity and sheds light on
future space missions.
Walking on the beach, we experience the drag of sand
under our feet, and very similarly, NASA’s Perseverance
rover experience it while driving on Mars. Along with re-
cent developments in space science and exploration, [1–
3], there is a growing interest in understanding the influ-
ence of gravity on granular drag. Two main motivations
are the development of effectively operational space ve-
hicles with reliable space aeronautics and the application
of projectiles in asteroid impact mitigation studies [1–
5]. In this context, the most relevant physics question
is to which extent the drag force laws known for simple
liquids [6] can be connected to complex granular flows
which are driven far from thermodynamic equilibrium [7].
For fluid drag, continuous investigations since the pioneer
work of Poisson have led to practical ways to classify var-
ious flow regimes and to quantitatively estimate drag co-
efficient [8, 9]. For granular drag, specific tasks are to
understand the dissipation mechanisms experienced by
projectiles and rovers under microgravity conditions [10–
14], and to identify the most significant characteristic
length and time scales of these processes [15, 16].
In experiments,
various non-invasive experimental
techniques have been introduced to track projectiles [17–
19] on granular beds. Based on the measurements, em-
pirical laws and theoretical models of granular drag have
been proposed for inertial and quasi-static regimes [5, 10–
12, 20]. Driven by the widespread applications such as
pile driving and impact cratering [1, 4], general models on
fluid-like drag forces have been introduced, evolving from
Poncelet’s ballistic analysis to unified drag force model
that incorporate depth-dependent terms [5, 20–22]. More
elaborated approaches that include the influence of inter-
stitial air, packing density, cohesion on granular drag, as
well as conditions for infinite penetration of the projec-
tile, reminiscent to terminal velocity in a fluid, have been

cone shape with a certain opening angle, in contrast to

Figure 1 shows a sketch of the setup used in the ex-
periment campaign at the Bremen Droptower (ZARM),
which provides an excellent microgravity condition of
10−6gE with gE ≈9.81 m/s2 gravitational acceleration
on earth [34].
The setup is composed of a cylindrical
polyvinyl chloride (PVC) container with inner diameter
D = 31.0 cm and height H = 21.5 cm. Before each ex-
periment, expanded polypropylene (EPP) particles of a
certain mass are added to the container till filling height
of 15 cm. More specifically, Neopolen P9230 or P9270 [35]
with bulk densities ρb ≈30 kg/m3 or 70 kg/m3 and diam-
eter dp ≈3.4±0.4 mm or 2.7±0.3 mm are used in different
experiments, respectively. Before each capsule drop, the
granular sample is air fluidized to provide reproducible
initial packing density ϕ ≈0.60, which is also used in sim-
ulations. Side view images are captured with a high speed
camera (Phantom FASTCAM MC2).
A loaded spring
mounted on top of the container ejects the projectile into
the granular medium at specific initial velocities v0. A
pre-calibrated IMU sensor board (Mbientlabs, Metawear
C) is embedded in the 3D printed spherical projectile
with a diameter of d = 32 mm and mass mi = 15.72 g.
Acceleration a and angular velocity ω vectors of the sen-
sor are recorded with a sampling rate of 1600 Hz and
subsequently transmitted via Bluetooth to the on-board
controller (Raspberry PI). Because a and ω are measured
in the sensor coordinate system, post-processing involv-
ing stepwise coordinate transformation and integrations
are needed to reconstruct a three-dimensional trajectory
of the projectile [18].
The representative raw accelerometer data shown in
Fig. 1(b) illustrate three transition times (gray dashed
lines): (i) Start of microgravity triggered by capsule re-
lease, measured by the transition from a = gE to 0 with
a ≡|a|. The granular sample expands slightly (∼5%) at
the beginning because of the elastic potential release. (ii)
Launch of projectile by the release of the spring, which is
set to be 0.5 s after capsule drop. Subsequently, a starts
to fluctuate as the projectile moves. When the projec-
tile touches the granular layer after the free-flying period
[inset of Fig. 1(c)], acceleration in the vertical direction
az starts to increase, signifying the influence of granular
drag. Fig. 1(c) shows the reconstructed trajectory from
the IMU sensor, along with the position data captured
by analyzing the side-view images through Hough trans-
formation [36]. Subsequent peaks indicate abrupt events
such as collisions with the container bottom. (iii) Land-
ing of the capsule in the deceleration container, which is
also filled with Styrofoam particles.

As the projectile moves through granular me-
dia, continuous momentum transfer from the projectile
to the grains occurs with the momentum loss of the in-
truder and the gain of granular media being dpi = midv
and dpgm = ρbAdl · v with the stepwise penetration
depth dl ≈vdt, respectively. If we assume, quite ide-
ally, that all momentum being transferred along the di-
rection of v and the granular particles being pushed for-
ward are confined in a cylinder of diameter d and length
vdt, momentum conservation dpi + dpgm = 0 yields
Cgd = Fd0/(ρbAv2) = 1 with drag force under micrograv-
ity Fd0 ≡dpi/dt inertia dominated granular drag force.
In reality, Cgd is slightly larger than one for two possible
reasons: (i) Inelastic collisions of the projectile with in-
teracting granular clusters of average size Nc and normal
coefficient of restitution e ≈0.4 [17], following Clark et
al. [11]. Considering Nc ≈1 for cohesionless particles in
microgravity due to the absence of gravity induced force
chains, we have Cgd ≈1 + e. As a guide to the eyes, we
show two shaded region centered at the dash-dot 1 + e
and dotted 1 horizontal lines in Fig. 3. The width of the
shaded region corresponds to the uncertainty of ρb ≡ρpϕ
with particle density ρp and fluctuating packing density
ϕ. (ii) As revealed by numerical simulation (see insets of
Fig. 4), there exists momentum transfer along the radial
direction, leading to a cone shaped cavity with a certain
opening angle α ≈21◦, which grows slightly with ρb and
v0. Considering the additional factor tan (α/2), we have
Cgd ≈1.2.
Note that the range of drag coefficient is
in agreement with previous work [10, 16, 21]. However,
the above analysis indicates that gravity, as well as the
frictional angle, does not play an essential role in Cgd.

ing force chain network along with collapsing of the cavity
plays additional roles as the projectile penetrates gran-

From an application perspective, it is important to
quantify the initial force peak Fp under microgravity con-
ditions (see Fig. 5). In fact, Fp typically defines a thresh-
old value below which penetration fails [3, 29, 41].
As
shown in the inset of Fig. 5, Fp arises shortly after the
projectile touches the granular surface. The force then
reaches a plateau with fluctuations arising from collisions
with surrounding particles before gradually settling. In
agreement with previous investigations, as v0 increases,
the force values increase systematically, suggesting the
role of inertial drag. Fig. 5 examines the dependence of
Fp as a function of v0. Remarkably, when rescaling the
Fp experimental data by ρb, the data points collapse for
the same ˜g, and are in good agreement with power law
relation Fp = w(v0/vb)β with w and β fit parameters.
The presence of gravity leads to larger Fp and factor w.
Qualitatively, the granular particles are not yet mobilized
at the time of initial impact, thus the projectile interacts
with a larger cluster of particles (i.e., larger Nc following
previous discussion) and consequently leading to larger
peak for ˜g = 1. In microgravity, the transition ˜g →0 ef-
fectively weakens Fp from the collisions. Quantitatively,
the fits yield exponents β = 1.3 ± 0.1 and β = 1.6 ± 0.4
under normal gravity and microgravity, respectively. Al-
though the range of exponent is in agreement with pre-
vious work [16, 32, 42, 43], a higher β in microgravity is
found than that in normal gravity. Further experiments
with a wider range of v0 are needed to understand the
influence of gravity on the initial force peak.

To summarize, this work demonstrates that granular
drag in microgravity condition is dominated by inertial
drag Fd0 = −γv2, which leads to qualitatively differ-
ent dynamics such as infinite penetration. Importantly,
the dimensionless drag coefficient is found to be a con-
stant of 1.2±0.1, which can be understood with momen-
tum transfer. This value sets the baseline for the inertial
drag term, which includes an additional term ∝1/v0
in the presence of gravity. Moreover, the cavity gener-
ated by the projectile forms a cone-shaped cavity with
a fixed opening angle. This investigation paves the way
for quantitative predictions of granular drag coefficient
towards zero gravity, and provides additional insights on
the inertial drag term in the empirical drag force law.
Further investigations on the initial force peak and the
evolution of the cavity will shed light on a more com-
prehensive understanding of granular drag based on first
principles, which can not only help with future space mis-
sions, but also serve as a bridge between Newtonian and
non-Newtonian fluids on drag forces in general.
