1. INTRODUCTION
Let X be a smooth projective complex surface and let, as usual, pg(X) := h0(X, KX) be
the geometric genus, q(X) := h0(X, Ω1
X) the irregularity and κ(X) the Kodaira dimension.
It is a classical result that if κ(X) ≥0 then pg(X) ≥q(X) −1, and if pg(X) = q(X) −1 then
X is birationally equivalent to one of the following:
• a bielliptic surface
• an abelian surface
• a properly elliptic surface X such that the elliptic fibration f : X →C is a quasi-
bundle, namely all the singular fibers of f have smooth support. (cf. [Se93, Prop. 4.2]).
In particular, if pg(X) = q(X) −1 and X has maximal Albanese dimension then X is bira-
tionally either an abelian or a properly elliptic surface.
The above numerical invariants can be defined more generally in the logarithmic setting.
Given a smooth quasi-projective surface V := X \ D, where X is a smooth projective surface
and D ⊂X is a snc divisor, one sets P1(V) := h0(X, KX + D) “first log plurigenus”, q(V) :=
h0(X, Ω1
X(log D)) “log irregularity”, and lets k(V) “log Kodaira dimension” be the Iitaka
dimension of KX + D. One also defines (cf. §2.1) the (logarithmic) Albanese map aV : V →
Alb(V), with Alb(V) a quasi-abelian variety.
In this paper we establish sharp lower bounds for P1(V) in terms of q(V) when V has non-
negative logarithmic Kodaira dimension, and we give restrictions on the possible boundary
examples.

The proof of (1.1) relies on a classical argument involving (a logarithmic version of)
Castelnuovo–de Franchis Theorem due to Bauer [Ba97]: the key point is that on a surface of
maximal Albanese dimension one can construct global 2-forms by wedging global 1-forms.
On the contrary, when the Albanese dimension is 1 there is no apparent connection be-
tween the existence of global 1-forms and that of global 2-forms. Indeed, the proof of (1.1)
in the projective case involves Noether formula, which is not available in the open case. The
proof of (1.2) is based instead on a delicate study of the geometry of the Albanese pencil.
This study is our motivation for introducing the notion of w-fiber of a projective fibration of a
surface, which is analyzed in detail in Section 4 and is, in our opinion, of independent inter-
est. Given a fibration X →B with general fiber of positive genus, we bound from below the
holomorphic Euler characteristic χ(X) in terms of the number of w-fibers, and characterize
the fibrations attaining the bound.

We do not distinguish between divisors an line bundles on smooth projective varieties and
use the additive and multiplicative notation interchangeably.
2. PRELIMINARIES
In this section we fix notation and terminology and we recall some general facts.
2.1. Logarithmic invariants. Given a smooth quasi-projective variety V of dimension n,
we can define its logarithmic (log) cohomological invariants by considering V as an open
subset of a smooth projective variety X such that the complement X\V is a divisor D with
simple normal crossing (snc) support. In this case, we say that the pair (X, D) is a smooth
compactification of V, or a compactification of V with smooth boundary D, and we can
consider the sheaf Ω1
X(log D), which is the subsheaf of the sheaf of rational 1-forms on X
consisting of forms that admit at most logarithmic poles along D. Since V is smooth and D is
snc, this is a vector bundle of rank n on X, with top wedge power Vn Ω1
X(log D) ≃ωX(D),
where ωX = OX(KX) denotes, as usual, the canonical bundle of X. While these sheaves
depend on the choice of compactification, their cohomology depends only on V. In particular
we denote:
• q(V) := h0(X, Ω1
X(log D)) is the log irregularity of V;
• Pm(V) := h0(X, ωX(D)⊗m) is the m-th log plurigenus of V;
• since ωX(D) is a line bundle, we can consider its Iitaka dimension κ(X, ωX(D)).
This is called the log Kodaira dimension of V and denoted by κ(V).
When dim V = 1 we write g(V) instead of P1(V).

The pair (Alb(V), albV) is called quasi-Albanese (or simply Albanese) variety of V.

by alb dim(V). We say that V has maximal Albanese dimension if alb dim(V) = dim V.
We also say that V is of Albanese general type if it has maximal Albanese dimension and
dim V < q(V); in particular the Albanese map of V is not dominant.

2.3. Irrational pencils. Let V be a smooth quasi-projective variety. An irrational pencil of
V of genus γ > 0 is a morphism φ: V →C, where C is a smooth curve with g(C) = γ such
that the general fiber Φ of φ is irreducible.
We recall the following generalization of the Castelnuovo–de Franchis theorem:
Theorem 2.2 ( [Ba97], Thm. 1.1). Let V = X \ D be a smooth quasi-projective variety and
let η1, η2 ∈H0(Ω1

maximal isotropic subspaces arise in this way.

From the work of Iitaka, it is commonly agreed that WWPB-equivalence is the correct
notion to use when studying the birational geometry of open varieties.
3. THE INEQUALITY P1 ≥q −1 FOR SURFACES OF MAXIMAL ALBANESE DIMENSION
Our first result is a straightforward consequence of the generalized Castelnuovo–de Fran-
chis theorem (Theorem 2.2).
Theorem 3.1. Let V be a smooth quasi-projective surface of maximal Albanese dimension.
Then:
P1(V) −q(V) + 1 ≥0.
Proof. Let (X, D) be a compactification of V. The maximal totally isotropic subspaces of

are proper subspaces, since V has maximal Albanese dimension by assumption, and there
are at most countably many, since they are mixed Hodge substructures of H1(V, C). So,
if η ∈H0(Ω1
X(log D)) is very general, then the kernel of the map ∧η : H0(Ω1

Remark 3.2. Theorem 3.1 is sharp. Indeed, if Φ, C are quasi-projective curves with g(Φ) = 1
and g(C) > 0, then V := Φ × C has maximal Albanese dimension and P1(V) = g(C) =
q(V) −1. If Φ is the complement of a point in a curve Φ of genus 1, then V is of log general
type, in contrast with the projective case since surfaces of general type satisfy pg > q −1.
As recalled in the introduction, a projective surface of Albanese general type with pg =
q −1 is an elliptic quasi-bundle on a curve of genus q −1. Below we show the existence of a
fibration onto a curve of genus q −1 also in the logarithmic case.
Proposition 3.3. Let V be a smooth quasi-projective surface of Albanese general type such that
q(V) ≥3 and P1(V) −q(V) + 1 = 0.
Then there is a fibration φ: V →C such that g(C) = q(V) −1.
Proof. Let Y be a compactification of Alb(V) and let (X, D) be a compactification of V such
that the Albanese map of V extends to a morphism α: X →Y. The proof is split in two steps.
We denote by Σ ⊆H0(Ω1

Question 3.4. In the examples given in Remark 3.2 the irrational pencil is the projection
Φ × C →C and has general fiber with g = 1. We have not been able to find examples where

3.1. Surfaces of maximal Albanese dimension and log general type with P1(V) = 1,
q(V) = 2. We have no structure result for surfaces of log Albanese general type with

4. w-FIBERS
In this section we explore the notion of w-fiber (“wicked” or “weird” fiber), which is key
in the proof of the inequalities of §5.
Let X be a smooth projective surface and let f : X →B a fibration onto a curve of genus
b ≥0 with general fiber of genus g > 0. We say that a fiber eF of f is a w-fiber if it is supported
on a divisor F such that pa(F) = 0. Equivalently, eF is a w-fiber if all the components of eF are
rational curves, the support F of eF is snc and its dual graph is a tree.
Remark 4.1. If f is a relatively minimal elliptic fibration then the w-fibers are the fibers of
type I∗
n for n ≥0, II∗, III∗, IV∗. If f has fibers of type II, III, IV then by taking a suitable
sequence of blow-ups one can obtain a fibration f ′ : X′ →B such that the pull backs of these
fibers are w-fibers.

curve with general fiber of genus g > 0 and let ν denote the number of w-fibers of f. If ν > 0,
then:

Proof. This result is certainly well known to experts, hence we just sketch the proof.
After making a ramified base change on B and normalizing, we can assume that there is
a section B →X and hence that there are no multiple fibers. Note that, since fibers of type
II cannot be multiple, the existence of fibers of type II is preserved. After a further ramified
base change, we assume that h: X →B has a stable model Y →B, in which the singular
fibers are replaced by stable 1-pointed curves. Then a standard calculation reveals that, since
X is smooth, the fiber of Y →B corresponding to a fiber of type II curve in h: X →B is the
elliptic curve E with affine equation y2 = x3 + 1; hence Y →B has smooth fibers. Since the
moduli space of elliptic curves is A1, it follows that Y →B is isotrivial with fiber E.
