Mixtures of weakly and strongly hydrated anions induce non-additive changes in the LCST
of thermoresponsive polymers such as PNIPAM and PEO. Large-scale atomistic simula-
tions of PNIPAM–NaI–Na2SO4 mixtures show that these effects arise from the interplay
between favorable PNIPAM–iodide interactions and the depletion of strongly hydrated sul-
fate ions. Here, we investigate whether chemically specific polymer–anion interactions are
necessary to reproduce such behavior. To this end, we study the coil-to-globule transition
of a generic uncharged linear polymer with non-specific polymer–water and polymer–ion
van der Waals interactions in atomistic aqueous solutions of single and mixed salts. We
perform simulations at fixed concentrations of the strongly hydrated salt, Na2SO4, and in-
creasing concentrations of weakly hydrated salts, NaSCN and NaI. The generic polymer
qualitatively reproduces experimental trends in both pure NaSCN and Na2SO4 solutions,
as well as in mixed salt solutions. The model captures the mutual reinforcement between
SCN−accumulation near the polymer and SO2−
4
depletion that gives rise to non-additive
behavior, consistent with atomistic simulations in PNIPAM solutions. These features be-
come more pronounced with increasing background salt concentration and are further en-
hanced upon replacing SCN−with I−, owing to weaker polymer–iodide interactions. Our
results demonstrate that non-specific polymer–ion interactions are sufficient to reproduce
non-additive features, highlighting the dominant role of bulk ion–ion and ion–water inter-
actions.

I.
INTRODUCTION
The relative ability of anions to salt-out proteins in aqueous solution has been ranked accord-
ing to the anionic Hofmeister series.1,2 The typical salting-out order is given by CO2−

trast, weakly hydrated anions, on the right of the series, such as SCN−and I−tend to partition
to macromolecular surfaces leading to salting-in at low salt concentrations.3–5 Recently, there
has been growing interest in understanding specific ion effects on the phase behavior of ther-
moresponsive polymers such as Poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) and Polyethylene oxide
(PEO) .5–10 In particular, the dependence of the lower critical solution temperature (LCST) on salt
concentration has been widely investigated through experimental and theoretical methods in both
single and mixed salt solutions containing strongly and weakly hydrated anions and cations.5–13
In summary, these studies indicate that specific ion effects on the LCST arise from a complex
interplay between ion–ion, ion–water, and macromolecule–ion interactions, suggesting that they
cannot always be described as simple additive contributions of individual ion effects.
A particularly notable phenomenon is the non-additive ion effects on the LCST of thermore-
sponsive polymers in aqueous salt solutions containing mixtures of weakly and strongly hydrated
anions.6,8 Bruce et al.6 experimentally investigated the LCST behavior of PNIPAM in both single
and mixed salt solutions. In aqueous solutions of the weakly hydrated salt NaI, the LCST exhibits
a non-monotonic dependence on salt concentration, increasing initially and then decreasing with
increasing NaI concentration, indicating salting-in at low concentrations and salting-out at higher
concentrations. In contrast, in pure Na2SO4 solutions, the LCST decreases monotonically with
increasing salt concentration, indicating progressive salting-out. These trends in single salt so-
lutions are consistent with the Hofmeister series. However, in mixed salt solutions containing a
fixed concentration of Na2SO4, the LCST shows a non-monotonic dependence on NaI concentra-
tion with three distinct regimes. At low NaI concentrations (region I), the LCST decreases with
increasing NaI concentration, indicating salting-out in the presence of the strongly hydrated back-
ground salt. At intermediate concentrations (region II), the LCST increases with increasing NaI

concentration, corresponding to salting-in. At higher concentrations (region III), the LCST again
decreases, indicating reentrant salting-out behavior. Notably, the trends observed in regions I and
II differ qualitatively from those in pure NaI solutions, highlighting the non-additive nature of ion
effects in mixed salt systems. Note that these observations are not limited to PNIPAM. Bruce
et al.6 have also shown this non-additive behavior in other polymers such as Polyethylene oxide
and Poly(N,N-dimethylacrylamide) in systems containing different combinations of strongly and
weakly hydrated anions. To gain mechanistic insight, Zhao et al.13 performed molecular simula-
tions of PNIPAM in NaI–Na2SO4 solutions at low NaI concentrations (region I), and were able
to semi-quantitatively reproduce the experimentally observed changes in LCST in both single and
mixed salt solutions. These simulations show that iodide ions exhibit preferential accumulation
near the polymer, while sulfate ions are depleted from the polymer surface. Importantly, these two
effects are coupled, such that sulfate depletion and iodide accumulation near the polymer reinforce
each other. Within this framework, the non-additive behavior arises from the balance between
these coupled contributions: at low NaI concentrations, enhanced sulfate depletion dominates
over the favorable PNIPAM–iodide interactions, resulting in salting-out of the PNIPAM, whereas
at intermediate concentrations, increased PNIPAM–iodide interactions become dominant, leading
to salting-in and an increase in the LCST. An important question arising from this mechanism is
whether chemically specific polymer–anion interactions are necessary to reproduce the observed
behavior, and how their importance compares to that of bulk ion–ion and ion–water interactions.
In this work, we study the coil-to-globule transition of a model 32-bead hydrophobic polymer
in pure and mixed salt solutions using molecular dynamics simulations and advanced sampling
techniques, such as umbrella sampling. The polymer interacts with water molecules and ions
only via non-specific van der Waals interactions. We consider the mixed salt system NaSCN–
Na2SO4 at two different background salt concentrations. We also investigate the NaI–Na2SO4
system to examine the effect of different weakly hydrated anions on non-additive salt effects. Our
results show that the model polymer system qualitatively reproduces the experimentally observed
trends in both pure NaSCN and Na2SO4 solutions, as well as in mixed salt solutions across all
three regimes. We observe that the depletion of SO2−
4
ions and the preferential accumulation
of SCN−ions around the polymer are mutually reinforcing. The non-additive features become
more pronounced with increasing background salt concentration. For the same background salt
concentrations, these features are further enhanced when the weakly hydrated anion is changed
from SCN−to I−, which can be attributed to the weaker binding of I−ions to the polymer relative

II.
METHODS
A.
System and theoretical details
In this study, we employ a generic hydrophobic polymer model originally proposed by Zangi
et al.,14 which exhibits a two-state conformational equilibrium between the coil (C) and globule
(G) states, similar to that observed in thermoresponsive polymers such as PNIPAM.15–17 The sim-
plified coarse-grained representation of this model enables efficient sampling of conformational
transitions while avoiding the significant computational cost associated with fully atomistic poly-
mer simulations.
The polymer is modeled as a linear chain of 32 uncharged Lennard-Jones beads, each repre-
senting a united-atom CH2 group with a diameter of 0.4 nm and an interaction strength εpp of
1.0 kJ mol−1. Adjacent beads are connected by a harmonic bond potential with an equilibrium
bond length of 0.153 nm and a force constant of 3.2 × 105 kJ mol−1 nm−2. The angle between
adjacent bonds is modeled using a harmonic potential with an equilibrium angle θ0 = 111◦and a
force constant of 100 kJ mol−1. Water is modeled using the SPC/E model. Non- polarizable force
fields were employed for all the ions. For Na2SO4, the optimized forcefield developed by Bruce
et al. was utilized.6 Model I(4) in the work of Fyta and Netz was used for NaI.18 For SCN−, the
force-field employed in Kˇrížek et al. was used.19 The polymer–water interaction parameter (εpw)
is scaled by a factor λ = 1.075 using the Lorentz–Berthelot combination rule, i.e., εpw = λ√εpεw.
We investigated the coil-to-globule transition of this 32-mer polymer in aqueous solutions of
single and mixed salts using a combination of molecular dynamics simulations and umbrella sam-
pling. In single salt solutions, simulations were performed at varying concentrations of NaSCN
and Na2SO4. In mixed salt solutions, simulations were performed at varying NaSCN concentra-
tions at two fixed concentrations of Na2SO4 (0.5 and 1 m) to examine the effect of background
salt concentration. Simulations were also performed at varying NaI concentrations in the presence
of 0.5 m Na2SO4 to examine the effect of different weakly hydrated anions. The number of water
molecules was fixed at 32695 for all simulations. The number of ions at different concentrations

All molecular dynamics simulations were carried out using the GROMACS (version 2022.1)
software package.20 The equations of motion were integrated using the leap-frog algorithm with
a time step of 2 fs. All bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm. Long-range electro-
statics were computed using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method,21 with a real-space cutoff
of 1.4 nm, a Fourier grid spacing of 0.12 nm, and fourth-order interpolation. van der Waals inter-
actions were truncated at 1.4 nm, with long-range dispersion corrections applied to both energy
and pressure. The neighbor list was updated every 10 integration steps (20 fs). For each sys-
tem, energy minimization was performed using the steepest-descent algorithm, followed by a 2 ns
equilibration run in the canonical (NVT) ensemble, employing the v-rescale thermostat to main-
tain the temperature at 300 K with a time constant of 1 ps. Subsequently, equilibration in the
isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble was performed for 5 ns using the v-rescale thermostat22 and
the Berendsen barostat23 to maintain the pressure at 1 bar. A production run of 400 ns was then per-
formed in the NPT ensemble using the v-rescale thermostat and the Parrinello–Rahman barostat,24
with a pressure coupling time constant of 2 ps. This run was used to compute polymer-ion and
polymer-water radial distribution functions (RDFs), and the final configuration was subsequently
used as the starting point for the umbrella sampling calculations.

III.
RESULT AND DISCUSSION
A.
Polymer in aqueous solutions of a single salt
Figure 1(a) shows the non-monotonic dependence of the relative polymer collapse free energy,
∆∆GC→G, on sodium thiocyanate concentration in aqueous polymer solutions. The polymer chain
prefers a coil-like conformation in pure water (∆GC→G(pure water) > 0). At low NaSCN con-
centrations (cNaSCN < 0.2 m), ∆∆GC→G increases with increasing salt concentration, indicating
that salt addition shifts the coil–globule equilibrium toward the coil state. In contrast, at higher
salt concentrations, ∆∆GC→G decreases with increasing salt concentration, shifting the equilib-
rium toward the globule state.To rationalize these trends, we examine the polymer–SCN−and
polymer–water Kirkwood–Buff integrals (KBIs). Fig. 1(b) shows that the polymer–SCN−KBI,
Gpolymer−SCN−, is higher at low NaSCN concentrations, indicating stronger polymer–SCN−asso-
ciation, which correlates with the salting-in of the polymer. Additionally, Gpolymer−SCN−exceeds
Gpolymer−water at all concentrations except 0.3 m, indicating a higher affinity of the polymer for
SCN−relative to water. Consistent with Eq. 3, this implies preferential binding of SCN−ions
to the polymer (Γpol−SCN−> 0) at all concentrations except 0.3 m. Note that this preferential
binding represents an average over all polymer conformations and is not specific to either the coil
or globule state. Therefore, these results suggest that, at low salt concentrations, the SCN−ions
preferentially accumulate around the polymer and shift the equilibrium towards the coil state. The
radius of gyration of the polymer exhibits similar qualitative trends and its dependence on the salt
concentration can be seen in Fig. S7 in the Supplementary Information.
In contrast, ∆∆GC→G decreases monotonically with increasing sodium sulfate concentra-
tion (Fig. 2), indicating that salt addition shifts the equilibrium toward the globule state. The
polymer–SO2−
4
KBI remains smaller than the polymer–water KBI at all concentrations (Fig. 2(c)),
indicating depletion of sulfate ions from the polymer. The trends observed in Fig. 1 and Fig. 2
are in qualitative agreement with experimental observations for aqueous single-salt solutions of
Poly(N-isopropylacrylamide).6 In particular, non-specific polymer–anion van der Waals interac-
tions are sufficient to induce salting-in at low concentrations of weakly hydrated anions.

These results support the hypothesis that the coil–globule equilibrium is governed by the com-
petition between polymer–weakly hydrated anion interactions, which favor swelling, and depletion
of strongly hydrated ions, which favors collapse. In region I, sulfate depletion dominates, leading
to polymer collapse. With increasing cNaSCN, polymer–thiocyanate interactions become dominant,
stabilizing the coil state in region II. At higher concentrations, the balance shifts again, leading to
reentrant collapse (region III). For 0.5 m Na2SO4, these competing effects nearly compensate, re-
sulting in an approximately constant ∆∆GC→G. This is consistent with experiments showing that
non-additive effects become more pronounced with increasing background salt concentration.

anion shifts toward the right of the Hofmeister series. The radius of gyration of the polymer

IV.
CONCLUSION
We have studied the coil–globule equilibrium of an uncharged linear polymer consisting of 32
Lennard–Jones beads in aqueous solutions of the weakly hydrated salts NaSCN and NaI, in the
presence and absence of the strongly hydrated background salt Na2SO4, using atomistic molecular
dynamics simulations. In pure NaSCN solutions, our results show that the polymer initially swells
and then collapses with increasing NaSCN concentration. The swelling at low salt concentrations
is attributed to favorable polymer– SCN−interactions, which correlate with the preferential accu-
mulation of SCN−ions near the polymer. In contrast, the polymer chain collapses monotonically
with increasing salt concentration in aqueous Na2SO4 solutions due to the progressive depletion of
SO2−
4
ions from the vicinity of the polymer. In mixed salt solutions with a fixed concentration of
Na2SO4, our results show that the polymer initially collapses, then swells, and subsequently col-

salt effects become more pronounced with an increase in the background salt concentration. In
agreement with previous atomistic simulations on PNIPAM–NaI–Na2SO4, we observe that these
non-additive ion effects are governed by the interplay between polymer–weakly hydrated anion in-
teractions (favoring swelling) and the depletion of the strongly hydrated anion (favoring collapse).
Further, there is a mutual enhancement of the preferential accumulation of the weakly hydrated
anions and the depletion of the strongly hydrated anion. Consistently, our results show that the
non-additive effects become more pronounced as the weakly hydrated anion shifts from SCN−
to I−along the Hofmeister series, due to a decrease in polymer–anion affinity. In summary, our
results show that non- specific polymer–ion and polymer–water van der Waals interactions are suf-
ficient to qualitatively reproduce experimentally observed trends in pure and mixed salt solutions.
This suggests that the interplay between ion–ion and ion–water interactions plays a more dominant
role than specific polymer chemistry. Given that generic polymer simulations are computationally
inexpensive and enable the use of advanced sampling methods, this paves the way for systematic
computational investigations of other aspects of mixed salt systems.

To assess the convergence of the free-energy calculations, the potential of mean force (PMF),
denoted as w(Rg), was computed over multiple time intervals from the equilibrated segments of

of ∆GC→G corresponds to the mean of the values obtained from the different equilibrated profiles,
