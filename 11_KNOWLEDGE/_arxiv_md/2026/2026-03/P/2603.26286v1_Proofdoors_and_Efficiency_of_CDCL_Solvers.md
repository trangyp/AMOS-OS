Abstract
We propose a new parameter called proofdoor in an attempt to explain the efficiency of CDCL
SAT solvers over formulas derived from circuit (esp., arithmetic) verification applications. Informally,
given an unsatisfiable CNF formula F over n variables, a proofdoor decomposition consists of a
chunking of the clauses into A1, . . . , Ak together with a sequence of interpolants connecting these
chunks. Intuitively, a proofdoor captures the idea that an unsatisfiable formula can be refuted by
reasoning chunk by chunk, while maintaining only a summary of the information (i.e., interpolants)
gained so far for subsequent reasoning steps.
We prove several theorems in support of the proposition that proofdoors can explain the efficiency
of CDCL solvers for some class of circuit verification problems. First, we show that formulas with
small proofdoors (i.e., where each interpolant is O(n) sized, each chunk Ai has small pathwidth, and
each interpolant clause has at most O(log(n)) backward dependency on the previous interpolant),
have short resolution (Res) proofs. Second, we show that certain configuration of CDCL solvers can
compute such proofs in time polynomial in n. Third, we show that commutativity (miter) formulas
over floating-point addition have small proofdoors and hence short Res proofs, even though they
have large pathwidth. Fourth, we characterize the limits of the proofdoor framework by connecting
proofdoors to the partially ordered resolution proof system: we show that a poor decomposition of
arithmetic miter instances can force exponentially large partially ordered resolution proofs, even
when a different decomposition (i.e., small proofdoors) permits short proofs.

Introduction
The Boolean Satisfiability Problem (SAT) is the canonical NP-complete problem [12], and
it is conjectured to require exponential time in the worst case under the Exponential Time
Hypothesis (ETH) [16]. Modern SAT solvers are predominantly based on Conflict-Driven
Clause Learning (CDCL) and it has been shown that they are polynomially equivalent to the
resolution (Res) proof system under non-deterministic branching and restarts [26]. Further, it
was recently shown that the problem of automating Resolution, i.e., finding a short refutation
even when one exists, is NP-hard [4]. From this perspective, complexity theory predicts
strong limitations on the scalability of CDCL solvers.
Despite these barriers, modern CDCL solvers routinely solve large real-world instances
arising from hardware verification, software analysis, and planning, often involving millions

A natural explanation for this apparent contradiction is that NP-completeness captures

mathematical characterization of which real-world instances are tractable for CDCL and why

In this work, we attempt a proof-theoretic explanation for the efficiency of CDCL based
on the observation that solvers often reason through formulas in a sequential, local manner.
Rather than solving the formula globally, CDCL progressively processes parts of the instance
while maintaining a small summary of the information learned so far. We introduce the
notion of proofdoors, which capture such reasoning via a sequential decomposition of a
formula together with small interpolants summarizing the information learned so far. We
show that formulas admitting small proofdoors – a special case of proofdoors – have short
resolution refutations, and that under suitable configurations CDCL can efficiently refute
such formulas. We also analyze the limitations of such decompositions.
Why existing explanations fall short.
Early attempts to explain SAT-hardness focused on statistical properties of random k-
SAT formulas, such as the clause-variable ratio. Experiments showed that as this ratio
increases, the formulas undergo a phase transition, i.e., below a certain ratio they are typically
satisfiable and above the ratio they are typically unsatisfiable, and solver runtimes peak
near the threshold [21, 10]. This suggested that hardness might arise from formulas lying
near this region. However, these phenomena apply primarily to random instances and fail
to explain the difficulty of industrial formulas, whose hardness is largely uncorrelated with
density-based measures.
Another line of work studies graph-theoretic width parameters such as treewidth, path-
width, and cutwidth of the primal or incidence graph of a CNF formula. For formulas of
bounded width, SAT can be solved in polynomial time via the Davis–Putnam algorithm [2, 14].
However, real-world formulas typically have large global width yet remain easy for CDCL,
for example, BMC formulas [6], indicating that bounded width alone does not explain solver
performance.
Yet another set of theoretical concepts is backdoors and backbones, which capture the
idea that a small set of variables controls the complexity of a formula. Backdoors identify
variables whose assignment reduces the formula to a tractable class, while backbones consist
of variables that take the same value in all satisfying assignments [19]. While these notions
admit strong theoretical guarantees, empirical studies suggest that industrial instances often
have large backdoors, and backdoor size correlates weakly with CDCL runtime. In contrast,
backbone variables appear more frequently in practice, but their presence alone does not
explain how CDCL efficiently discovers the relevant assignments [34].
One empirical explanation is based on community structure, where formulas decompose
into loosely connected clusters of variables and clauses. Several studies report correlations
between solver performance and measures of modularity or hierarchical structure, suggesting
that CDCL benefits from local reasoning within communities [24]. However, there exist
strong counterexamples showing that community structure cannot be used as the foundation
of a theory [22].

Another suggested parameter, mergeability, measures the extent to which resolved clauses
can be combined during proof construction. Merge-based proof systems admit theoretical
analysis and yield insights into certain solver behaviors [31]. Yet it is unclear whether
industrial instances consistently exhibit high mergeability, and empirical evidence connecting
merge counts to solver efficiency is limited [33].
From a proof-theoretic perspective, CDCL solvers generate resolution proofs, and lower
bounds on resolution proof size translate directly to lower bounds on solver runtime. Such
results successfully explain solver failures on well-known hard families, including Tseitin
formulas and the pigeonhole principle [15, 29], but offer little insight into why many industrial
formulas admit short resolution proofs that CDCL can find efficiently in practice.
To summarise, despite decades of work, no existing parameter simultaneously captures
the structure of real-world SAT instances and provides rigorous guarantees on proof size
or proof search, leaving a persistent gap between empirical observations and theoretical
understanding.
Below we describe our contributions in detail.
Our Contributions.
1. Small Proofdoors: Our structural result shows that if an unsatisfiable formula has small
proofdoors, it admits a polynomial-size resolution refutation (Small Proofdoor Theorem).
We further show that a CDCL run (with appropriate nondeterministic branching, value
selection, and restarts) refutes such formulas in polynomially many conflicts. A key
ingredient is a link between CDCL and the CacheSAT algorithm for formulas of small
pathwidth. We show CacheSAT runs in polynomial time on such formulas (CacheSAT
Pathwidth Theorem), and that CDCL simulates CacheSAT (CacheSAT Simulation
Lemma).
2. FP Small Proofdoors: We show SAT encodings of commutativity of floating point
addition satisfy the conditions of our main theorem and thus admit short proofs (FP
Small Proofdoor Theorem).
3. Limitations: We characterize limits of the proofdoor framework by connecting proofdoors
to partially ordered resolution: proofdoors decompose a formula into clause chunks,
inducing a partial variable ordering in the resulting resolution proof. We show CNF
lower bounds on interpolants across these chunks imply partially ordered resolution lower
bounds (Lemma 17). Using this connection, we show a poor decomposition of arithmetic
miter instances can force exponentially large partially ordered resolution proofs, even
when another decomposition (i.e., small proofdoors) yields short proofs (Theorem 27).
4. Undecidability: Finally, we show that deciding whether a general formula family admits
polynomial-size proofs is undecidable (FPSP Undecidability Theorem), highlighting
fundamental limits on any complete characterization of the efficiency of CDCL solvers.
Related Work
A primary inspiration for our work came from SAT encodings of arithmetic identities, such as
the commutativity of integer multiplication (i.e., xy ̸= yx), as studied in [5]. These instances
surprisingly admit short resolution proofs despite being conjectured otherwise. The key
proof idea is to decompose the formula into structured chunks corresponding to the circuit
components and derive the equality of the output bits incrementally. Each chunk involves
only a small pathwidth subcircuit, and the final proof of unsatisfiability can be obtained
by combining the local proofs. This can also be understood as computing interpolants

The classical Davis Putnam algorithm [14], that is the foundation for all CDCL SAT

computing intermediate resolvents that are implied by a part of the formula. The elimination
process can be viewed as computing interpolants; when a variable is eliminated, the remaining
clauses encode all the consequences of the formula only over the surviving variables. While
Davis-Putnam is not polynomial-time in general, makes no attempt to compute small
proofdoors, and may incur exponential blowup, upon reflection, it serves as the first example
of proof generation via proofdoors.
Another relevant work is the well known paper by Ken McMillan [20] on interpolation
model checking, which introduced the explicit use of interpolants extracted from resolution
proofs to refine over-approximations in SAT-based bounded model checking (BMC). In that
setting, interpolants summarize the information obtained from one unrolling of the transition
system and are used to strengthen the constraints in the next unrolling. However, their
work didn’t attempt to explain the power of CDCL SAT solvers. Further, their system
explicitly computes interpolants to speedup the underlying solver. By contrast, we provide
mathematical proofs that certain class of verification instances admit small proofdoors and
hence short resolution proofs. Further, an idealized version of CDCL can leverage them to
find short proofs.

Preliminaries
We denote Boolean variables by lowercase letters (e.g., x). Bit-vectors are written in boldface
(e.g., x), with subscripts denoting individual bits (e.g., xi). Other sets of variables are
denoted by uppercase letters (e.g., S). A literal is a Boolean variable or its negation. A
clause is a disjunction (or set) of literals, and a CNF formula is a conjunction (or set) of
clauses. For a clause C or formula F, we write C ↾α or F ↾α for their restrictions under α.
A resolution refutation of a CNF formula F is a sequence of clauses π = (D1, . . . , DL) with
DL = ⊥such that each Di is either a clause of F, or is obtained by resolving two earlier clauses
on some variable x, i.e., Di = Resx(Dj, Dk) for some j, k < i, where Resx(B ∨x, C ∨¬x) :=
B ∨C.
Resolution can be visualized as either a sequential list of clauses or a directed acyclic
graph (DAG), where each vertex represents a clause and edges represent the derivation
dependencies. A resolution refutation is ordered if along any leaf-to-root path in its DAG,
the variables are resolved in an order consistent with a fixed total ordering of the variables.
The notion of “proofdoors” for SAT solving is closely connected to partially ordered
resolution [17], a relaxation of ordered resolution in which the variable order is a partial
order rather than a total order.

respects the partial ordering ≺: whenever x ≺y and both x and y are resolved on along P,
the path P resolves on x before y.
▶Definition 2. Let X, Y be disjoint sets of Boolean variables. Denote by X ≺Y the partial
ordering {x ≺y|x ∈X, y ∈Y }.

▶Definition 3 (Interpolant [13]). Let Φ(X, Y, Z) = A(X, Z) ∧B(Y, Z) be an unsatisfiable

Depending on context, “interpolant” may refer to either the Boolean function I(Z) itself, or
to a CNF formula that computes I. This notion captures the idea of separating a formula
into two parts and summarizing the contribution of one part to the overall contradiction. In
this sense, interpolants formalize the idea of reasoning across a cut using only the information
that must be communicated between the two sides. Depending on context, “interpolant”

We now introduce the central notion of this work by generalizing the idea of a single
interpolant between two partitions of an unsatisfiable formula into a sequence of interpolants,
allowing us to reason through a formula chunk by chunk. Intuitively, a proofdoor captures
the idea that an unsatisfiable formula can be refuted by reasoning sequentially through its
components or chunks, while maintaining only a summary of the information gained so far.
▶Definition 7 (Proofdoor Decomposition). A proofdoor decomposition of an unsatisfiable
CNF formula F is an expression
F = A1(X1, Z1) ∧A2(X2, Z2) ∧· · · ∧Ak−1(Xk−1, Zk−1) ∧Ak(Xk),

satisfy that I1 interpolates from A1 to A2 ∧· · · ∧Ak, and each subsequent Ij interpolates
from Ij−1 ∧Aj to Aj+1 ∧· · · ∧Ak.

▶Definition 8 (Proofdoors). Let F be an unsatisfiable CNF formula over n variables, and
let c, w, s ≥1 be integers. We say that F admits a proofdoor with parameters (c, w, s) if there
exist an integer k ≥1 and a proofdoor decomposition F = A1 ∧· · · ∧Ak with interpolants
(I1, . . . , Ik−1) satisfying:
1. ∀j ∈{1, . . . , k −1}, the interpolant Ij contains at most c clauses.
2. ∀j ∈{1, . . . , k −1} and every clause C ∈Ij, there exists a set S(C) ⊆Ij−1 of size at
most s such that Aj ∧S(C) |= C.
3. The interpolant Ik−1 contains at most s clauses.
4. ∀j ∈{1, . . . , k}, the chunk Aj has clause-variable incidence pathwidth at most w.
The conditions above are independent. If the interpolants are unrestricted in size, a
proofdoor may exist trivially by doing DP elimination over all non-shared variables from
the prefix. Even if each chunk has a small pathwidth, small interpolants need not exist
without additional structural constraints. In particular, arbitrary partitions of F do not
guarantee small proofdoors. For example, partitioning the formula into single clauses does
not prevent the interpolants from becoming large, and placing most clauses on one side
of a cut may induce large pathwidth. Thus, both bounded interpolant size and bounded
pathwidth are required. Note that this definition allows the interpolant clauses to repeat
among the interpolants. We consider the following decision problem.
▶Theorem 9 (Proofdoor computation is NP-hard). Determining whether an unsat-
isfiable CNF formula F, together with a partition F = A1 ∧· · · ∧Ak and integers c, w, s,
admits a proofdoor with respect to c, w, and s is NP-hard.
Proof Sketch. Consider the special case k = 1, so F = A1. Then the question reduces to
whether F has pathwidth at most w. Since deciding whether a graph has pathwidth at most
w is NP-hard [8], the result follows.

Proofdoor Theorem
We now present our main result, which connects small proofdoors to short proofs and
then to CDCL. Formulas with a small cutwidth can be solved by a procedure called Cache-
SAT [27, 23]. Cache-SAT is a DPLL-style procedure that branches on variables according to a
fixed ordering and caches previously encountered subformulas to avoid redundant exploration.
Under a fixed variable ordering of cutwidth w, only 2O(w) distinct residual subformulas can
arise. We show that the Cache-SAT runtime analysis can be extended to formulas with small
clause-variable incidence pathwidth, and further, CDCL can simulate Cache-SAT. Using
this, we show that each interpolant can be derived with a short refutation from the previous
part of the formula and the previous interpolant. While Theorem 13 could also be obtained
using known results on short proofs for bounded-treewidth formulas [2], we go through the
CacheSAT analysis since it makes the variable ordering induced by the path decomposition
explicit, which in turn naturally suggests the branching order used in the CDCL execution
below.
▶Theorem 11 (CacheSAT Pathwidth Theorem). Cache-SAT runs in time O(n · 23w)
on any CNF formula whose clause variable incidence pathwidth is at most w over a fixed
variable ordering derived from the path decomposition.
The proof of Theorem 11 is in Appendix A.

order π that refutes F using at most poly(m) conflicts.

proofdoor with respect to c, w, and s, then F has a resolution refutation of size O(n23w+3skc).
Furthermore, this refutation respects all of this proofdoor’s cutting partial orders.
Proof. Let F = A1 ∧· · · ∧Ak be a proofdoor decomposition with interpolants I1, I2, · · · Ik−1
satisying the properties of Definition 7. We show that each interpolant Ij can be derived
from Ij−1 ∧Aj by a resolution derivation of size O(n23w+3s · c), and that combining these

Fix an index j ∈{1, . . . , k −1}. Consider an arbitrary clause C = (ℓ1 ∨ℓ2 ∨· · · ∨ℓt)
appearing in the interpolant Ij. By the definition of interpolant, we have (Ij−1 ∧Aj) |= C.
By our assumption we also have (S(C) ∧Aj) |= C. Equivalently, if we let α be the partial
assignment that falsifies all literals of C, then the restricted formula (S(C) ∧Aj) ↾α is
unsatisfiable.
Observe that Aj ↾α has pathwidth at most w with respect to the same path decomposition
under restriction. Moreover, we need only s clauses from the previous interpolant to derive C.
So, the restriction S(C)↾α contributes at most s clauses. Now, the formula (S(C) ∧Aj)↾α
is also a bounded pathwidth formula, as one can create a new path decomposition of
(S(C) ∧Aj) ↾α by creating new bags for all the variables not in Aj but in S(C) after the
path decomposition of Aj. And we add all the clauses in S(C) to all the bags, but as the
number of clauses is just s this will increase the pathwidth by maximum s.
Reversing the restriction α yields a resolution derivation of the clause C from Ij−1 ∧Aj of
the same size. Since (S(C) ∧Aj)↾α has pathwidth at most w, it has a resolution refutation

▶Theorem 15 (CDCL-Proofdoor Theorem). Suppose F admits small proofdoors with
parameters (c, w, s) via the partition F = A1 ∧· · · ∧Ak and interpolants (I1, . . . , Ik−1) as
in Theorem 13. Then there exists a CDCL run (with non-deterministic value selection,

clause database and refutes F with at most poly(n) conflicts.
Proof. We now argue that CDCL can realize the above derivations. Fix a chunk index j
and let C ∈Ij. Since (S(C) ∧Aj) |= C, the partial assignment α := ¬C (which falsifies all
literals of C) makes the restricted formula (S(C) ∧Aj)↾α unsatisfiable.
We guide the CDCL solver to branch according to α, following the an order derived from
the path decomposition of Aj. If at some point BCP forces a literal satisfying C, Then α
cannot be completed. In this case, C is already absorbed by the current clause database,
and we treat C as derived.
Otherwise, the solver completes the assignment α and continues branching on the remain-
ing variables. Since (S(C) ∧Aj)↾α is unsatisfiable, CDCL must eventually reach a conflict.
We now bound the number of restarts/conflicts needed while keeping α fixed.
By assumption, (S(C) ∧Aj)↾α has bounded pathwidth (at most w) with respect to the
order induced by the path decomposition. By Lemma 12 (CDCL simulates CacheSAT ), A
CDCL run with restarts and a fixed decision order encounters at most poly(n) conflicts.
Under DECISION learning, each conflict produces a learned clause that blocks the current
branch; since we always decide α first after each restart, every such learned clause blocks (at
least) the assignment α (or a subassignment of it). Hence, after at most poly(n) restarts,
the solver can no longer complete α: either BCP forces a literal satisfying C, or the learned
database yields an immediate conflict under α. In either case, C is absorbed (or a clause
subsuming C is learned), and we treat C as derived.
Restarting after each such conflict and repeating this procedure for all clauses in Ij derives
the entire interpolant Ij from Ij−1 ∧Aj within the same asymptotic bound. Iterating over
j = 0, 1, . . . , k −1 yields a CDCL refutation of F of size poly(n).

We now consider the commutativity of floating-point addition. We model a floating-point
representation inspired from IEEE 754 [1]. A normalized floating-point number is represented
as x = (−1)S · (1.F) · 2E, where S is the sign, E the unbiased exponent, and F the fraction
bits. We consider positive normalized inputs only, and use round-to-nearest, ties-to-even. We

do not model NaNs, infinities, subnormals, or signed zeros. Given two inputs a = (Ea, Ma)
and b = (Eb, Mb) where Ea, Eb are m bits and Ma, Mb are n bits, we encode a simplified
floating-point addition circuit inspired by the standard pipeline, consisting of the following
stages:
1. Exponent Comparison: Determine which operand has the larger exponent. A comparator
determines whether Ea > Eb. It outputs ordering signals GT and LT and selects the
larger and smaller exponents using a multiplexer Elarge, Esmall.
2. Mantissa Alignment: Right-shift the smaller mantissa by the exponent difference. This
introduces Guard (G), Round (R), and Sticky (S) bits. The exponent difference Diff =
Elarge−Esmall determines the right shift of the smaller mantissa. The outputs at this stage
are the aligned significand M′
small together with guard, round and sticky bits G, R, S.
3. Significand Addition: Perform significand addition in extended precision (includes G, R, S).
The aligned significands are added, producing a sum Σ and carry-out c.
4. Normalization: If the result overflows the leading 1-position, adjust the exponent and
shift the significand accordingly. If c = 1 then the sum is shifted right by one position
and the exponent is incremented. The outputs are the normalized significand Σnorm, the
updated G, R, S bits Gnorm, Rnorm, Snorm, and the updated exponent Eout.
5. Rounding: Use G, R, S bits (round-to-nearest, ties-to-even) to produce the final repres-
entable mantissa. A rounding increment inc is computed from Rnorm, Snorm, and the
least significant bit of Σnorm. The final outputs are Σfinal and Efinal.
The complete CNF encoding has size O(nm + n + m). Detailed gate-level encodings are
given in the appendix E.
▶Theorem 16 (FP Small Proofdoor Theorem). The CNF encoding of the commutativity
of floating-point addition admits small proofdoors. In particular, there exists a proofdoor
decomposition with parameters (c, w, s) = (O(m + n), O(1), O(1)), where m denotes the
number of mantissa bits and n denotes the number of exponent bits.

Partially Ordered Resolution Lower Bounds
As Theorem 13 shows, the resolution proof associated with a proofdoor respects all of its
cutting partial orders. Therefore, partially ordered resolution lower bounds identify proofdoor
decompositions that cannot yield short proofs.
6.1
Lower bounds via interpolation
We now prove the key interpolation lemma used in establishing our lower bounds.
▶Lemma 17 (Interpolants for partially ordered resolution). Let Φ be an unsatisfiable
CNF formula written as Φ = A(Vbefore, Vshared) ∧B(Vafter, Vshared), where Vbefore and Vafter
are disjoint sets of variables. Let ≺be the partial ordering Vbefore ≺Vafter ∪Vshared.
From a ≺-ordered resolution refutation of Φ of size s, we can construct a CNF formula
C of size at most s that is an interpolant from A(Vbefore, Vshared) to B(Vafter, Vshared).
Proof. Let π be a ≺-ordered resolution refutation of Φ of size at most s. Each leaf-to-root
path P in π that resolves on at least one Vbefore-variable contains a “boundary clause” CP
that is produced from the last resolution step on a Vbefore-variable on the path P (see
Figure 2). The remaining clauses on the path P must result from resolving on variables
from Vafter or Vshared. Therefore CP cannot contain variables from Vbefore: if it did contain a
variable v ∈Vbefore, this variable cannot be eliminated in any later resolution steps of the
path P. Yet the path P ends in the empty clause, which is a contradiction. The clause CP
also cannot contain variables from Vafter, since it was derived by resolving only on Vbefore
variables, meaning it was derived only using clauses from A(Vbefore, Vshared).
Consider the set C containing the boundary clauses CP for every leaf-to-root path P in
π. We will show that this set C forms a CNF for an interpolant function C(Vshared) from
A to B. The implication A(Vbefore, Vshared) |= C(Vshared) follows from the soundness of the
resolution rule. The unsatisfiability of C(Vshared) ∧¬B(Vafter, Vshared) follows from observing
that the clauses C ∪B form a cut in the proof DAG of π, and is therefore an unsatisfiable
set of clauses.

Warm-up: Lower bounds for Function Encoding
Janota [17] proved an exponential partially ordered resolution lower bound for type of formula
family he termed Function Encoding; Lemma 17 generalizes this result.
For a Boolean function f : X →{0, 1}, consider the contradiction f(X)∧¬f(X). Encode
f(X) and ¬f(X) as two separate CNF formulas F +(T +, X) and F −(T −, X), where T + and
T −are disjoint sets of fresh auxiliary variables. The encoding is valid if for every assignment
τ to X, exactly one of F +(T +, τ) and F −(T −, τ) is satisfiable, namely F + when f(τ) = 1
and F −when f(τ) = 0.
Janota [17] considered the case where f(X) is the parity function f⊕(x1, . . . , xn) =
x1 ⊕· · · ⊕xn. He showed that for a standard CNF encoding of f⊕(X) that produces the
formula F + ∧F −, any resolution refutation respecting the partial ordering (T + ∪T −) ≺X
is exponentially large in n.
Lemma 17 immediately generalizes this result to any valid encoding of any Boolean
function f that requires a large CNF (e.g. parity or majority). Our lower bound also applies
to a more general partial order (see Corollary 18).

resolution refutation of F +(T +, X)∧F −(T −, X) respecting the partial order (T +) ≺(T −∪X)
has size at least s.
Proof. Observe that f(X) is the unique interpolant from F +(T +, X) to F −(T −, X). The
theorem then follows directly from Lemma 17.

Lower bounds for arithmetic miter circuits
A miter circuit checks equivalence of two Boolean circuits by computing their outputs on
the same inputs and asserting that they differ. Such circuits are standard in equivalence
checking and other formal-verification workflows. We prove lower bounds for a natural class
of arithmetic miters.
▶Definition 20. A tree-like arithmetic circuit is defined as a binary tree graph T whose
internal nodes are labeled by + or ×, and leaf nodes representing input bit-vectors x0, x1, . . ..
Let T(n) denote the Boolean circuit obtained by instantiating each leaf as an n-bit input, and
replacing the + and × nodes of T with sufficiently large adder and multiplier circuits such
that no overflow occurs. Let outT denote the output bit-vector of T(n), corresponding to the
root node of the underlying graph T. Semantically, outT represents an algebraic expression
of the input bit-vectors x0, x1, . . . at the leaf nodes.
Our arguments do not depend on the particular implementation of the adders and
multipliers, only on their functional correctness.
▶Definition 21. An n-bit tree-like arithmetic miter circuit consists of two tree-like arithmetic
circuits T1(n) and T2(n) over the same leaf inputs, together with a Boolean circuit E that
outputs 1 iff outT1 ̸= outT2. In the instances of interest, T1 and T2 compute the same
arithmetic expression, so the overall miter is unsatisfiable.

Undecidability of the Short Proof Family Problem
Finally, we show a fundamental limit to characterizing whether a family of CNF formulas
admits short proofs by establishing undecidability results.
This demonstrates that no
algorithm can fully separate “easy” and “hard” formula families purely at the level of proof
size.
▶Definition 30 (Family of CNF formulas). A family of CNF formulas is a computable sequence
F = (Fi)i∈N of CNF formulas, i.e., there exists a computable G with G(i) = Fi.
▶Definition 31 (Family Proof Size Problem (FPSP)). A family F = {Fi} of unsatisfiable
CNF formulas is a polynomial-proof family (in Resolution) if there exists a polynomial p such
that every Fi has a resolution refutation of size at most p(|Fi|). Otherwise, it is a hard-proof
family.
The Family Proof Size Problem (FPSP) asks: given an algorithm G that generates a family
FG = {G(i)}i∈N of unsatisfiable CNF formulas, determine whether FG is a polynomial-proof
family or a hard-proof family in the Resolution proof system.
▶Theorem 32 (FPSP Undecidability Theorem). The Family Proof Size Problem
(FPSP) is undecidable.
Proof. We reduce the Halting Problem [30] to FPSP. Given a Turing Machine M and input
x, construct for each t ∈N a CNF formula Ft encoding the t-step computation of M on x.

Conclusion
In this paper, we proposed proofdoors as a proof-theoretic framework for understanding
the efficiency of CDCL SAT solvers on structured verification instances. We showed that
small proofdoors guarantee polynomial-size resolution refutations and can be exploited by
suitable CDCL runs to obtain polynomial-time refutations. As a concrete application, we
proved that floating-point addition commutativity formulas admit small proofdoors. Finally,
by relating proofdoors to partially ordered resolution, we identified inherent limitations of
the framework and showed that the choice of decomposition can be the difference between
short and exponential proofs. We believe proofdoors provide a useful new lens on CDCL
performance and a promising direction for further structural study of industrial SAT instances.
A natural direction for future work is to investigate empirically the extent to which proofdoors
are predictive of modern CDCL solver performance.
