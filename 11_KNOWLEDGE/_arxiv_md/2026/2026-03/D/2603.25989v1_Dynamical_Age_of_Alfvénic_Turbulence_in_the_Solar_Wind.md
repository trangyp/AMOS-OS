Abstract
An evolving turbulent flow such as the solar wind can be meaningfully characterized by its
“turbulence age” – an estimate of the number of nonlinear times that have elapsed during a
plasma parcel’s propagation from the Sun to a given point in space. Recent observations of the
near-Sun solar wind by the Parker Solar Probe (PSP) indicate high correlation between velocity
and magnetic fluctuations (i.e., cross helicity, σc), which is known to impede development of
magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) turbulence. Here we propose a new formulation of the turbulence
age (At) of the solar wind that explicitly accounts for the Alfvénic nature of the fluctuations in
the inner heliosphere. At is then evaluated for slow and fast wind streams using a variety of data
sources – observations from the PSP, Advanced Composition Explorer, and Voyager missions, and
a global solar wind simulation that includes turbulence transport. Compared to the formulation
employed in previous work that neglected Alfvénicity, the present approach yields smaller values
of At in medium-to-high σc solar wind; similar turbulence ages are then obtained for slow and
fast wind in the ecliptic. The radial evolution of At between heliocentric distances of r ∼0.2 to
40 AU is examined. The rate of increase of At is found to decrease until ∼5 AU, indicating a
gradual slowing of the in situ development of turbulence in the inner heliosphere. Beyond ∼5
AU this rate begins to increase, likely due to turbulence driving by pick-up ions. This paper
highlights the important role of σc in modulating MHD turbulence, and the results will aid in
further interpretations of observations of the radial evolution of various turbulence parameters
in the solar wind.

Introduction
Large-amplitude fluctuations in the velocity and magnetic fields of the solar wind have been recorded
since the dawn of the space age (Belcher and Davis, 1971). The associated turbulence cascade is
recognized as an important heliospheric phenomenon that can play a fundamental role in heating
and accelerating the plasma (Matthaeus and Velli, 2011) and transporting solar energetic particles
(SEPs) and cosmic rays (Engelbrecht et al., 2022). While many theoretical approaches employed in
the study of heliospheric turbulence can trace their roots to hydrodynamics, the interaction of the
plasma with electromagnetic fields introduces substantial modifications (Zhou et al., 2004). A key
aspect is the Alfvén-wave like nature of the observed turbulence, especially in regard to the cross
helicity (σc, the correlation of velocity and magnetic fluctuations Bruno and Carbone, 2013).

This Alfvénic character is related to the solar source, where convective motions below the photo-
sphere can induce fluctuations in the magnetic “footpoints” of the solar wind, thus launching Alfvén
waves into the atmosphere (De Pontieu et al., 2007). Observations of fast wind (especially at high
heliolatitudes) indicate that the embedded fluctuations have large σc even at several AU from the
Sun, while the slow ecliptic wind generally has low σc at 1 AU (Bruno and Carbone, 2013). Recent
observations by the Parker Solar Probe (PSP) indicate that even the slow wind has large σc near the
Sun (helioradius r ≲0.2 AU; Chen et al., 2020). Turbulence dynamics in this crucial region, where
the wind is heated and accelerated and SEPs injected into the heliosphere (Raouafi et al., 2023),
are therefore likely to be significantly influenced by the large σc. Theoretical work on magnetohy-
drodynamic (MHD) turbulence has shown that large σc can suppress the efficacy of the nonlinear
interactions that produce turbulent cascade (Kraichnan, 1965; Zhou et al., 2004). Indeed, recent
studies have characterized near-Sun turbulence as less developed compared to 1 AU (and beyond),
in terms of standard diagnostics like spectral slope and multifractal statistics (Alberti et al., 2020;
Telloni et al., 2021).
The concept of a turbulence age (At) is of utility in the interpretation of such observations of
evolving turbulent flows. It represents the level of turbulence development in terms of the number of
nonlinear times that have elapsed during the passage of a flow between two points in space and/or
time, thus defining a natural, intrinsic “clock” for the dynamics. Arbitrary time intervals can then
be expressed in units of At, and the relative importance of turbulence compared to other relevant
processes can be evaluated by comparison of characteristic timescales.1
In the context of the solar wind, the turbulence age concept was introduced by Matthaeus et al.
(1998) (henceforth M1998), who proposed and evaluated various formulations that employed both
modeling and observations. They found that the solar wind experiences around 40 “eddy” turnover
times between 1 and 40 AU, with a gradual “slowing” of the turbulence as it ages. The concept
has since then been employed in a number of studies, including a recent analysis of PSP data that
investigated whether the observed variability of the magnetic field’s spectral index was organized
by by At (McIntyre et al., 2023). However, M1998 neglected Alfvénicity in their formulations and
left consideration of non-zero σc for future work. It is the intent of this paper to modify the M1998
formulation to account for cross helicity effects, compare the two formulations, and evaluate the
turbulence age of the solar wind in an expanded domain that includes the young solar wind (r < 1
AU) and polar regions. Observations from the PSP, Advanced Composition Explorer (ACE), and
Voyager missions will be employed, along with data from a global solar wind simulation that includes
turbulence transport.

Results from observations and numerical modeling
3.1
Turbulence age evaluated from ACE at 1 AU
We employ in-situ observations from ACE, situated at L1 and spanning the period from 1998-02-05
00:00:00 to 2023-12-31 23:59:00 (UTC). The magnetic field was obtained from the MAG instrument
(Smith et al., 1998) and plasma data (ion number density and velocity) from the SWEPAM instru-

(Bruno and Carbone, 2013, ∼1 hour at 1 au;), which is a standard approach when computing
turbulence statistics (Matthaeus and Goldstein, 1982).3

Turbulence age evaluated from the Parker Solar Probe between 0.2 and 0.8
AU
We analyze in-situ measurements from PSP’s orbits 1–25, covering the period from 2018 Oct 31
00:00:00 to 2025 Aug 01 00:00:00 UTC. Although PSP’s perihelia extend as low as ∼0.04 AU, the
validity of Taylor’s hypothesis becomes questionable below ∼0.2 AU (Chhiber et al., 2019; Chen
et al., 2020), and we therefore limit our attention to r ∼0.2 – 0.8 AU. Magnetic field measurements
from FIELDS/MAG (Bale et al., 2016) and ion moments from SWEAP/SPC (Kasper et al., 2016;
Case et al., 2020) instruments are employed. The downloaded MAG data have a time cadence of
3.4 ms, and we interpolate its timeseries to match timestamps of the downloaded ion data. The
latter’s cadence varies through the orbits from ∼0.436 s to 27.5 s, and is relatively higher during
solar encounters (r ≲0.3 AU). To avoid interpolation of the magnetic field across large data gaps
we set it to NaN if the nearest valid (SPC) timestamp for interpolation is more than 5 s away. SPC
data are further filtered by setting values to NaN at timestamps when data quality flags (DQF or

proton speed and density, magnetic field, and turbulence quantities (Z2, σc, λ) are obtained for
each interval as described in Sec. 3.1. A minor difference arises in computation of λ: we employ
6-hour intervals instead of the 2-hour ones in order to accommodate a maximum temporal lag of 3
hours, so that the correlation function can be allowed to decorrelate to 1/e, and sufficient samples
are available for averaging at large lags (e.g., Matthaeus and Goldstein, 1982). The three 2-hour
intervals constituting the 6-hour interval are each assigned the same value of λ.
With these values of U, Z2, λ, and σc (at 2-hour cadence) in hand, we bin them in helioradius
r using a bin-width of 0.05 AU. We compute means, σ, and quartiles for each quantity, within
each radial bin. This procedure allows us to infer radial trends in the quantities of interest, in the
sense of a long-term average over ∼7 years. The radial evolution of the mean values (and 1σ)
are rather similar to the results shown in Chhiber et al. (2021) based on the first five PSP orbits,
and are not shown here for brevity. We note here the following overall trends in the radial means
observed between 0.2 and 0.8 AU: U increases from ∼300 to 400 km s−1; Z2 decreases from ∼105
to 2 × 104 km2 s−2; λ increases from ∼2 × 105 to 5 × 105 km; and σc decreases from ∼0.75 to 0.4.
To assess the differences between the turbulence age of slow and fast wind, we separately perform
the above radial binning for the two categories of intervals.8 The most notable distinction (apart
from U) is in Z2, which is several times larger in the fast wind (Shi et al., 2023; Chhiber et al.,
2025). λ and σc are both slightly (but systematically) smaller and larger, respectively, in the fast
wind. These trends are qualitatively consistent with the ones obtained from ACE at 1 AU (Sec.
3.1). We next evaluate Eqs. (4) and (8), keeping r1 fixed at 0.2 AU and increasing r2 through the
mid-points of the radial bins up to 0.8 AU, with the integral evaluated numerically. The results are
shown in the left panel of Fig. 3.
We immediately notice the impact that radial variation of solar wind parameters has on the
integration: compared to Fig. 2, the computed At at 0.8 AU is almost twice as large. We also note
the decrease in At when accounting for σc (indicated as ‘Alfvénic decrease’ in Fig. 3), similar to the
ACE calculations. Finally, we note the apparent radial decrease in the rate of increase of At with r,
indicating the slowing of turbulence aging as the solar wind expands through the inner heliosphere.

3.3
Turbulence age evaluated from a global solar wind simulation between 0.2
and 40 AU
Here we evaluate At within a three-dimensional MHD model of the global solar wind (Usmanov
et al., 2025).
The model couples mean-field equations for bulk velocity, density, magnetic field,
and temperature to transport equations for statistical turbulence parameters, including Z2, λ, and
σc. This coupling is able to effectively model the heating and acceleration of the solar wind by
turbulence (Matthaeus and Velli, 2011), an approach widely employed in global heliospheric modeling
(Gombosi et al., 2018). For further details on our model we refer the reader to Usmanov et al. (2025),
emphasizing here that it is mature, well-tested, and has been validated by comparison with a variety
of heliospheric observations (e.g., Usmanov et al., 2018; Bandyopadhyay et al., 2020; Chhiber et al.,
2021; Usmanov et al., 2025).
For the present study we use a simulation run based on an untilted solar magnetic dipole (de-
scribed in detail in Usmanov et al., 2025), rather than photospheric magnetograms corresponding
to particular time periods (c.f. Chhiber et al., 2021), to obtain a generic view of typical solar wind
conditions (especially during low solar activity). This simulation is symmetric in heliolongitude, and
we evaluate At along two reference radial “spokes” at ∼7◦and ∼77◦heliolatitude, corresponding to
near-equatorial slow wind and polar fast wind, respectively (McComas et al., 2000; Usmanov et al.,
2025). Eqs. (4) and (8) are evaluated as described in Sec. 3.2, with r1 fixed at 0.2 AU and r2
increased until 40 AU. The results are shown in the right panel of Fig. 3.
The low-latitude wind has a turbulence age (At1 and At2) that behaves quite similarly to the
corresponding PSP results in the left panel. The polar fast wind, in contrast, undergoes a rapid
increase in At1 to very large values, reaching above 100 at 1 AU, on account of its high Z2 and small
λ (Usmanov et al., 2025). On the other hand, accounting for its high |σc| with Eq. (8) dramatically
decreases the computed turbulence age, to values that are around half of the corresponding values
for the slow wind. The rate of increase of At with r decreases until around 5 AU, beyond which
there is an apparent increase in the slope of the curve (see also Fig. 4, below). We return to this
final point below, adding a comparison with Voyager 1 observations.

The Voyager result (dashed curves) has been adapted from M1998, who computed At using Eq. (4)
along the Voyager 1 trajectory. Naturally, their integration began at r1 = 1 AU and their results
are therefore agnostic to the accumulated turbulence age from r < 1 AU. Accordingly, for our
visualization we shift their computed At upward by (constant in r) ad-hoc amounts so that the
Voyager result “lines up” at 1 AU with our PSP and global simulation results, thus producing the
two dashed curves in the top panel of Fig. 4.9
The bottom panel of Fig. 4 shows estimates of the rate of change of At with r: changing variables
from time to radial distance (dt = dr/U), we compute dAt/dt as U dAt/dr, evaluating the radial
derivative numerically for the various curves in the top panel. For the Voyager result a constant
U = 400 km s−1 is assumed.
We observe from Fig. 4 that At in the global simulation increases at a slower rate compared with
the overall trend from PSP; while we do not expect a high level of agreement between the generic

in our simulations (see Usmanov et al., 2025).

Discussion and Conclusions
In this paper we have proposed a new formulation for estimating the turbulence age of the solar
wind that accounts for the varying levels of Alfvénicity (specifically, cross helicity) that are routinely
observed in the heliosphere. Our work builds on Matthaeus et al. (1998), who introduced this type
of quantitative determination of turbulence age to space physics studies, but left an accounting of
non-zero σc to future work. We evaluated At based on the two formulations, employing data from

We find that neglect of σc may produce unreasonably large estimates of At for the fast solar wind
(especially in polar regions); the new formulation yields values indicating a similar level of turbulence
development across wind speeds, which is a noteworthy result in itself. In the ecliptic (ACE and
PSP), the fast wind is slightly more aged than the slow wind, while the polar wind (evaluated in the
global simulation) has a smaller turbulence age than equatorial wind. These findings add nuance to
the view that slow wind is generically “more turbulent” than fast wind (e.g., Dasso et al., 2005; Wang
et al., 2024). They may also provide insight on the role of turbulence in heating and accelerating
the fast wind, in view of the observed positive correlations between speed, proton temperature, and
fluctuation amplitude (Démoulin, 2009; Elliott et al., 2012; Shi et al., 2023; Chhiber et al., 2025).
We also examined trends in the evolution of turbulence age as the solar wind expands through the
inner and outer heliospheres. Around 20 nonlinear times may pass between 0.2 and 1 AU, indicating
well developed turbulence, although the rate of development is much larger near the Sun. Around 5
AU, both Voyager and global simulation results show an increase in the rate of aging, likely related
to in-situ driving of turbulence by pickup ions (Fraternale et al., 2022). Our analysis also suggests
that the state of the turbulence at 1 AU is both source and path dependent: slow and fast wind
(originating in different solar sources; e.g., Schwenn, 2006) may have different At, but neglecting the
variation of various parameters during the passage of the solar wind may lead to underestimation
of the turbulence age (compare At2 from ACE and PSP in Sec. 3).
These results may help interpret observations of other turbulence parameters and their complex
radial evolution. E.g., the Reynolds number appears to decrease between ∼0.1 to 10 AU (Cuesta
et al., 2022b; Adhikari et al., 2025), while small-scale intermittency increases in the young solar
wind (r < 1 AU; Alberti et al., 2020; Telloni et al., 2021) before decreasing between ∼1 and 10
AU (Cuesta et al., 2022b). Further signatures of turbulence aging with distance are found in the
steepening inertial-range spectral slope and increase in correlation scale (Chen et al., 2020; Cuesta
et al., 2022a), along with reduction in cross helicity (Roberts et al., 1992). Better understanding of
times scales and aging of turbulence in the outer heliosphere may be achieved by taking into account
the developments reported above. This may be particularly relevant to the recently launched IMAP
mission (McComas et al., 2025) which seeks to understand the influence of 1 AU conditions on the
outer heliospheric boundaries and related energetic particles.
In future work, it may be of interest to revisit interpretations that were based on the definition
of turbulence age that neglected cross helicity (e.g., McIntyre et al., 2023). The turbulence age can
also be evaluated from observations outside the ecliptic from Ulysses and Solar Orbiter (McComas
et al., 2000; Müller et al., 2020), and compared with the type of global simulations we analyzed here.
The turbulence age in the sub-Alfvénic corona observed by PSP would also be of great interest (e.g.,
Bandyopadhyay et al., 2022), although the interpretation may be complicated by issues relating to
Taylor’s frozen-in hypothesis and the sampling direction bias at PSP’s perihelia (Chhiber et al.,
2019; Cuesta et al., 2022a).
