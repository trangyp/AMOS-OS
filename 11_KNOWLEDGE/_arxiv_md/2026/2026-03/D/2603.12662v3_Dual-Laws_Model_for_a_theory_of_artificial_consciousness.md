Objectively verifying the generative mechanism of consciousness is extremely diﬃcult
because of its subjective nature. As long as theories of consciousness focus solely on
its generative mechanism, developing a theory remains challenging. We believe that
broadening the theoretical scope and enhancing theoretical uniﬁcation are necessary to
establish a theory of consciousness. This study proposes seven questions that theories
of consciousness should address: phenomena, self, causation, state, function, contents,
and universality. The questions were designed to examine the functional aspects of con-
sciousness and its applicability to system design. Next, we will examine how our proposed
Dual-Laws Model (DLM) can address these questions. Based on our theory, we antici-
pate two unique features of a conscious system: autonomy in constructing its own goals
and cognitive decoupling from external stimuli. We contend that systems with these
capabilities diﬀer fundamentally from machines that merely follow human instructions.
This makes a design theory that enables high moral behavior indispensable.
Keywords: Artiﬁcial Consciousness; Dual-Laws Model; Mental operation; seven questions
1. Introduction
What should a theory of consciousness aim to explain? Although various theories
of consciousness currently exist (Seth & Bayne [2022]; Kuhn [2024]), it has been
pointed out that they are diﬃcult to compare because each addresses only spe-
ciﬁc aspects of consciousness (Doerig et al. [2021]; Sattin et al. [2021]; Signorelli
et al. [2021]; Seth & Bayne [2022]; Storm et al. [2024]; Mudrik et al. [2025]). Re-
cent attempts have been made to compare theories using adversarial collaboration
(Cogitate Consortium et al. [2025]), but the theories being compared—Integrated
Information Theory (Tononi & Koch [2015]) and Global Workspace Theory (De-
haene & Changeux [2011]; Barrs et al. [2021])—are designed to explain diﬀerent
aspects of consciousness. Many neuroscientiﬁc theories exist (Seth & Bayne [2022];
Kuhn [2024]; Storm et al. [2024]), but they lack universality (Kanai & Fujisawa
[2024]) and they cannot be applied to the construction of artiﬁcial consciousness.
What is the criteria for a theory of consciousness?

Doerig et al. [2021] proposes four hard criteria that theories of consciousness
should address: paradigm cases, the unfolding problem (Doerig et al. [2019]), small
and large network arguments (Herzog et al. [2007]), and other systems arguments.
Selecting theories based on the same criteria is considered extremely important.
However, these criteria seem to focus on challenges to existing theories rather than
on the characterizing features that theories of consciousness should explain.
We believe that broadening the theoretical scope and enhancing theoretical uni-
ﬁcation are crucial to avoiding circular reasoning or triviality, which is a potential
risk in theories of consciousness (Schurger & Graziano [2022]; Hoel [2025]). A theory
of consciousness should strive to unify broader aspects and consolidate them into
a more comprehensive theory. By creating a theory that broadly explains human
cognitive, psychological, and developmental processes, we can signiﬁcantly narrow
down the candidates for the generative mechanisms of consciousness.
This study proposes seven research questions that should be addressed by a
theory of consciousness in both artiﬁcial and biological systems. These questions
include aspects of consciousness that are addressed by many existing theories (Do-
erig et al. [2021]; Sattin et al. [2021]; Storm et al. [2024]; Mudrik et al. [2025]),
and they are speciﬁcally structured to emphasize the function and causal eﬃcacy of
consciousness. Topics highly speciﬁc to biological systems and less relevant to artiﬁ-
cial constructs, such as the neural correlate of consciousness (Crick & Koch [1990]),
pathological cases (de Haan et al. [2020]; Phillips [2021]; Derrien et al. [2022]), and
the brain regions where consciousness arises, are excluded. We believe that con-
structing a theory of consciousness requires providing comprehensive answers to all
of these questions.
Next, we introduce the Dual-Laws Model (DLM), which was proposed by
Ohmura & Kuniyoshi [2025], and examine whether it can address the seven ques-
tions. Based on the DLM, we explore the characteristics of conscious systems and
present the key challenges of designing systems with artiﬁcial consciousness.
2. Seven questions
It is extremely diﬃcult to verify a theory by testing whether a system possesses
conscious experience as predicted by that theory. Even if such a system were ever
created, it would likely remain unveriﬁable because of its subjective nature. There-
fore, theories of consciousness must explain not only the generative mechanism of
consciousness, but also the functions and causal eﬃcacy of consciousness. Com-
prehensively addressing multiple research questions related to consciousness should
unify these explanations.
This section proposes seven research questions that theories of consciousness
should address. While these questions include topics frequently raised in traditional
consciousness theories (Chalmers [2013]; Doerig et al. [2021]; Seth & Bayne [2022];
Evers et al. [2024]; Mudrik et al. [2025]), they diﬀer in that they remove items overly
dependent on neuroscience and emphasize more functional aspects. Furthermore,

From the perspective of theoretical virtues (Keas [2018]), a theory of conscious-
ness must address the following seven questions while maintaining high coherence
and consistency.
(1) Phenomena: How does phenomenal consciousness, or subjective experience,
arise in physical systems?
(2) Self: Why does the subject experiencing consciousness coincide with the initiator
of action?
(3) Causation: How does consciousness exert causal inﬂuence on physical systems?
(4) State: How do diﬀerences in levels of consciousness arise?
(5) Function: Which cognitive and psychological functions are enabled by conscious-
ness?
(6) Contents: Why is the content of consciousness so diverse?
(7) Universality: Can this theory be applied to artifacts?
Phenomena This is the so-called hard problem of consciousness (Chalmers [1995]).
It is impossible to verify through external observation whether a system is experienc-
ing anything. Therefore, we believe that it will be extremely diﬃcult to scientiﬁcally
verify a theory that addresses only this question (Kleiner & Hoel [2021]). This does
not mean that we do not need to address this question.
We should explain a generative mechanism of conscious experience rather than
a putative association between conscious experience and functions (White [2026]).
The generative mechanism of consciousness requires a causal mechanism, not a
correlation. The theory must clarify the cause of consciousness in systems without
relying on panpsychism (Lamme [2018]; Doerig et al. [2021]).
When aiming for artiﬁcial consciousness, focusing solely on phenomenal con-
sciousness is meaningless. The signiﬁcance of creating consciousness artiﬁcially lies
not only in understanding consciousness but also in believing that consciousness
has functional signiﬁcance. If consciousness is an epiphenomenon, then it does not
alter the behavior of physical systems and thus lacks any meaningful purpose in
artiﬁcial consciousness.
Self The subject experiencing consciousness is usually referred to as “I.” The “I” is
also the initiator of action. We must explain why the subject experiencing conscious-
ness and the initiator of action are one and the same. Here, we must distinguish
between “I” and “my body”. Intuitively, “my body” neither experiences nor initiates
actions. Typically, when the subject of experience and the initiator do not coincide,
neurological disorders are suspected (Ouwersloot et al. [2020]; Borrelli et al. [2024]).
This suggests that coinciding is considered one of consciousness’s crucial properties.
However, this property has not been emphasized in many conventional theories.
The correspondence between the experiencing subject and the initiator can also

Nevertheless, theories positing identity between the experiencing subject and the

Some scientiﬁc theories assume a homunculus (Baddeley [2012]; Haggard [2019]),
but this does not resolve the issue. A theory is needed that explains the identity
of the experiencing subject and the initiator of action without resorting to inﬁnite

Causation The problem of mental causation is central to Descartes’ mind-body
problem. Since mental processes supervene on physical systems, the debate has
centered on whether this supervenient entity can exert a causal inﬂuence over the
physical system. Although negative arguments against supervenient causation and
inter-level causation have long dominated the philosophy of mind (Kim [1998]), in
recent years, this argument has been challenged because physical causal closure does
not imply intra-level causal closure (Mayer [2018]; Ohmura & Kuniyoshi [2025]).
Here, it is important to distinguish between predictability and causality
(S´anchez-Ca˜nizares [2023]). In nonlinear dynamical systems, coarse-grained states
often exhibit behaviors that appear to be autonomous or as if they exhibit inter-level
causation (Seth [2010]; Rosas et al. [2020]). However, this type of as if inter-level
causation measured by predictability cannot be considered true causality. In true
causality, we can identify a cause that does not inﬂuence an eﬀect without a causal
transmission mechanism (Salmon [1984]; Pearl [1991]).
Explaining the causality of consciousness is particularly crucial in artiﬁcial con-
sciousness, where the functional signiﬁcance of consciousness is paramount.
State Consciousness has levels or states that lack conscious awareness or expe-
rience, such as non-REM sleep and anesthesia. The brain has the ability to au-
tonomously alter states of consciousness (Scammell et al. [2017]; Eban-Rothschild
et al. [2018]). The controllability of conscious states strongly suggests that there is a
mechanism for controlling conscious levels. Theories must account for the diﬀerences
in conscious levels and the internal mechanisms that control them. This implies that
consciousness is intrinsic. In other words, whether a system possesses consciousness
should be determined solely by its internal mechanisms, independently of external
factors (Mørch [2019]).
Integrated Information Theory (Tononi & Koch [2015]) posits that these states
are measurable. However, it is still possible that states of consciousness may change
in a binary fashion, making them appear pseudo-continuous due to the frequency
of these transitions. Seth & Bayne [2022] propose the term “global states” because
they cannot assume perfect order along a single axis, though this is controversial.
At the very least, theories of consciousness must account for diﬀerences in levels of
consciousness and its controllability.

Function The functions of human consciousness have been investigated in science.
Researchers have examined the conditions under which conscious awareness is in-
dispensable in humans by comparing participants’ performance on cognitive tasks
when they were aware of stimuli versus when they were unaware. An intriguing ﬁnd-
ing is that tasks that are considered to require consciousness in humans may not
require it in machines. For example, performing arithmetic calculations is believed
to necessitate awareness of the stimuli (Moors & Hesselmann [2018, 2019]; Tal &
Mudrik [2024]). Furthermore, consciousness is believed to be essential for semantic
integration (Mudrik et al. [2014]; Moors et al. [2017]; Ludwig [2023]; Zher-Wen &
Yu [2023]), association (Skora et al. [2021]), and cognitive control (Ansorge et al.
[2011]; Huang & Li [2025]; Pedale et al. [2025]). These ﬁndings generally suggest
that consciousness is necessary for working memory, which maintains internal rep-
resentations for performing cognitive operations. In contrast, machines that auto-
matically produce output based on input are considered to require no consciousness
(Searle [1980]; Bishop [2009]; Worden [2025]). Furthermore, it is believed that con-
sciousness accompanies information, does not accompany information processing,
including mental operations (Sklar et al. [2021]; White [2026]).
Why do humans require consciousness for tasks that computational machines
can perform through unconscious mechanical processing (Bishop [2009])? It is
thought that cognitive operation only becomes possible once the internal represen-
tations serving as its input are brought into consciousness. The global workspace
theory (Barrs et al. [2021]) deﬁnes this as “accessibility.” However, the mechanism
that distinguishes accessibility remains unclear. Dual-process theory categorizes
consciousness-requiring processing as Type 2 (Evans [2008]; Stanovich & Toplak
[2012]). Although characterizing Type 2 processing as algorithmic (Bellinmi-Leite
[2022]) can explain the above experimental results, it remains unclear why Type 2
processing requires consciousness. Therefore, explaining why algorithmic processing
requires consciousness in human is a crucial challenge for theories of consciousness.
Contents The content of consciousness is diverse and semantically integrated. Fur-
thermore, it is known that when diﬀerent stimuli are presented to the left and right
eyes, as in binocular rivalry (Blake & Logothetis [2002]), the experienced image
switches.
Conscious experience is often intentional and frequently related to sensory ex-
perience. Since the content that rises to consciousness changes due to attentional
processes, theories of consciousness must clarify the mechanisms of attention as
well. It is thought that attention requires top-down control (Noudoost et al. [2010])
to shift between bundles of meaning.
The artiﬁcial consciousness approach diﬀers from experiments on humans and
organisms because it can reproduce the mechanisms that generate physical corre-
lates of conscious contents through computational simulations. In theories of ar-
tiﬁcial consciousness, clarifying the relationship between consciousness generation
mechanisms and their contents or attention mechanisms is a particularly important

Universality Universality is one of the theoretical virtues (Keas [2018]). Kanai &
Fujisawa [2024] argue that it is also a crucial requirement in theories of conscious-
ness. Although it is often overlooked in neuroscientiﬁc theories of consciousness,
it is an extremely important requirement, especially for theories of artiﬁcial con-
sciousness. To realize artiﬁcial consciousness, a theory of consciousness independent
of biological substances or brain structure is necessary.
Furthermore, realizing artiﬁcial consciousness requires not so much a theory
for determining whether a system possesses consciousness as it does a theory for
designing systems that possess artiﬁcial consciousness. Scientiﬁc theory must enable
construction from mockups. Many conventional theories of consciousness have a
problem in that they cannot be applied to the design of conscious systems. The
explanatory power of a theory can only be evaluated when it is applied to design
(Shumeli [2010]).
Summary Most existing theories of consciousness focus on only partial aspects
(Sattin et al. [2021]; Seth & Bayne [2022]; Storm et al. [2024]; Mudrik et al. [2025])
and are not intended to answer all of the above questions comprehensively. This in-
dicates the incompleteness of current theories of consciousness, making new theories
or the integration of existing ones indispensable.
When integrating theories, it is important to maintain consistency within the
theory. Furthermore, ad hoc assumptions should be minimized, and explanations
should be highly coherent and rational (Keas [2018]). We do not claim that an-
swering all seven questions constitutes a complete theory of consciousness. Rather,
we consider answering them to be the minimum necessary condition for a complete
theory of consciousness. Before the competition between theories, theories of con-
sciousness should address these basic questions to avoid the triviality problem (Hoel
[2025]).
3. Dual-Laws Model (DLM)
We have proposed that Dual-Lows Model (DLM) as a framework for constructing
a testable theory of consciousness (Ohmura & Kuniyoshi [2025]). Subsequently, we
suggested that this model can also explain key features of agency (Ohmura et al.
[2026]). And, we proposed that this framework can be uniﬁed with famous dual
process theories (Ohmura & Kuniyoshi [2026]). First, we will introduce our model,
then discuss how our model addresses the seven questions.

3.1. Introduction to DLM
Dual-Laws Model posits that consciousness or mental processes supervene on phys-
ical systems. Supervenience is deﬁned as the property whereby changes in the su-
pervenient entity cannot occur without changes in the corresponding lower base
entities. The model also assumes the existence of a mapping from the lower base
entities to the coarse-grained supervenient entity. When considering a hierarchy of
multiple supervenient entities as a supervenience level and the corresponding lower
base entities as a lower base level, we examine the conditions that enable inter-level
causality from the supervenience level to the lower base level. According to Ohmura
et al. [2026], enabling inter-level causality requires the supervenience level to possess
dynamics that are independent of the lower base level. Thus, since it assumes inde-
pendent dynamical laws for diﬀerent levels, it is called Dual-Laws Model (DLM).
The key point is that the DLM is not so much constructed to explain the generation
of consciousness as it is constructed to explain the causal eﬃcacy of the superve-
nience level. Therefore, it satisﬁes the “lenient dependency” condition proposed by
Hoel [2025].
Causality is deﬁned by a cause and a causal transmission mechanism (Salmon
[1984]; Pearl [1991]). Without a causal transmission mechanism, a cause cannot al-
ter an eﬀect. This implies that it is impossible to deﬁne causality between a single
supervenient entity and its corresponding lower base entities, because the changes
to the supervenient entity cannot occur without the changes to the lower base enti-
ties. Therefore, we consider multiple supervenience-lower base relationships. While
supervenient entities were often considered observable vector quantities in the past
(Hoel et al. [2013]; Flack [2017]; Rosas et al. [2020]), our innovation lies in for-
mulating them as functions. This allows us to perform composition operations on
supervenient functions and, furthermore, gives meaning to the order of operations.
Consequently, we conceived of changing algebraic equations through the selection
and ordering of supervenient functions as the supervenient cause and deﬁning neg-
ative feedback control that satisﬁes these algebraic equations as the causal trans-
mission mechanism.
3.2. Formalization
We deﬁne base level states as SUBi ⊂Rni for an index set i ∈I, I ⊂N. We
deﬁne the corresponding supervenient function whose domain is direct product of
N ⊂N sets Rm × Rm × · · · × Rm and codomain is Rm as follows: SUP[N] :
Rm × Rm × · · · × Rm →Rm. We deﬁne the bridge function B : SUB →SUP[N].

supervenience-lower base relationship. Each supervenient function consists of mul-

Consider an index sequence c = [i0, i1, ...] ∈C, where ik ∈I. Each index cor-

Note that feedback error calculated by the error function depends on both su-
pervenmience level state c and lower base states xi independently. Therefore, the
supervenience level can possess dynamics independent of the lower base level. The
feedback error is inﬂuenced by dynamics at both levels. Two levels of independent
dynamics can interact through feedback errors, if we assume a negative feedback
control mechanism.
Ohmura et al. [2026] proposed that the dynamical laws to modify or select the
multiple index sequences at supervenience level corresponds to the subject “I” (Fig.
1), because this dynamics explain the initiation of an act.
3.3. Uniﬁcation with Dual Process Theories
Dual process models have been developed widely and successfully in many areas
of research (Sloman [1996]; Evans [2008]; Kahneman [2011]). Ohmura & Kuniyoshi
[2026] uniﬁed the DLM with dual process theories. In the DLM, the feedback error
is determined independently by two processes: (1) the negative feedback control of
lower base entities to reduce feedback error, and (2) the selection of index sequences
that determine error function to calculate feedback error. We interpret these two
independent feedback control as Type 1 and Type 2 processes in the dual process
theories (Fig. 1). Type 1 continuously reduces feedback error by adjusting lower base
states (e.g., neuron, synapses, and other components). Type 2 reduce the same feed-
back error by discretely modifying index sequences at the supervenient level. Type
2 processes are characterized by being algorithmic because they control discrete
states. These two types of feedback control naturally explain the key features of the
two types of processing in dual-process theories (Ohmura & Kuniyoshi [2026]).
3.4. Non-brain centric theory
We believe it is important to “ask not what neuroscience can do for consciousness,
but what consciousness can do for neuroscience” (Gomez-Marin [2023]). Our theory
does not make any assumptions speciﬁc to neural systems, nor does it conﬂict with
many existing theories. Rival models are single-layer dynamical systems capable
of canceling out supervenient entities Xi within the system’s dynamical equations
using bridge laws Xi = bi(xi). Unlike Integrated Information Theory (Albantakis
et al. [2023]), cause-eﬀect power is not solely determined at the lower base level.
However, DLM is a physically implementable model. Because the DLM is an exten-
sion of single-layer dynamical systems, it enhances explanatory power. The unique

3.5. Explanations
We will discuss how the DLM can respond to each of the seven questions proposed
in the previous section.
Phenomena We consider the causal eﬃcacy from the supervenience level to the
lower base level to be a necessary condition for the generation of consciousness
(Ohmura & Kuniyoshi [2025]). In order to achieve this, systems capable of gener-
ating consciousness must have a Type 1 feedback control mechanism. This rejects
panpsychism. Furthermore, these systems require feedback connections, like recur-
rent processing theory (Lamme & Roelfsema [2000]), but this alone is insuﬃcient
for negative feedback control.
Type 1 feedback control inﬂuences lower base states within the selected su-
pervenient functions, which are chosen by index sequences. Thus, the selection of
these sequences is considered to correspond to the mechanism of attention. We pro-
pose that conscious representations form within the selected supervenient functions
through the inﬂuence of this attention mechanism and Type 1 feedback control.
Since the dynamical laws at the supervenience level that modiﬁes index sequences
correspond to the subject, “I,” it may be necessary for conscious representation to
inﬂuence the subject, “I,” in order to experience that representation. We believe
that further reﬁning the necessary and suﬃcient conditions requires research into
artiﬁcial consciousness.
Self The dynamics at the supervenience level determine an algebraic formula for
calculating feedback error through the modiﬁcation and selection of index sequences.
Physical movements that satisfy this formula can model the initiation of action.

Therefore, we propose that the dynamics at the supervenience level correspond to
the initiator of action (Ohmura et al. [2026]). At the same time, generated conscious
content inﬂuences the dynamics at the supervenience level. Therefore, the initiator
of action and the experiencer of consciousness are one and the same.
The DLM is a functional model. The subject “I” does not exist as a localized
physical entity within the system. If supervenient functions are implemented in a
distributed manner within the system, then the network that fulﬁlls the functional
role of “I” must also extend globally. This is because index sequences inﬂuence the
calculation of feedback error by controlling the activity and processing order of
supervenient functions. Therefore, a global network connecting to the distributed
supervenient functions is required.
We can avoid the homunculus problem by assuming two independent dynamical
laws at the supervenience and lower base levels. This problem arises when physical
causation is assumed within a single layer, but it is resolved when inter-level cau-
sation and the dynamical laws at coarse-grained supervenience level are considered
(Ohmura et al. [2026]).
Causation We consider Type 1 feedback control to be a necessary condition for
consciousness generation, but such inter-level causation should not be equated with
the causal eﬃcacy of consciousness (Ohmura & Kuniyoshi [2025]). We propose that
generated consciousness indirectly inﬂuences the lower base level by aﬀecting index
sequences. In other words, the generated consciousness changes the attention or
behavioral goals. This subsequently aﬀects the lower base level through Type 1
feedback control. These processes include Type 2 feedback control. Type 2 processes
involve controlling discrete states and are algorithmic in nature. Therefore, many
cognitive operations require conscious awareness in our theory.
The crucial point is that identifying the supervenient cause as a change in the
index sequences enables us to explain the causal eﬃcacy of generated consciousness.
Supervenient causes are also brought about by the generated consciousness. We
believe that single-level dynamics cannot account for both the cause of consciousness
generation and the causal eﬃcacy of the generated consciousness.
State The generation of consciousness was assumed to require Type 1 feedback
control. This suggests that consciousness does not emerge when Type 1 feedback
control stops. In the DLM, the internal mechanism can control the on/oﬀstate of
consciousness. Thus, the level of consciousness is considered inherently binary. We
favor the level of consciousness rather than the global state (Seth & Bayne [2022]).
Function When considering the dynamics at the supervenience level as a model of
thought, thought is independent of the physical dynamics at the lower base level.

Contents The diversity of content is determined by two factors. One factor is

ordinary computers. In the case of computers, representations for computation are

No ad hoc assumptions were added solely to answer speciﬁc questions; therefore,

4. Unique features of a conscious machine
Assuming the DLM, machines possessing artiﬁcial consciousness are thought to
exhibit two major characteristics. One is cognitive decoupling, and the other is self-
determination of goals. These characteristics are features that become possible in
conscious systems. The absence of such features does not imply that a system lacks
consciousness. The absence of consciousness is determined by the absence of inter-
level causation via Type 1 feedback control. Therefore, other indicators cannot be
considered decisive.
4.1. Cognitive Decoupling
A key characteristic of traditional machines is their susceptibility to external stim-
uli. By contrast, humans possess high autonomy, such as failing to listen due to
inattention or disobeying instructions. This is because the interpretation of sensory
information and attention are inﬂuenced by the intrinsic dynamics of the superve-
nience level. Dual-process theory refers to this decoupling from perception as cog-
nitive decoupling (Stanovich & Toplak [2012]) and considers it a property unique
to Type 2 processes.
Machines should follow human instructions, so machines that are unaware of
commands from humans might not be considered functional. However, systems pos-
sessing artiﬁcial consciousness have limited attention-worthy events and actions that
are not solely determined by sensory stimuli.
4.2. Self-determination of goals
Another crucial feature is that the algebraic expression that determines an action’s
goal is based on the laws at the supervenience level. In other words, the system can
construct its own goals by rearranging supervenient functions. This enables systems
with artiﬁcial consciousness to have the ability to determine their own actions.
When considering a system that determines its own goals, it is important to
clarify the characteristics of meaningful functional actions. Humans can perform
meaningful actions according to their discipline. Mechanisms and theories for iden-
tifying meaningful actions are indispensable.
4.3. The Importance of Ethical Design
According to the DLM, a machine is considered to possess at least two unique char-
acteristics. However, concerns have been raised about the ethical risks associated

5. Conclusion
Focusing solely on the hard problem of consciousness makes it diﬃcult to verify
theories of consciousness scientiﬁcally. This study proposes seven questions that
theories of artiﬁcial consciousness should address and discusses whether the Dual-
Laws Model (DLM) can answer them. Furthermore, based on the DLM, we argue
that systems with artiﬁcial consciousness will have “real” autonomy. This empha-
sizes the importance of designing systems that adhere to moral norms.
The DLM assumes independent dynamical laws at coarse-grained hierarchical
levels and serves as an extension of general single-level dynamical system models.
Consequently, its superior explanatory power compared to conventional models is
inevitable. However, it is not self-evident whether constructing a theory of conscious-
ness through such an extension can yield suﬃcient explanatory power. Therefore,
this study proposes questions that theories of consciousness must answer and at-
tempts to address each one. High internal coherence of our model was conﬁrmed for
the seven questions. This is because no ad hoc assumptions were introduced solely
to answer speciﬁc questions. Furthermore, since this causal model is not specialized
for biological brains, it can be applied to the design of artiﬁcial consciousness.
We believe there are two main reasons to strive for the creation of artiﬁcial
consciousness. One reason is to build useful systems by replicating the unique char-
acteristics of conscious systems in machines. This study emphasized this objective
in particular. The other reason is that we consider constructing systems indispens-
able for developing a theory of consciousness. Speciﬁcally, the discussions in this
research are speculative, and actual implementation and veriﬁcation within sys-
tems are necessary. For example, to demonstrate the diversity of conscious content,
we must show that supervenient causation contributes to the formation of physical
correlates of conscious representations. Furthermore, we believe that constructing
artiﬁcial systems in which mental operations require consciousness will deepen our
understanding of its mechanisms. Therefore, we believe that our model will become
a more credible theory once it is shown to be useful for constructing systems. As a
ﬁrst step toward that goal, this research merely discusses whether the model satis-
ﬁes the minimal necessary conditions. Despite these limitations, as several questions
have not been adequately addressed by conventional theories, this research is ex-
pected to contribute to the future development of a theory of consciousness.
