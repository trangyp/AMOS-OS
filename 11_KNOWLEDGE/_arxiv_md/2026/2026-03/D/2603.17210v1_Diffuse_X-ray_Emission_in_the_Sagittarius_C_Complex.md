ABSTRACT
The Sagittarius C (Sgr C) complex, located on the western edge of the Central Molecular Zone
(CMZ), hosts a mixture of star-forming and non-thermal activity whose X-ray properties remain poorly
understood. Using deep archival Chandra and XMM-Newton observations, we resolve the diffuse X-ray
emission in Sgr C into two components: an H ii region coincident with the radio peak and a brighter
diffuse feature located to its southwest. Spatially resolved spectroscopy reveals the presence of a soft
(kT ≤1 keV) plasma with metal abundances consistent with the elevated metallicity expected in the
CMZ in both regions, along with a harder (∼8 keV) thermal component within the H,ii region. The
observed diffuse X-ray emission and its association with an expanding [C ii] shell suggest that the hot
gas may originate from a young supernova remnant (SNR) embedded in the H ii region. Under this
interpretation, the inferred shock velocity (∼800 km s−1) and SNR age (≥1.7 kyr) are consistent with
a core-collapse SNR in the Galactic Center. These results reveal Sgr C as a potential host of a SNR
and highlight the complex interplay between massive-star feedback, magnetic fields, and molecular gas
in the CMZ.

1. INTRODUCTION
The inner regions of our Galaxy exhibit prominent
diffuse X-ray emission, arising from a combination of
truly diffuse hot plasma and unresolved point sources.
The Galactic Center diffuse emission has been observed
extensively with Chandra, XMM-Newton, and Suzaku,
revealing a complex mixture of thermal and non-thermal
components associated with supernova activity, stel-
lar winds, and possible past outbursts of Sgr A* (K.
Koyama et al. 1996; M. P. Muno et al. 2004; G. Ponti
et al. 2015). A particularly striking feature of the Galac-
tic Center diffuse emission is the presence of strong 6.4

On larger scales along
the Galactic plane, the Galactic Ridge X-ray Emission

plasma, has been traced by the ∼6.7 keV He-like iron (Fe

characterize the diffuse X-ray emission in the Galactic
Center, it is essential to account for both the reflected
component from molecular clouds and the large-scale
GRXE.
Sagittarius C (hereafter Sgr C) is a massive and active
star-forming region located at the western side of the
Central Molecular Zone (CMZ), and it thus serves as a
useful laboratory for testing theories of star formation
in the relatively extreme environment of the CMZ (M.
Tsuboi et al. 1999; S. Kendrew et al. 2013; J. D. Hen-
shaw et al. 2023). However, in contrast to Sagittarius B2
(Sgr B2), which lies on the opposite side of the CMZ and
has been extensively studied, Sgr C has received rela-
tively little attention until recent years. The 1.28 GHz
MeerKAT Galactic Center Mosaic (I. Heywood et al.
2022a) provides a detailed radio view of Sgr C, reveal-
ing intense emission at the location of the H ii region
and a spectacular non-thermal filament (NTF) extend-
ing toward the north1. In 2023, observations from the
James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) mapped the Sgr C
complex, revealing bright hydrogen recombination line
Br-α emission filling the H ii region (S. Crowe et al.
2025; J. Bally et al. 2025). More recently, D. Riquelme-
V´asquez et al. (2025, submitted) reported an expanding
shell feature surrounding the Sgr C H ii region in the
[C ii] emission map obtained with the GREAT instru-
ment on SOFIA. Those authors argue that their esti-
mated expansion velocity, 23.4 km s−1, cannot be ex-
plained solely by stellar feedback within the H ii region,
and raise the possibility of a recent supernova within
the H ii region. Furthermore, using 214 µm polarimet-
ric observations from SOFIA/HAWC+, R. J. Zhao et al.
(2025) traced the magnetic field structure and found its
orientation to be tangential to the surface of the Sgr C
H ii region.

The first X-ray observations of the Sgr C region were
made with Suzaku by T. G. Tsuru et al. (2009). who
presented images and X-ray spectra of both the cen-
tral diffuse emission and an extended north-south fea-
ture they referred to as the “Chimney”, but which we
will hereafter refer to as the Sgr C “Channel”2. They
estimated the total thermal energy of the Sgr C com-
plex to be 1.4 × 1050 erg, and were the first to propose
the presence of a supernova remnant (SNR) within this
region. However, due to the limited angular resolution
of Suzaku and to the offset of the peak of the diffuse X-
ray emission from the H ii region, an association of the
emitting plasma with the H ii region could not be firmly
established.
More recently, Q. D. Wang (2021) presented a Chan-
dra survey of the central 2◦× 4◦field of the Galaxy, re-
vealing a much clearer morphology of the diffuse X-ray
emission in Sgr C. In addition, D. Chuard et al. (2018)
analyzed Chandra and XMM-Newton data of Sgr C,
mapping the fluorescent Fe Kα emission and present-
ing the XMM-Newton spectrum based on an effective
exposure of ∼130 ks. Their analysis, however, primar-
ily focused on tracing the past high-energy activity of
Sgr A∗rather than of the Sgr C region itself.
Chandra and XMM-Newton have been actively mon-
itoring the Galactic Center since 1999.
The archival
Chandra observations cover the central 4◦× 6◦region
of the Galaxy, providing high-resolution imaging of the
diffuse X-ray emission. Meanwhile, the ongoing XMM-
Newton Heritage Survey (PI: G. Ponti) is mapping a
much wider field of view (350◦< l < 7◦, −1◦< b < 1◦)
across the inner Galactic disk, with the primary goal of
tracing the flow of hot baryons that drive large-scale en-
ergetic phenomena such as the Galactic center chimneys
(G. Ponti et al. 2019, 2021), the eROSITA bubbles (P.
Predehl et al. 2020), and the Fermi bubbles (M. Su et al.
2010). By using both Chandra and XMM-Newton obser-
vations, we obtain the deepest exposure to date for Sgr
C, enabling high-quality, spatially resolved spectroscopy
of this region.
In this work, we make use of archival Chandra and
XMM-Newton observations to re-visit the environment
of the Sgr C H ii region and molecular cloud. Section 2
describes the observations employed in this study and
the details of the data reduction procedures.

2. OBSERVATIONS AND DATA REDUCTION
2.1. Chandra
The Galactic Center has been frequently observed
by Chandra’s Advanced CCD Imaging Spectrometer
(ACIS) since 1999. To map the Sgr C region, we utilized
5 observations taken in ACIS-I VFAINT mode, which
accumulated a total of 197 ks of clean exposure (See
Table 1).
All data were reprocessed using the CIAO data anal-
ysis package, version 4.16, and the latest calibration
database (CALDB 4.11.5) distributed by the Chandra
X-ray Observatory Center. We removed the cosmic rays
and bad pixels from all level-1 event files using the CIAO
tool chandra repro with VFAINT mode background
event filtering. For each observation, we extracted the
lightcurve in the 9–12 keV band to detect possible con-
tamination from flare events. To remove the time inter-
vals with anomalous background, we applied the CIAO
tool deflare to filter out the times where the back-
ground rates exceed ±2σ of the average value.
We utilized stowed background files to estimate
the non-X-ray background (NXB). All stowed back-
ground event files were combined and reprocessed with
acis process events using the latest gain calibration
files. For each observation, we scaled the NXB to match
the 9-12 keV count rate of the observation.
We extracted and combined the 1–4 keV count im-
ages using merge obs.
Background images were gen-
erated from the stowed observation files.
We calcu-
lated the exposure maps using a weighted spectrum
file generated by make instmap weighted, where the
spectral model is an absorbed apec model with kT =
1 keV, which is the average plasma temperature mea-
sured by the previous Suzaku study (T. G. Tsuru et al.
2009). Point sources were identified using the CIAO tool
wavdetect, masked from the images, and the masked re-
gions were refilled using dmfilth. After correcting the
NXB-subtracted count image with the combined expo-
sure map and smoothing it with a Gaussian kernel of 11
× 11 pixels (5.5 ′′x 5.5 ′′), we obtained the Chandra flux
map shown in the left panel of Figure 1.

3. X-RAY IMAGING
3.1. Morphology and properties
As shown in Figure 1, the entire structure exhibits an
inverted L-shaped morphology. Prominent emission is
spatially associated with the Sgr C H ii region; however,
the brightest component is located toward the southwest
of the H ii region, offset by approximately 2.7′. We refer
to this diffuse feature as the bright “Blob,” enclosed
within a roughly circular region of radius 1.2′.
The H ii region appears as a separate X-ray source
largely coincident with the radio H ii region, but the pro-
jected image raises the possibility that it is connected to
the Blob. The H ii region counterpart exhibits a surface
brightness of 7.7×10−13 erg cm−2 s−1 arcmin−2 in 1.5-8
keV, whereas the Blob is approximately ∼70% brighter,
with 1.3×10−12 erg cm−2 s−1 arcmin−2. An X-ray dark
region is observed south of the H,ii region, bounded by
two well-defined edges (yellow dashed lines in Figure 1),
likely caused by strong absorption from foreground cold
dust (see Figure 6 and Section 5.3).
In addition, as shown in Figure 2, diffuse emission con-
stituting the Channel extends northward from the H ii
region, as was first reported by T. G. Tsuru et al. (2009)
(and referred to by them as the “chimney”).
Taking
advantage of the larger field of view covered by XMM-
Newton, we measure this feature to extend to a length
of ∼26 pc, although its surface brightness is significantly
weaker than that of both the H ii region and the bright
Blob. As shown in Figure 3, the prominent radio non-
thermal filament (NTF; see H. S. Liszt 1985; H. S. Liszt
& R. W. Spiker 1995, for details) appears to overlap spa-
tially with the Channel although it exhibits a slightly
different orientation. Without a much higher resolution
and higher signal-to-noise X-ray image, it remains un-
certain whether these two features are physically con-
nected or are projected as a chance alignment.
The
properties of the Channel are further discussed in Sec-
tion 5.4.
3.2. Hardness Ratio
For XMM-Newton, a contour binning algorithm (J. S.
Sanders 2006) was applied to this flux map, to create
a binned image with each bin reaching a signal-to-noise
ratio (S/N) of 10 (see Figure 4).
The hardness ratio (HR) is defined as HR = (FH −
FL)/(FH + FL), where FH and FL refer to the photon
fluxes in the high energy (4-6 keV) and low energy (2-4
keV) bands, respectively.

4. SPECTRAL ANALYSIS
We study the diffuse emission in the Sgr C complex
through a spectroscopic analysis of the XMM-Newton
data. To investigate the difference between the H ii re-
gion and the brighter Blob, we extracted spectra sepa-
rately from these two circular regions. The local back-
ground was estimated by averaging the spectra of three
source-free regions surrounding the Sgr C complex (see
the dotted green circles in Figure 3) and subtracting
that background from the source spectra, with proper
weighting for area.
Spectral modeling
was
carried out
using
XSPEC
(v12.14, K. A. Arnaud 1996). The spectra were binned
to have at least one count per channel.
Initially, we
attempted to model the spectra as an absorbed ther-
mal plasma in collisional ionization equilibrium using
the tbabs*vapec model.
However, as shown in top-
left panel of Figure 5, a significant residual in the Fe
Kα band (6–8 keV) emerged in the spectral fitting
result for the H II region.
We also tested the non-
equilibrium (vvnei) and recombining plasma models
(vvrnei) for the cool component, but saw no improve-
ment in the fitting statistics. By adding an additional
higher-temperature apec component, the fitting statis-
tics were improved, and more importantly, the residuals
in the Fe Kα band become more evenly distributed.
When modelling the spectrum of the Blob with a sim-
ple absorbed thermal model, tbabs*vapec, we noticed
an excess of 6.4 keV Fe Kα line emission as shown in top-
right panel of Figure 5, which is highly likely to be con-
tributed by reflection emission, as reported in D. Chuard
et al. (2018) (See Appendix A for more details).
We
therefore added an additional reflection model, crefl16
(uniform Cloud REFLection model version of 2016; E.
Churazov et al. 2017), to the absorbed thermal plasma
model. The model calculates the reflected spectrum of
a uniform spherical cloud illuminated by a power-law
spectrum with a photon index Γ, extending up to 1 MeV.
For spectral fitting, we set free the column density,
plasma temperatures, abundances and the normaliza-
tions for each component.
We fit jointly the EMOS
and EPN spectra, minimizing the C-statistic (W. Cash
1979). The protosolar abundance table from K. Lodders
et al. (2009) has been applied for the abundances. We
show the spectra of both the H ii region and the Blob
in Figure 5, along with the best-fit models and text la-
bels for prominent emission lines. Table 2 presents the
best-fit parameters and their corresponding 1-σ uncer-
tainties. Both features – the H II region and the Blob –
exhibit a cool plasma component with kT ≲1 keV and
similar abundances, suggesting a common origin. As-
suming a common origin for both features, we jointly
fitted the spectra of the H II region and the Blob, ty-
ing the temperature (kTc) and metallicity of their cool
plasma components. As presented in the right part of
Table 2, the joint fit yields an acceptable statistical re-
sult with a consistent thermal plasma model character-
ized by kT ∼0.84 ± 0.03 keV and Z ∼1–2.5 Z⊙.
To further investigate the spatial variation of abun-
dance in the H II region, we attempted to divide the
circular region into four sectors (NE, NW, SE, SW).
However, within the limited statistical uncertainties, no
significant abundance variations are observed between
the various sectors.
5. DISCUSSION
It is not a rare case that we find an X-ray counterpart
to the H ii region. Observations with Chandra, XMM-
Newton, and Suzaku have revealed diffuse X-ray emis-
sion associated with massive H ii regions such as the
Carina Nebula (e.g., K. Hamaguchi et al. 2007; L. K.
Townsley et al. 2011) and 30 Doradus (e.g., L. K. Towns-
ley et al. 2006). Stellar winds from O- and B-type stars,
as well as colliding-wind binaries, can drive shocks that
heat gas to X-ray emitting temperatures (106–107 K),
while expanding wind-blown bubbles and superbubbles
often exhibit diffuse, soft X-ray emission (e.g., L. K.
Townsley et al. 2003; Y.-H. Chu 2008).
5.1. A SNR?
As demonstrated in D. Riquelme-V´asquez et al. (2025,
submitted), the velocity-integrated [C ii] map reveals an
expanding shell surrounding the H ii region. The mas-
sive shell expands at an unexpectedly high velocity of
∼23 km s−1, exceeding what stellar feedback alone can
plausibly explain and suggesting alternative drivers such
as a SNR.
A supernova could be a candidate for producing the
X-rays within the H ii region, and given the presence of
massive stars there (F. Nogueras-Lara 2024), the most
likely type of supernova to occur there would be a core-
collapse supernova.
However, if a supernova had oc-
curred within the H ii region, we could expect it to have
enhanced the metallicity, at least of the X-ray emitting
plasma.

It is noteworthy that we find evidence for a hard ther-

contribution decreases with increasing distance from the
plane. The ∼8 keV excess persists even after adopting
the revised local background, indicating that its origin
is unlikely to be associated with the GRXE.
As discussed in Section 5.1, the hot component could
be associated with the SNR. In the following, we discuss
two other possible origins. This high-energy component
might come from strong shocks where the strong winds
of the WCL Wolf-Rayet stars (WRs) impact the dense
shell surrounding the H ii region. A K-band spectro-
scopic survey of over 500 highly reddened point sources
in the CMZ carried out by T. R. Geballe et al. (2019)
revealed two WRs (17443734-2927557 and 17444083-
2926550) in or near the H ii region of the Sgr C complex.
By fitting the broadening of the He i emission lines, we
obtained a rough estimate of the wind speed for both
stars on the order of ∼2000 km s−1 (see the spectra
presented in Figure B1 of Appendix B ). Assuming that
the bulk kinetic energy of the upstream flow is fully con-
verted into thermal energy of the post-shock gas, we
estimate the gas temperature using Equation 1. For a
pre-shock velocity of 2000 km s−1, this yields a post-
shock temperature of kT ≃4.7 keV, representing an
upper limit on the X-ray temperature attainable in this
scenario. This mechanism therefore seems to fall short
of being a viable source of the 8 keV component of the
spectrum.
Alternatively, this hot component could arise from col-
liding winds of massive stars in the H ii region. Sim-
ilarly to W49A, an ultracompact H ii region contain-
ing a dozen nascent early-type stars, Chandra has de-
tected hard X-ray plasma ∼7 keV (M. Tsujimoto et al.
2006). The authors interpreted the observed hard X-ray
emission from W49A in terms of an ensemble of point
sources – O-type stars – of shocked interacting winds,
and a wind-blown bubble interacting with ambient cold
matter. A similar scenario could be applicable to the
Sgr C H ii region, if such stars are found to be present.
A recent investigation by F. Nogueras-Lara (2024, and
personal communication) shows that the immediate en-
vironment of the Sgr C H ii region hosts ∼105 M⊙of
young stars with minimum ages of ∼20 Myr. The winds
from the most massive of these stars could interact with
the strong stellar winds of the WR stars and produce
the hot X-ray emission.
5.3. The offset X-ray “Blob”
In X-ray morphology, the bright Blob appears physi-
cally connected to the nearby H ii region. The compara-
ble column densities and the presence of a similarly cool
thermal component in both regions suggest that they
may share a common origin. The consistently high fore-
ground column densities also exclude the possibility that
either component arises from a foreground source. The
relatively low hardness ratio of the Blob further hints
that it could represent material outflowing from the H ii
region. However, this interpretation is challenged by the
fact that the Blob is significantly brighter in X-rays than
the H ii region itself, which is difficult to reconcile with
a simple outflow scenario.
To further investigate why the Blob exhibits a higher
surface brightness, we examine near- and far-infrared
images that trace both warm and cold dust.
The
Spitzer/IRAC 8 µm map (S. Stolovy et al. 2006; R. G.
Arendt et al. 2008) shows warm dust concentrated
around the H ii region, with much fainter emission to-
ward the Blob (Figure 6). Similarly, the JCMT/SCUBA
450 µm image (D. Pierce-Price et al. 2000) reveals strong
far-infrared emission from cold dust toward the H ii re-
gion, whereas a clear deficit of cold dust is seen along
the line of sight to the Blob. For a more detailed dis-
cussion of the infrared morphology of Sgr C—including
prominent features visible in Figure 6, such as the
G359.44–0.10 EGO—see R. J. Zhao et al. (2025). The
dust distribution provides a natural explanation for why
the Blob appears brighter than the H ii region: fore-
ground dust absorption suppresses the X-ray emission
from the H ii region, and reshapes the overall X-ray
morphology of the Sgr C complex. We also note that
the boundaries of the X-ray–dark regions closely follow
the dense cold-dust structures traced by SCUBA (see
yellow lines in both Figure 1 and Figure 6), suggest-
ing that intrinsic X-ray emission may be emitted by the
southeastern part of the Sgr C H ii region and its imme-
diate surroundings, but is obscured by intervening dust.
An alternative possibility is that the Blob is a sepa-
rate source that is merely projected adjacent to the H ii
region along the line of sight. The Blob is infrared-dark,
as reported by R. J. Zhao et al. (2025), who reported
a strong spatial anti-correlation between this southwest
X-ray peak and far-IR emission. It is also radio-dark, as
indicated by the deep red region shown in the left panel
of Figure 3. Since no convincing counterpart has been
detected at any other wavelength, the physical origin of
the X-ray emission from the Blob remains unclear, but
the hypothesis that the emitting plasma in the Blob has
been extruded from the H ii region remains as a possi-
bility.

5.4. The extended Channel
Previously discovered and named as the “Chimney”
by T. G. Tsuru et al. (2009), this diffuse feature actu-
ally extends further in latitude than they had reported,
being limited by the Suzaku field of view. T. G. Tsuru
et al. (2009) suggested that the Channel is physically
connected with the unresolved main structure comprised
of both the H ii region and the Blob, based on their mea-
sured spectral properties.
With our XMM-Newton image as shown in Figure
2, we find that the Channel extends 26 pc north-
ward from the H ii region, and has a maximum width
of 12 pc.
The feature appears very soft, exhibiting
an observed surface brightness of SB1.5–4 keV = 3.0 ×
10−14 erg s−1 cm−2 arcmin−2, which is only 15% higher
than the local background. However, at higher energies
(> 4 keV), we detect no significant flux excess in the
Channel. The limited photon statistics of the Channel
prevent a reliable determination of the Channel’s spec-
tral properties from the XMM-Newton data.
The fact that the Channel is within a few degrees
of being perpendicular to the Galactic plane raises the
possibility that the X-ray-emitting plasma in the Chan-
nel is being collimated by the relatively strong vertical
magnetic field present in the Central Molecular Zone,
evidenced by the forest of predominantly vertical fila-
ments of nonthermal radio emission, or NTFs (F. Yusef-
Zadeh et al. 2004; M. R. Morris 2006; I. Heywood et al.
2022b). These NTFs delineate and illuminate the mag-
netic field in the intercloud medium where relativistic
electrons are produced (M. R. Morris 2015). It is there-
fore noteworthy that one of the most prominent vertical
NTFs is superimposed upon the Channel (compare Fig-
ures 2 and 3), and has an orientation that differs by only
about 10 degrees from that of the Channel. That Sgr C
NTF is therefore a candidate for being a synchrotron-
illuminated part of the magnetic field that confines the
Channel. The neighboring and much larger-scale X-ray
Chimney has also been considered to be confined by the
vertical magnetic field (G. Ponti et al. 2019; M. Zhang
et al. 2021).
6. CONCLUSION
In this work, we have analyzed the available XMM-
Newton and Chandra observations of the Sgr C complex.
Compared to previous Suzaku studies, our analysis re-
veals the detailed X-ray morphology of Sgr C with im-
proved spatial resolution, showing that it consists of two
thermally emitting regions: the H ii region and a bright
Blob. We find that:
• The H II region shows an abundance pattern
broadly consistent with the elevated metallicity
expected in the CMZ. When considered alongside
the expanding [C II] shell observed in the far-
infrared and possible mixing between the ambient
gas and supernova ejecta, the evidence is consis-
tent with a SNR origin for the X-ray emission.
• We measure a ∼0.8 keV post-shock plasma with
an electron density of 1.7 ± 0.1 cm−3 for the H
II region. Under the SNR interpretation, assum-
ing the remnant is in the Sedov–Taylor phase, we
estimate an age of ∼1.7 kyr for the SNR. These
values are broadly consistent with a core-collapse
supernova origin in the Galactic Center.
• The bright Blob exhibits higher surface brightness
than the H II region.
Although this is difficult
to reconcile with a scenario in which the emitting
plasma in the Blob is outflowing from the HII re-
gion, a joint fit of the spectra of the two sources in-
dicates very similar temperatures and foreground
column densities, so a common origin for these
sources remains plausible.
• Compared to previous studies, our XMM-Newton
analysis provides a more complete view of the
elongated vertical Channel, which extends ∼26 pc
northward from the H ii region and reaches a max-
imum width of ∼12 pc.

APPENDIX
A. REFLECTION EMISSION
To investigate the reflection component in our regions
of interest, we examine the 6.32–6.48 keV flux map over-
laid with the Fe Kα–emitting clumps identified by D.
Chuard et al. (2018), namely Sgr C1, Sgr C2, and Sgr
C4. Because the reflection component is time-variable,
we have slightly adjusted the centers and sizes of these
clumps to more accurately match our XMM mosaic com-
bined from observations spanning 2000 to 2020. A sig-
nificant portion of the bright Blob overlaps with Sgr C4,
whereas the H ii region shows only slight spatial coin-
cidence with Sgr C1. This spatial configuration helps
explain why a residual reflection component is present
in the spectrum of the Blob but not in that of the H ii
region.
To check this further, we extracted the spectrum
from the Blob region excluding the area overlapping
with Sgr C4. The spectrum was fitted with the model
tbabs*(vapec+crefl16).
As shown in Figure A2, the
best-fit normalization of the reflection component is
(1.38 ± 0.50) × 10−3, which is not statistically signifi-
cant at the 3σ level. This result, plus the known spatial
and temporal variability of the reflection emission, sup-
ports the conclusion that the 6.4 keV Fe Kα emission
originates from reflection by the molecular cloud.

B. WIND SPEED OF THE WR STARS
In Figure B1, we present the K-band spectra of the two
WR stars (17443734–2927557 and 17444083–2926550)
located within or at the edge of the H ii region in Sgr C.
To obtain an approximate estimate of their wind veloc-
ities, we fitted the spectra with a power-law continuum
and two Gaussian components to model the He I emis-
sion line.
From the best-fit FWHM of the Gaussian
profiles, we derive wind velocities on the order of ∼2000
km s−1 for both stars.
