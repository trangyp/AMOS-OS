Abstract
We explore a nuance to ’no arbitrage’ in relation to ’information efficiency’: acting
immediately on an arbitrage is sometimes suboptimal; in such cases optimised trading
can suppress the anticipation of predictable risk-outcomes, thereby creating an apparent
Status Quo Bias, with Momentum and Low-Risk effects. This is shown in continuous time
under model- or event-risk, where, unlike existing approaches, we allow pre-horizon risk-
resolution and Risk-Neutral Equivalent pricing, with the technical challenges overcome
through results from the ’weak viability’ and ’side/inside information’ literature. Thus
the tension between ’no arbitrage’, ’information efficiency’ and ’outcome anticipation’ is
treated in a practically relevant setting.

Introduction
We examine a nuance to ’no arbitrage’ and its impact on ’information efficiency’, which
commonly refers to how well market prices ’bake in’ what is known. Of the various notions
of ’no arbitrage’, the benchmark, and the basis for FTAP (Fundamental Theorem of Asset
Pricing), is NFLVR (’No Free Lunch with Vanishing Risk’). For concreteness, we confine
ourselves to continuous-time asset-pricing under a model- or event-risk 𝐵with outcome 𝑏or
𝑏becoming public at a finite time (e.g. war or no? recession or no? CEO X or Y? change or
status quo?). In our context, a ’model’ is a probability law from a set of alternatives, and ’risk’,
the objective or subjective randomness of an outcome. Risks with unknown (ambiguous) laws
shall be referred to as ’uncertainty’, after Knight (1921) and Bewley (2002).
An Apparent Viability-Efficiency Dichotomy
Call a market 𝐵-unsure if it is NFLVR under public information, and {𝐵= 𝑏}-sure if it is NFLVR
under {𝐵= 𝑏}-enlarged public information. Consider the viability of 1) 𝐵-unsure markets to
𝑏-insiders, who are {𝐵= 𝑏}-sure; 2) {𝐵= 𝑏}-sure markets to 𝑏-outsiders, who are 𝐵-unsure.
In case-1, the 𝐵-unsure markets offer 𝑏-insiders only unscalable arbitrage without sure pre-
disclosure payoffs (Pikovsky and Karatzas (1996), Ankirchner and Imkeller (2005), Chau et al.
(2018)): there is NFLVR-viability pre-disclosure and NUPBR-viability1 overall. In case-2, any
{𝐵= 𝑏}-sure market, to 𝑏-outsiders, has a non-equivalent absolutely continuous martingale
measure (nACMM), a market-type proposed by Delbaen and Schachermayer (1995) while
acknowledging its ’obvious unviability’: all {𝐵≠𝑏}-contingent rights, worthless in this market,
face infinite demand from 𝑏-outsiders.
Hidden above is a seeming dichotomy: viability favours 𝑏-outsiders, who seek cheap {𝐵≠𝑏}-
rights, unlike 𝑏-insiders, who must issue {𝐵≠𝑏}-obligations and manage {𝐵≠𝑏}-liabilities
(margin-calls) till 𝐵-resolution; so markets, having to be viable against any sceptics of any
fact or certainty, would fail information efficiency in the real world; or would they?
1NUPBR (’No Unbounded Profit with Bounded Risk’) is the minimum for utility-maximisation problems to
be well-posed (Karatzas and Kardaras (2007), Fontana (2011a), Fontana (2011b), Acciaio et al. (2016)).

Asset-Pricing under Model- or Event-Risk
Making do with a ’working model’ refined over time is routine in practice.
Attempts to
embed it into the Rational Expectation framework include theories of asset-pricing under
Parameter Learning (e.g. Guidolin and Timmermann (2007), Collin-Dufresne et al. (2016)),
Robust Control (e.g. Hansen et al. (2006), Hansen and Sargent (2011)), and Ambiguity
(e.g. Epstein and Wang (1995), Chen and Epstein (2002), Ju and Miao (2012), Machina
and Siniscalchi (2014)).
While fruitful in many ways, the impact of these on mainstream theory and practice has
been limited, arguably, due to complexity and a lack of connection to day-to-day asset-pricing
tasks: they ’shun’ risks that resolve in finite times and any form of Risk-Neutral Equivalent
(RNE) asset-pricing2, so as to avoid certain technical complications, sidestepping some highly
relevant theoretical and practical questions as a result.
Overview of Our Findings
To the standard setup we apply RNE pricing and add risk-specific data that enable pre-horizon
risk-resolution. The resulting price dynamics (Section 2) show that it is sometimes suboptimal
to position immediately for a perceived mispricing or arbitrage (Section 3). As such, optimal
trading can inhibit the anticipation of predictable outcomes in market pricing; this effect is
reinforced under Ambiguity, via Bewley’s Inertia Axiom (Bewley (2002)). The Status Quo
Bias (Samuelson and Zeckhauser (1988), Kahneman et al. (1991)) thus created is analysed
(Section 4) and shown to be capable of inducing the disparate phenomena of Momentum
(Jegadeesh and Titman (1993)) and Low-Risk (Ang et al. (2006)). Our findings remove the
’viability-efficiency dichotomy’, but at the expense of ’outcome anticipation’.

(2012)), unified under Smooth Ambiguity, have no such issues (Proposition 3.6 of Burzoni et al. (2021)).

Basic Background Assumptions
We work in a frictionless, continuous and complete-market setting. The core stochastic basis
is Wiener, based on the classical Wiener space 𝐶0[0,∞), either restricted to a closed interval,
essentially 𝐶0[0,1], or identified with that of an half-open interval, essentially 𝐶0[0,1); the
latter provides an explicit representation of finite-time risk-resolution.
Asset-pricing under the binary 𝐵-risk is to be rational with respect to the reference beliefs:
two 𝐵-sure laws of data and one law of the risk 𝐵. The resulting inferential stochastic basis
is the natural basis for asset-pricing. Let the reference 𝐵-sure laws be correct by default, or
else, equivalent to the correct ones. The law of 𝐵-risk may be unknown (ambiguous) and so
the inferential beliefs about 𝐵, evolving with data, can be entirely subjective.
Technical Issues in Learning New Facts in Finite Time
In standard asset-pricing, data comprise only the direct inputs to ’worth’, firmed-up benefits3.
Such a setup is irreducible: future benefits are inferred from past ones. The model- or event-
risk therein cannot resolve in finite time, and the resulting market cannot eliminate a priori
ignorance or fallacies. Learning in this setup is backward-looking and never-ending.
The above is diametric to the real world: many types of data (’indicators’) are relevant,
even predictive (’forward-looking’), and risks do resolve, often at pre-announced dates, before
horizon/maturity; knowing ’truth will out’ matters, given the viability of essentially arbitrary
pricing otherwise (Remark 4). Adding these features however faces technical challenges.
Learning seems to make the pricing of resolvable risk intractable
Risks resolvable pre-horizon are subject to arbitrage pricing, not necessarily economic pricing
(assignment of risk-premia by recursive optimisation based on inference and preference). Yet,
without the latter it is hard to specify the price dynamic enough to apply arbitrage pricing.
Moreover, learning makes RNE probabilities path-dependent and any RNE measure, even in
basic cases, intractable (e.g. Guidolin and Timmermann (2007)).
3Classically dividend/interest, ’benefits’ can be anything deemed ’of value’, widening the notion of ’worth’.

We show that these issues diminish in continuous time or in a compound, non-irreducible,
data structure. Explicit price dynamics emerge, and under common market conditions they
have an intuitive form and a price-of-risk with familiar properties. For hedge pricing, the RNE
measure is identifiable outright, or up to an easily absorbable drift.
FTAP-viability can be impossible for processes that resolve risks
Under model- or event-risks, the inferential basis of asset-pricing can differ from the physical
(realised) basis. This is inevitable for 𝐵-risks due to facts, hypotheses, parameters or rare-
events, whose randomness is largely subjective so that all outcomes but one are unphysical
or rarely realisable. Moreover, prices FTAP-viable in the inferential basis cannot be so in the
physical, true-outcome, basis, due to the divergence of ’signal-to-noise’ into risk-resolution.
Questions of ’actual’ bias and viability then arise, as does the joint-hypothesis problem (”is
an apparent market anomaly due to market bias or flawed theories of market-price-of-risk?”;
see Fama (2014)). These issues are moot or ambiguous in the absence of pre-horizon risk-
resolution; in our approach they must be confronted.
We show that the more robust and general NUPBR-viability naturally arises in our setting.
Moreover, under heterogeneous beliefs about model- or event-risk, a market with nACMM is
found to emerge and persist, despite the arbitrage it offers to inferential traders, thanks to
such arbitrage being suboptimal to act upon until imminent risk-resolution.

5
Summary and Discussion
Asset-pricing under model- or event-risk and its equation of motion are derived in a continuous
inferential stochastic basis that is standard but for the presence of risk-specific data enabling
pre-horizon risk-resolution (Proposition and Corollary 1). Under natural economic constraints,
such prices have a canonical form (Corollary and Proposition 2, Remark 8), with familiar and
intuitive price-of-risk properties (Corollary 3, Remark 6-7).
Our risk-neutral equivalent approach ensures FTAP-viability, aka. NFLVR, which is known
to be identical to being simultaneously NUPBR and NCA (’No Classical Arbitrage’). We find
that our NFLVR price processes in the inferential basis are only NUPBR in the physical, true-
outcome, basis (Appendix B.2), agreeing with known results in the literature. It effectively
says that agents with foreknowledge have only CA trades that have neither pre-resolution
sure-payoff nor scalability (due to the necessity for interim credits).
It also says ’anything goes’ pre-resolution. For instance, under a model-change risk that
is predictable, it is suboptimal to do pro-’change’ trades without sufficient pro-’change’ data
intensity (Proposition 2). Indeed, asset-prices that completely dismiss ’change’-risk act as a
stable attractor, capable of delaying any ’change’-anticipation to ’the last minute’. A non-
anticipative market, which jumps upon ’change’-confirmation, has nACMM in the inferential
basis and offers arbitrage to those fearing ’change’. Despite this, it persists, as shown.
Were this not so, no market could be viable against any doubt or fear from any trader. The
above thus removes the ’viability-efficiency dichotomy’; for any risk whose law is unknown,
Bewley’s Inertia Axiom under Ambiguity reinforces the lack of dichotomy: as such, ’viability’
and ’efficiency’ can be said to be more commonly mutually enhancing.
21


## Page 22


As seen however, outcome anticipation, a key goal of any market, is suppressed as a result.
Our findings suggest that less continuity or less predictability incentivises the building of
anticipatory positions. Yet, if pushed too far in this direction, a ’viability-efficiency dichotomy’
develops. Finding the right balance is then of great theoretical and practical interest.
