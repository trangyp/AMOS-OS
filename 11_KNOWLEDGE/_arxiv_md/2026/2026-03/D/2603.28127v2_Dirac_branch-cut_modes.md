Bound states arising in Dirac fields are usually attributed to two kinds of features: domain walls
where a real Dirac mass field changes sign, which host Jackiw–Rebbi states, and phase singularities
in a complex Dirac mass field, which host Jackiw–Rossi zero modes. We show that phase discontinu-
ities, such as branch-cuts of complex branch functions, supply a third distinct binding mechanism.
We derive the existence of guided modes that propagate along the cut, called Dirac branch-cut
(DBC) modes, which obey an effective one-dimensional relativistic Dirac equation with a reduced
mass determined by the phase difference across the cut. When the mass field has fixed magnitude,
the DBC modes’ transverse confinement lengths are energy-independent, unlike Jackiw–Rebbi and
Jackiw–Rossi states or conventional boundary modes. Using acoustic metamaterials, we realize DBC
modes experimentally, and verify their relativistic dispersion, robust transverse confinement length,
and ability to propagate along freeform paths. These results show that phase discontinuities in a
complex Dirac mass field constitute a versatile design principle for guided modes, with interesting
application possibilities for photonic and acoustic metamaterials.
The emergence of bound states in Dirac media is of
profound importance for many fields of physics, includ-
ing particle physics, condensed matter physics, and pho-
tonics. For example, the Jackiw–Rebbi state [1], which
appears at a domain wall in one-dimensional (1D) space
where a Dirac mass field changes sign, is recognized as
a theoretical prototype for the edge states of topologi-
cal insulators in 1D or higher dimensions [2]. Such edge
states have many interesting properties, and have been
observed in a variety of real materials [3, 4], as well as
metamaterials based on photonics, acoustics, and other
classical-wave platforms [5–10].
The concept has also
been extended beyond domain walls to features such as
higher-order boundaries [11–18] and spatial defects [19–
24]. Notably, a leading conceptual framework for under-
standing defect-bound topological states is the Jackiw–
Rossi model [25], a generalization of the Jackiw–Rebbi
model that features a Dirac field in two-dimensional (2D)
space with a complex mass field (composed of two inde-
pendent Dirac mass parameters); a vortex in the phase
of the complex mass binds a localized zero-energy state
that is robust against mass field variations that do not al-
ter the winding number [26]. These Jackiw–Rossi states
form the conceptual foundation for Majorana excitations

in topological superconductors [27–29], and have recently
also been experimentally realized in Kekul´e-modulated

ongoing research into using “Dirac vortex” modes for
enhancing light-matter interactions, designing new laser
cavity designs, and other practical applications [33–38].
While the effects of domain walls and vortices in Dirac
mass fields have been extensively studied, an important
intermediate case—a phase discontinuity along a curve—
has received little attention. Such discontinuities can oc-
cur when the mass is set to a branch of a multi-valued
complex function, like a root or logarithm, which contains
a branch-cut [39, 40]: i.e., a 1D curve in 2D space along
which there is a dislocation, or phase discontinuity, in the
complex function. Here, we identify and characterize a
new class of bound states arising on such cuts, which we
call Dirac branch-cut (DBC) modes. We show that these
modes possess a tunable relativistic dispersion, with a
reduced effective mass determined by the phase discon-
tinuity. Remarkably, when the magnitude of the mass
field (and hence the bulk band gap) are fixed, the DBC
modes’ transverse confinement lengths are independent
of energy, unlike conventional boundary modes, whose
confinement weakens near the band gap edge. These fea-
tures persist for different cut choices. If the mass field
has nonzero winding at infinity, one DBC mode also cor-
responds to the mid-gap Jackiw–Rossi state [25].
We then realize DBC modes experimentally using clas-
sical acoustic structures.

acoustics, photonics, and other settings.

From these findings, we hypothesize that the DBC
modes arise chiefly from the presence of a discontinuity
in θ(r), and will also exist for phase dislocations along ar-
bitrary 1D curves. A convenient way to generate phase
dislocations is to use a branch-cut—a 1D curve connect-
ing different Riemann sheets of a complex multi-valued
function, like a complex root or logarithm [39]. A branch
function is specified by choosing one Riemann sheet, and
is differentiable for all r except at the cut, where there is
a constant phase discontinuity given by the monodromy
(i.e., the multiplicity of the Riemann sheets). As an ex-
ample, take f(z) = (z −z1)α(z −z2)β, where z = x + iy,
and α, β ∈Q with α + β ∈Z. The branch points are
z1 and z2, with no branch point at infinity; we can place
the cut arbitrarily, so long as it does not cross itself and
terminates at the branch points. All along the cut, there
is a phase discontinuity of ∆θ ≡2πα ≡−2πβ (mod 2π).

A major advantage of DBC modes is their inherent
flexibility: they can transmit along arbitrary paths that
can be easily generated by assigning branch-cuts.
To

spaced points across the sample. Evidently, the acoustic

the spiral, with no sign of Anderson localization in the
longitudinal direction.

green line). Moreover, we note that the DBC modes ap-
pear to be at least as performant as the freeform topo-
logical waveguides generated using amorphous lattices
[46, 47], but are easier to generate as there is no need
to generate a lattice by computational close-packing.
In summary, we have proposed and implemented a
new class of robust guided modes in Dirac media called
Dirac branch-cut (DBC) modes, which occur along 1D
phase discontinuities and are distinct from both Jackiw–
Rebbi [1] and Jackiw–Rossi [25] bound states. We have
proven theoretically, and verified experimentally, that
DBC modes obey a 1D relativistic dispersion relation.
We have presented an explicit acoustic realization; this
design is easily transferrable to photonics (see Supple-
mentary Information). We have verified that the acous-
tic DBC modes can form line cavities and propagate ro-
bustly along complicated paths.
In future, we expect
that the lattice modulation scheme can be further re-
fined to bring the actual DBC modes closer to their ideal
counterparts, including making the dispersion relations
hyperbolic over a larger portion of the bulk gap. The sim-
ple realization scheme makes DBC modes versatile can-
didates for photonic (see Supplementary Information) or
acoustic waveguides, and their strong confinement could
be helpful for managing inter-waveguide crosstalk. Thus,
our results point to a promising pathway for practical de-
vice applications based on the fundamental properties of
relativistic band structures.

SAMPLE FABRICATION AND
CHARACTERIZATION
Each acoustic sample is composed of an upper plate,
lower plate, and sealing plug. The upper plate is made of
transparent acrylic with modulus 3000 MPa and density
1190 kg/m3, with holes at designated positions for insert-
ing acoustic probes. The lower plate and sealing plug are
manufactured via stereolithography, using photosensitive
resin with modulus 2880 MPa and density 1100 kg/m3.
The pillars are fabricated directly on the lower plate.
For the dispersion measurements of Fig. 2c, we set up
an acoustic source (Br¨uel & Kjær Type 2735) to emit
a monochromatic signal through a narrow tube (radius
≈1.5 mm and length 100 mm) to a point on the sam-
ple’s left edge.
The signal is picked up by two probe
microphones (Br¨uel & Kjær Type 4182). The first micro-
phone scans over 48 small holes (diameter 2 mm, step size
12.56 mm) placed along the line of the phase dislocation
(parallel to the x direction). During each measurement,
the other holes are filled with sealing plugs to prevent
acoustic leakage. The second microphone is fixed towards
the source to pick up a reference signal. The acoustic
pressure at different positions is recorded with a data col-
lector (Br¨uel&Kjær Type 3560-C), and a Fourier trans-
form is performed to obtain the kx-dependence.
This
process is repeated at different frequencies to obtain the
results in Fig. 2c.
For the intensity profile measurements of Figs. 3 and 4,
we use a monochromatic acoustic source and two probe
microphones in a similar manner. The acoustic signal is
picked up along a grid of holes with spacing 16.74 mm in
both the x and y directions.

HIGHER-ORDER DBC MODES
In Fig. 1h of the main text, we plotted the spatial profiles for several Dirac branch-cut (DBC) modes on an
exemplary bounded cut. Here, we present the spatial profiles for several other modes. In Fig. S1a, we plot the profiles
for DBC modes # 4 and # 7 at ∆θ = π (all other parameters are the same as in Fig. 1h of the main text). It can be
seen that the mode profiles are extremely similar to those of modes # 1 and # 10 from in the main text. In Fig. S1b,
we plot all four modes’ profiles along the center line x = 0. This reveals that the confinement lengths are all highly
similar, consistent with the analytic prediction derived from straight cuts.
In Fig. S1c, we plot the spatial profiles of two representative modes for the ∆θ = 2π/3 case. The corresponding
profiles along x = 0 are plotted in Fig. S1d. Once again, the modes at very different energies have the same confinement
lengths, averaging to κ ≈0.87, while the analytic value [Eq. (5) of the main text] is κ = sin(π/3)/ sin(π/2) ≈0.86603.
In Fig. 1c–e of the main text, we presented a case where the Jackiw–Rossi state coincides with one of the DBC
modes, and has properties similar to all the other DBC modes. It is also possible to design m(r) functions hosting

at E = 0. At the same time, the phase discontinuity at the cut is 2π/3, so the DBC modes are gapped—i.e., their
energies cannot overlap with that of the Jackiw–Rossi state, as shown in Fig. S2b. The spatial profiles for several

S4.
ACOUSTIC STRUCTURE DESIGN
The acoustic structure that we use to realize the Jackiw–Rossi model, and the consequent DBC modes, is similar
to the design in Ref. 49. We begin with a triangular lattice of solid pillars surrounded by air, as shown in Fig. 2a of
the main text. If all pillars have equal radii, the band structure has two Dirac points at the corners of the hexagonal
Brillouin zone. If the lattice is viewed in terms of an expanded unit cell with six pillars each, both Dirac points
are folded to the center, Γ, of the shrunken Brillouin zone [8]. In Fig. S3a, the gray lines show the acoustic band
structure calculated by FEM. Near Γ, therefore, we have four bands (two from each valley) governed by an effective
Jackiw–Rossi model with m1 = m2 = 0.
We then apply a Kekul´e-type modulation by varying the pillar radii according to Eq. (6) of the main text, which
involves a modulation amplitude ∆R ̸= 0 and phase θ. The modulation lifts the Dirac point degeneracy, as seen in
Fig. S3a (the red lines show the bands for θ = −0.35π). Since the modulation phase parameter θ is an angle variable
by construction, we infer that it maps to the phase parameter arg[m] for the complex mass field [49]. In fact, this
parameter mapping is not exact: the modulation phase also induces a variation in |m|, as seen in the variation in
the bulk gap in Fig. S3b. In the present work, we neglect the |m| variation when designing our DBC waveguide
structures; in the future, further optimizing the modulation scheme may enable creating more performant structures.
Thus, to realize a Jackiw–Rossi model with spatially-varying arg[m(r)], we apply the corresponding spatially-varying
modulation phase parameter θ(r) [33, 35]. For instance, it is possible to generate a Jackiw–Rossi (or “Dirac vortex”)
state with a θ(r) distribution like the one shown in Fig. S3c, containing a phase singularity. Using this θ(r), we

COMPARISON TO DEFECT MODE CAVITY
In the main text, we observed that the DBC modes can be used to form line cavities, by using a branch cut
terminating at finite branch points.
Similar line cavities can also be formed with conventional defect modes, by
altering a line of unit cells in an otherwise uniform lattice [54]; typically, these altered cells are placed in straight line,
since conventional defect modes are susceptible to Anderson localization in the longitudinal direction (i.e., along the
cavity line). Aside from the Anderson localization issue, DBC modes may also possess an advantage in having more
uniform transverse confinement lengths, as noted in the main text. Here, we use FEM simulations to perform such a
comparison.
For the DBC-based line cavity, we use the acoustic structure described in Sec. S4 and the main text, with ∆θ = π.

line cavity, with no coupling waveguide. The simulated eigenmode Q-factors are plotted in Fig. S4b. We will focus
on the four cavity eigenmodes around 10 kHz, which are formed by the lower band of DBC modes, because their

gap edge or just inside the bulk band (see Fig. S4b).

PHOTONIC ANALOGUE
In this section, we present FEM simulation results showing that the present scheme for realizing DBC modes in

case, we can use the DBC modes to form various configurations such as line cavities, as shown in Fig. S5c–d. Photonic
realizations at higher frequency regimes can also be achieved by scaling down the dimensions of the structure.
