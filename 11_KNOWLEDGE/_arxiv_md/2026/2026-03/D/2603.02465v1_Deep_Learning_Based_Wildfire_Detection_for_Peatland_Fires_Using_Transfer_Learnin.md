ABSTRACT
Machine learning (ML)–based wildfire detection methods have been developed in recent years, primarily
using deep learning (DL) models trained on large collections of wildfire images and videos. However,
peatland fires exhibit distinct visual and physical characteristics—such as smoldering combustion, low flame
intensity, persistent smoke, and subsurface burning—that limit the effectiveness of conventional wildfire
detectors trained on open-flame forest fires. In this work, we present a transfer learning–based approach
for peatland fire detection that leverages knowledge learned from general wildfire imagery and adapts
it to the peatland fire domain. We initialize a DL-based peatland fire detector using pretrained weights
from a conventional wildfire detection model and subsequently fine-tune the network using a dataset
composed of Malaysian peatland images and videos. This strategy enables effective learning despite the
limited availability of labeled peatland fire data. Experimental results demonstrate that transfer learning
significantly improves detection accuracy and robustness compared to training from scratch, particularly
under challenging conditions such as low-contrast smoke, partial occlusions, and variable illumination.
The proposed approach provides a practical and scalable solution for early peatland fire detection and has
the potential to support real-time monitoring systems for fire prevention and environmental protection.
Keywords: Peatland Fires, Smoldering Fire, Peat Fire Detection, Transfer Learning, Deep Learning

INTRODUCTION
Wildfires pose a significant threat to ecosystems, infrastructure, and human health worldwide. In recent
years, machine learning, and deep learning–based wildfire detection systems have emerged as effective
tools for early fire identification using visual data from cameras, drones, and satellites [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8].
These systems typically rely on convolutional neural networks (CNNs), vision transformers or video-based
deep learning architectures trained on large datasets of wildfire images and videos, enabling rapid and
automated fire detection.
While existing approaches perform well for open-flame forest and grassland fires, their effectiveness is
substantially reduced when applied to peatland fires. Peatland fires differ fundamentally from conventional
wildfires in both appearance and behavior. They often involve low-temperature smoldering combustion,
minimal visible flames, persistent smoke plumes, and underground fire spread. As a result, visual cues
commonly used by standard wildfire detectors—such as bright flames or high-contrast fire regions—are
often absent or weak in peatland fire scenarios.

Peatland fires are particularly critical due to their long duration, difficulty of suppression, and severe
environmental impact, including regional air pollution. Countries with extensive peatland regions, such
as Malaysia and Indonesia, face recurring peat fires that demand early and reliable detection to mitigate
damage. However, the scarcity of labeled peatland fire data presents a major challenge for training deep
learning models from scratch.
Toaddressthischallenge, thispaperproposesatransferlearning–basedpeatlandfiredetectionframework.
Transfer learning allows a model trained on a large and diverse wildfire dataset to transfer its learned feature
representations to a related but distinct domain. In this work, we initialize a deep learning–based peatland
fire detector using pretrained weights from a general wildfire detection model. The model is then fine-tuned
using a curated dataset of Malaysian peatland fire images and videos, enabling adaptation to the unique
visual characteristics of peat fires.
The contributions of this work are threefold:
(i) We demonstrate that transfer learning is an effective strategy for peatland fire detection under limited
data conditions.
(ii) We present a peatland fire dataset derived from real-world imagery and videos captured in Malaysian
peatland regions.
(iii) We show that the proposed approach improves detection performance and robustness compared
to conventional wildfire detection models applied directly to peatland scenarios.
(iv) We use a Walsh Hadamard Transform (WHT)-based ResNet in this application and show that the
WHT-based ResNet produces better results than a standard ResNet because the transform is orthogonal,
and it learns better, with smaller number of parameters, than the standard ResNet model.
The proposed method offers a practical path toward deployable, real-time peatland fire monitoring
systems, supporting early intervention, and contributing to wildfire prevention efforts not only in Malaysia
and the United States, but also across diverse geographic regions worldwide. Also, low contrast wildfire
signatures also arise in spotting, where lofted embers can be difficult to detect reliably, especially under
smoke and variable illumination. The transfer learning and lightweight spectral feature ideas used here are
also compatible with ember detection pipelines in beyond visible imagery [9].

BACKGROUD
2.1 Transfer Learning
Transfer learning is a practical strategy that allows a model trained on one task to be rapidly adapted to
a related task with minimal additional data and training time [10]. In the context of wildfire and smoke
monitoring, this approach is especially valuable because collecting and annotating large image datasets for
every new environment or fire type can be costly and time–consuming. Instead of training a new model
from scratch, an already robust wildfire detection model can be reused as a strong starting point and then
fine–tuned for a different but related scenario, such as peatland fire or peat smoke detection.
In this work, models that were first trained on the GWFP (Global Wildfire Prevention)1 dataset
were subsequently fine–tuned on the target dataset composed of Malaysian peatland images and videos
dataset. Only a small amount of additional training was required for the model to adapt to the new visual
characteristics, such as differences in terrain, vegetation density, and smoke appearance. This process
significantly reduced training effort while maintaining high performance.

The main idea of our modified Walsh-Hadamard-transform (WHT)-based convolutional neural network

4
CONCLUSION
This study demonstrates that transfer learning is an effective and practical strategy for adapting deep
learning–based wildfire detection models to the more challenging task of peatland fire detection. Unlike
open–flame forest fires, peatland fires often exhibit low–intensity combustion, persistent smoke, and visually
subtle cues that make them difficult to identify using models trained solely on conventional wildfire imagery.
By initializing the detector with weights learned from a large and diverse wildfire dataset and subsequently
fine–tuning it on a smaller peatland–specific dataset, the proposed approach achieves strong detection
performance while requiring significantly less data and training effort than building a model from scratch.
