Abstract
Graph diffusion models have gained significant attention in graph generation tasks, but they often
inherit and amplify topology biases from sensitive attributes (e.g. gender, age, region), leading to unfair
synthetic graphs. Existing fair graph generation using diffusion models is limited to specific graph-based
applications with complete labels or requires simultaneous updates for graph structure and node attributes,
making them unsuitable for general usage. To relax these limitations by applying the debiasing method
directly on graph topology, we propose Fair Graph Diffusion Model (FairGDiff), a counterfactual-based
one-step solution that mitigates topology biases while balancing fairness and utility. In detail, we construct
a causal model to capture the relationship between sensitive attributes, biased link formation, and the
generated graph structure. By answering the counterfactual question “Would the graph structure change
if the sensitive attribute were different?”, we estimate an unbiased treatment and incorporate it into the
diffusion process. FairGDiff integrates counterfactual learning into both forward diffusion and backward
denoising, ensuring that the generated graphs are independent of sensitive attributes while preserving
structural integrity. Extensive experiments on real-world datasets demonstrate that FairGDiff achieves
a superior trade-off between fairness and utility, outperforming existing fair graph generation methods
while maintaining scalability.

Introduction
In recent years, substantial efforts have been dedicated to generated graph generation across various domains,
such as privacy-preserving graph synthesis (Fu et al., 2023), molecule generation (Jin et al., 2018), traffic
modeling (Yu and Gu, 2019), and code completion (Brockschmidt et al., 2018). Among the diverse techniques
developed for graph generation, graph diffusion models (Vignac et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Kong et al.,
2023) has emerged as a leading technique for this task. These models are typically trained to reverse a
diffusion process, which progressively corrupts training graphs by adding noise, enabling them to effectively
model and reproduce the underlying distribution of training graphs.
However, while diffusion models excel at capturing graph distributions, they are susceptible to inheriting
and even amplifying topology biases presented in the training graphs. Topology biases frequently exist in
real-world graphs due to the homophilic effect caused by certain sensitive node attributes (e.g., gender, race,
age) (Wang et al., 2022; Dai and Wang, 2021; Ling et al., 2023), where a pair of nodes is more likely to form
an edge if they share the same sensitive node attribute value. Without proper intervention, the diffusion model

Mitigating topology biases in graph generation is a crucial yet largely underexplored challenge. One
straightforward solution is to utilize a two-step strategy, which perform graph structure purification (such
as FairDrop (Spinelli et al., 2021)) before model training. However, this approach is often found to be
suboptimal, as the quality of the generated graphs heavily relies on the purification method. Additionally,
modifying the original training distribution through such preprocessing can degrade the utility of both the
diffusion model and the generated graphs. This issue can be further evidenced in Figure 1, in which we
compare the downstream performance of original graphs, generated graphs by vanilla diffusion trained on the
original graphs (i.e., Original + Diffusion) and generated graphs via this two-step solution (i.e., FairDrop +
Diffusion). The results demonstrate that such two-step strategies often fail to generate graphs that achieve a
balanced trade-off between utility and unbiasedness for downstream usage.
Motivated by the drawbacks of two-step strategies for fair graph generation mentioned above, some
recent works, such as FairWire (Kose and Shen, 2024) and FairGen (Zheng et al., 2024), attempt to enforce
fairness during the training of graph generative models, rather than relying on the pre-processing step in
two-step approaches. However, FairWire (Kose and Shen, 2024) requires simultaneous updates for graph
structure, node features, and sensitive attribute information to ensure bias mitigation, which may limit its
applications when the generated features are substantially different from node features in downstream tasks.
FairGen (Zheng et al., 2024) requires label information to perform the debiasing method via random walk,
which is also a limitation for general graphs where labels are unavailable or incomplete. These limitations
highlight the need for fairness-aware graph generation methods that operate effectively without requiring
explicit attribute modifications or label dependencies.
In this work, we aim to relax these limitations by directly disentangling the sensitive attribute based
homophilic pattern from the graph topology when generating the synthetic graph, which results in a general
one-step solution. In other words, it is not necessary to acquire an unbiased input graph using graph
purification methods before diffusion model training, and such disentanglement and diffusion model training
occur simultaneously. By saying it general, since the homophilic pattern only concerns biased edges between
nodes with similar sensitive attributes, its disentanglement neither requires label information nor interferes
the original node features. Specifically, we disentangle the homophilic pattern on sensitive attributes through

Related Work
Graph fairness learning has garnered significant attention in recent years (Chen et al., 2024; Laclau et al.,
2022). Current efforts in graph fairness aim to address three key notions: group fairness (Dai and Wang,
2021; Rahman et al., 2019), individual fairness (Song et al., 2022), and counterfactual fairness (Guo et al.,
2023). To mitigate bias in graph learning, existing methods typically intervene at one of three stages: (1)
pre-processing the graph before training (Li et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022b), (2) modifying the training
procedure itself (Li et al., 2021), or (3) applying post-processing techniques to adjust the outputs (Masrour
et al., 2020). Although promising progress has been made in graph fairness learning, most existing studies
focus on node classification, link prediction, and graph classification tasks. This leaves a significant research
gap in addressing fairness within graph generative models.
A limited study, FairDrop (Li et al., 2021), explores graph fairness by deleting suspicious biased edges
of a certain probability. Yet, this approach is often suboptimal, as randomly deleting some edges can
distort the training distribution and reduce the quality of generated graphs. To address these limitations,
FairWire (Kose and Shen, 2024) jointly updates graph structure, node features, and sensitive attributes to
mitigate bias. However, this may restrict its applicability when the generated features differ significantly
from those required in downstream tasks. Another study, FairGen (Zheng et al., 2024), relies on label
information to apply its debiasing method via random walks, presenting a limitation in scenarios where labels
are unavailable or incomplete. These limitations motivate our proposed fairness-aware graph generation
method that avoids reliance on attribute changes or label information.

3.2
Graph Diffusion Model
Graph diffusion models have emerged as a class of widely adopted graph generative methods across various
domains, which work by iteratively refining the graph information (e.g. structure, attribute or representation)
to simulate a stochastic diffusion process. There are four main categories of graph diffusion models, namely
denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) (Jin et al., 2018; Bojchevski et al., 2018; Vignac et al.,
2022; Haefeli et al., 2022; Anand and Achim, 2022; Trippe et al., 2022; Luo et al., 2022), score-based
generative models (SGMs) (Niu et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2022a), latent diffusion models (Yang et al., 2024;
Evdaimon et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2024), and stochastic differential equation based diffusion models (SDEs)
(Luo et al., 2023; Jo et al., 2022), differing in the design of the forward diffusion process and backward
denoising process, which in general can be summarized as follows.
Forward Diffusion Process (Noise Injection).
The forward process gradually corrupts a given graph by
adding noise over multiple timesteps. Given an initial graph G0, the process iteratively adds Gaussian noise
to transform it into a nearly random graph. We use z0 to denote the initial graph information, which can either

2022; Trippe et al., 2022; Luo et al., 2022), SGMs (Niu et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2022a), and SDEs (Luo
et al., 2023; Jo et al., 2022), the noise is added directly to the input graph i.e., z0 : = G0 = (X0, A0); and

3.3
Causal Model
Counterfactual causal inference aims to find the causal relationship between treatment and outcome. A key
concept in causal inference is counterfactual reasoning, which asks, “Would the outcome have been different
if the treatment had changed”. A fundamental goal of causal inference is to estimate the causal effect of a
treatment T on an outcome Y. Given a confounder Z that influences both T and Y , a common causal structure
is represented as: Z →T →Y , as show in Figure 2.

4.2
Unbiased Counterfactual Treatment with Causal Model
Construction of Causal Model.
To mitigate the effect of topological bias in graph diffusion, we construct a
causal model as shown in Figure 2 that captures the dependency between biased input graph GF = (U, AF ),
treatment of the link between two nodes on the graph, and the generated graph ˆG = ( ˆU, ˆA). In our causal
setting, the confounder Z is the biased input graph GF , which influences both the treatment assignment and
the structure of the generated graph. The treatment variable T determines whether the link formation process
is biased or not. In a topology-biased setting, edges preferentially form between nodes with the same sensitive
attribute. Conversely, an unbiased link formation process is independent of sensitive attribute homophily.
And the outcome Y in the causal model is the generated graph ˆG by the trained graph diffusion model,
which inherits the biased or unbiased property from T. This causal dependency follows the structure of
causal model Z →T →Y , where Z influences T by increasing or decreasing the likelihood of homophilic
connections between node pairs on the graph, which in turn affects outcome (i.e. generated graph ˆG) by
reinforcing structural biases in the generated graph. Therefore, in order to achieve the goal of training a fair
graph diffusion model, we attempt to address the following key counterfactual question: “Given a biased
input graph GF = (U, AF ) and the identified bias T F , would the graph structure AF change if the sensitive
attribute homophily (i.e. T F ) were different?”

4.3
FairGDiff Training with Counterfactual Treatment
To generate fair graph structures while preserving utility, FairGDiff integrates counterfactual treatment
throughout the entire diffusion process, ensuring that both forward diffusion and backward denoising
incorporate counterfactual learning. In the forward diffusion phase, noise is progressively added to both the
factual and counterfactual graph representations, ensuring that the model learns structural variations under
different treatment assignments. During the backward denoising phase, the model reconstructs the graph
while conditioning on both factual and counterfactual treatments, effectively mitigating bias inherited from
sensitive attributes. By leveraging both factual and counterfactual diffusion paths, FairGDiff ensures that
the generated graph structure remains independent of sensitive attributes while preserving key topological
properties.

4.4
Complexity Analysis
The complexity of the Variational Graph Autoencoder (Evdaimon et al., 2024) is determined by a GIN
encoder and a three-layer MLP decoder, with a complexity of O(Ld2N + Ld|E|) (Wu et al., 2020) and
O(dh(d + 2)|E|) (Zhao et al., 2022) respectively, where L is the number of GIN layers, d is the number of
attributes, |E| is the number of edges, and dh is the number of neurons in the hidden layer. The complexity of
the Latent Diffusion Model is limited to O(Tl2) where l is the dimension of the latent representation z and T
is the denoising time step. Therefore, the proposed Unbiased Graph Generator has linear time complexity
w.r.t. the sum of node and edge counts.

5.1
Experiment Settings
Datasets. In our experiments, we use four real-world datasets: NBA (Dai and Wang, 2021), German
Credit (Asuncion et al., 2007), and Pokec (including both Pokec-n and Pokec-z) (Takac and Zabovsky, 2012).

financial records with gender as the sensitive attribute, commonly used for fairness evaluation in credit
scoring. Pokec-n and Pokec-z are regional subsets of the Slovak social network Pokec, where gender is the
sensitive attribute, making them suitable for studying biases in social link formation. These datasets provide
diverse scenarios for evaluating fairness in graph generation.
Graph Diffusion Models. For the diffusion model for training in FairGDiff, we utilize a latent graph
diffusion model (Evdaimon et al., 2024) in our experiments due to computational resource constraints limiting
the feasibility of other graph diffusion models discussed in Sec. 3.2.
Baselines. We mainly compare our experimental results with four fair graph generative models, namely
FairAdj (Li et al., 2021), FairDrop (Spinelli et al., 2021), FairWire (Kose and Shen, 2024), and FairGen (Zheng
et al., 2024). FairAdj (Li et al., 2021) aims to obtain a fair adjacency matrix under some fairness restrictions
for link prediction. FairDrop (Spinelli et al., 2021) finds a debiased adjacency matrix by randomly dropping
some biased edges in graph data. FairWire (Kose and Shen, 2024) is a diffusion-based graph generation
framework that embeds a theory-guided fairness regularizer into the denoiser to mitigate structural bias in
both link prediction and synthetic graphs. FairGen (Zheng et al., 2024) defines a supervised random walk
strategy to avoid biased connections by incorporating both label information and parity constraints.

Evaluation Metrics
- Metrics for Utility. In our experiments, we measure utility using two common metrics: test accuracy for

- Metrics for Fairness. For fairness evaluation, we adopt statistical parity (∆DP ) and equal opportunity

∆DP = |P(ˆy = 1|s = 0) −P(ˆy = 1|s = 1)|,

- Metrics for Efficiency. To evaluate the efficiency of FairGDiff, we use Floating Point Operations Per Second

5.3
Node Classification on Synthetic Graphs
To assess the efficacy of FairGDiff, we conduct node classification experiments on generated graphs generated
by FairGDiff and baseline methods. We evaluate on NBA (Dai and Wang, 2021), Pokec-n, and Pokec-
z (Takac and Zabovsky, 2012) datasets. For each dataset, nodes are randomly split into 50% for training,
25% for validation, and 25% for testing. A GCN classifier is trained on the generated graphs to predict node
labels, enabling a fair comparison across generative models.
The results shown in Table 1 demonstrate that FairGDiff consistently outperforms baseline methods with
better trade-off between utility (Accuracy) and fairness (∆DP and ∆EO) across three different datasets. This
implies that our approach mitigates topology-induced bias while preserving the predictive performance of
synthetic graphs.
5.4
Graph Contrastive Learning on Synthetic Graphs
Since FairGDiff is trained without node label information, it naturally aligns with self-supervised learning
frameworks, making Graph Contrastive Learning (GCL) (You et al., 2020) a suitable evaluation approach.
GCL learns node representations by contrasting similar (positive) and dissimilar (negative) node pairs,
allowing us to assess whether FairGDiff generates embeddings that retain structural utility while mitigating
bias.
10


## Page 11


To be specific, we first learn node representations from all generated graphs using graph contrastive
learning. To evaluate the quality of the learned representations, we conduct two downstream tasks: node
classification and link prediction. For node classification, we train a GCN classifier using embeddings
obtained from graph contrastive learning. In terms of link prediction, we use the learned embeddings to
predict missing links in the graph. Here, we conduct experiments on NBA (Dai and Wang, 2021), Pokec-n,
Pokec-n (Takac and Zabovsky, 2012), and German Credit (Asuncion et al., 2007) datasets. For Pokec-n and
Pokec-z, we randomly split 10%, 10%, and 80% for training, validating, and testing, and the mini-batch
training pipeline is used to reduce computation complexity for these large scaled datasets. For NBA (Dai and
Wang, 2021) and German Credit (Asuncion et al., 2007), a 20%, 35%, and 45% splitting ratio is allocated to
training, validating, and testing, and a whole-batch training strategy is adopted with them.
The trade-off performance for node classification and link prediction by GCL is presented in Table 2
and Table 3 respectively. For node classification, the evaluation is conducted on embeddings learned via
GCL rather than directly using raw node features. This setup allows us to assess whether the learned
representations from generated graphs effectively capture structural information while mitigating bias. We
also evaluate the effectiveness of embeddings learned through GCL for link prediction tasks by measuring
how well they preserve meaningful connectivity patterns. The results in both cases indicate not only a superior
utility–fairness trade-off over baselines, but also some gains in individual utility and individual fairness
metrics, particularly on the link prediction task.

In this work, we propose FairGDiff, a novel fairness-aware graph generation framework that mitigates

Parameter Sensitivity Analysis
There are two essential hyperparameters in FairGDiff, namely γ1 controlling the utility of synthetic graphs
and γ2 alleviating the bias during the denoising process. We train FairGDiff on NBA with different γ1 and γ2
to determine the parameter sensitivity. With the sensitivity analysis, we conclude the best ranges to achieve a
high utility score with good fairness constraints.
We set the hyperparameters γ1 in [1, 2, 5, 10, 20] and γ2 in [0.1, 0.2,
0.5, 0.75, 1] via node classification on NBA. We can find that when γ1 ≤5 and γ2 ≤0.5, FairGDiff reaches
the best balance between classification accuracy and fairness metrics. The detailed results of the sensitivity
analysis are shown in Figure 5, where we conclude the best choice of γ1 and γ2 to be γ1 = 5 and γ2 = 0.2.
