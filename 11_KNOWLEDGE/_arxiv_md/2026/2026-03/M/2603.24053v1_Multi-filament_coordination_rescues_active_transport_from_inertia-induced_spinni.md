Multi-filament coordination rescues active transport from inertia-induced spinning
arrest
Anuradha Rajput,1 Arnab Bhattacharjee,1, ∗and Annwesha Dutta1, †
1School of Computational and Integrative Sciences,
Jawaharlal Nehru University, New Delhi 110067, India
(Dated: March 26, 2026)
Active filaments driven by tangential forces can become trapped in a spinning state when attached
to a heavy head, where activity and inertia drive persistent rotation rather than directed transport.
Using three-dimensional Langevin dynamics of tangentially driven bead-spring chains anchored to
a common heavy head, we demonstrate that increasing the filament number Nf systematically res-
cues directed transport by sterically preventing the coiled conformations that underlie spinning.
The rescue is established through three independent diagnostics: (i) the mean-square displacement
recovers monotonic growth (transport rescue), (ii) the spatial tangent autocorrelation loses its neg-
ative dip signaling helical coiling (conformational rescue), and (iii) the tangent time autocorrelation
ceases crossing zero (orientational rescue). At high bending stiffness (κb = 1000), coiling is fully
eliminated at a critical filament number N ∗
f ≈3. At moderate stiffness (κb = 100), residual coiling
persists (min Cs ≈−0.13) yet transport is still rescued—demonstrating that the destruction of spin-
ning coherence, not coiling elimination, is the essential mechanism. The multi-filament architecture
achieves up to five orders of magnitude transport enhancement. Two physically distinct rescue path-
ways emerge: at high stiffness, steric constraints force filaments into a coordinated bundle sustaining
directed propulsion; at low stiffness, steric interactions destroy orientational coherence, producing
enhanced active diffusion.
These results demonstrate a purely mechanical, density-independent
route to overcome inertia-induced motility arrest, with implications for synthetic microswimmer
design, motor-driven filament assays, and multi-filament organization in biological systems.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Active filaments—polymers driven out of equilibrium
by internal or external forces—are ubiquitous across bi-
ological scales. Motor-driven cytoskeletal filaments form
the basis of intracellular transport and cell motility [1–
4], bacterial flagella propel cells through viscous envi-
ronments [5–9], and cilia generate fluid flows through
synchronized beating [10, 11].
These systems operate
far from equilibrium, and their motility emerges from a
complex interplay of activity, elasticity, dissipation, and
inertia [12–18]. In the overdamped regime, tangentially
self-propelled semiflexible polymers exhibit states rang-
ing from open beating to compact spirals [1, 16, 35],
with activity producing enhanced diffusion [3, 36], spi-
ral formation [16, 58], activity-dependent persistence
length [2, 37], and conformational collapse [34].
A key development has been the recognition that iner-
tia qualitatively alters active filament dynamics. While
classical active matter theory operates in the overdamped
regime [17, 18], underdamped dynamics introduce new
phenomena: L¨owen [24] showed that finite mass modi-
fies the ballistic-to-diffusive crossover, Scholz et al. [25]
demonstrated inertial delay effects, and Caprini et al. [26]
showed that inertia modifies active phase behavior. For
active filaments specifically, Isele-Holder et al. [16] iden-
tified spontaneous spiral formation, and Karan et al. [19]

mapped a re-entrant phase diagram featuring open, beat-
ing, and spinning-spiral states as a function of inertia
and activity.
Khalilian et al. [20] analyzed the spiral
onset as a subcritical pitchfork bifurcation, Fazelzadeh
et al. [21] showed that inertia enhances effective persis-
tence length, and Tejedor et al. [23] demonstrated inertia-
strengthened head–tail asymmetry. Further studies have
characterized inertial effects on polymer relaxation [22],
active nematics [28], crowded transport [29], and dynam-
ical scaling [27]. Complementary work on asymmetric ac-
tivity distributions [30–33] has shown that concentrating
forces—whether active or inertial—at specific locations
along the backbone profoundly affects the motility mode.
Taken together, these findings establish that spinning
arrest—persistent rotation rather than translation—is a
generic feature of inertial active filament dynamics. The
central question motivating this work is: can this motility
arrest be overcome, and if so, by what mechanism?
In biology and synthetic systems, active filaments are
often organized into multi-filament architectures: flagel-
lar bundles on bacterial cell bodies [8, 9, 38, 39], actin
stress fibers at focal adhesions [40], microtubule asters
at centrosomes [41], and interacting filaments in motility
assays [42, 43].
Whether such organization provides a
functional advantage specifically by suppressing spinning
arrest has not been investigated.
Two prior studies are particularly relevant.
Janzen
and Matoz-Fernandez [44] showed that in dense two-
dimensional suspensions, inter-filament collisions desta-
bilize spirals through a density-dependent reentrant
transition—demonstrating that steric interactions can
disrupt spinning, but requiring high density. Buglakov et

C.
Spatial tangent autocorrelation (SACF)
The bond tangent vector is defined consistently with
the active force direction as ˆti = (ri−1 −ri)/|ri−1 −ri|,
pointing from bead i toward the head. The spatial tan-
gent autocorrelation function
Cs(s) = ⟨ˆti(t) · ˆti+s(t)⟩
(14)
measures orientational correlations along the chain con-
tour at separation s (in bond units). The average is taken
over all body-bead indices i (excluding the head), over
time, and over all independent trajectories. In the multi-
filament case, Cs(s) is computed separately for each fila-
ment and then averaged over filaments.
For a passive worm-like chain in equilibrium, Cs(s)
decays exponentially as exp(−sσ/ℓp), remaining positive
for all s. The appearance of a negative region in Cs(s)
at finite s is the structural hallmark of helical coiling:
it indicates that bonds separated by s along the back-
bone are oriented in approximately opposite directions,
meaning the chain has doubled back on itself. The depth
of the negative minimum (denoted min Cs(s)) quantifies
the degree of coiling, while its position along s reflects
the characteristic coiling wavelength (approximately half
the coil circumference).

The tangent time autocorrelation function

measures how a given bond’s orientation decorrelates

filament to reveal inter-filament differences in orienta-

For a passive polymer, Ct(τ) decays monotonically
from unity to zero on the longest relaxation timescale.
For a beating (non-spinning) active filament, Ct(τ) also
decays monotonically but with an activity-modified rate.
For spinning filaments, the TTAF exhibits qualitatively
distinct behavior: it oscillates through zero with a well-
defined period Tspin equal to the spinning rotation pe-
riod, reflecting the periodic reversal of bond orientations
as the coiled chain rotates. The decay envelope of these
oscillations defines the spinning coherence time τspin—
the duration over which spinning maintains phase co-
herence before thermal fluctuations randomize it. The
TTAF thus provides both the spinning frequency (from
Tspin) and the spinning coherence time (from the enve-
lope decay rate).

V.
MULTI-FILAMENT RESCUE
A.
Transport rescue: MSD recovery
The visual contrast is immediate: three-dimensional
trajectories (Fig. 6) show that the spinning-arrested sin-
gle filament at Mh=30 is confined to ∼30σ, while the
five-filament system recovers directed transport compa-
rable to the unimpeded beating state.
Figure 7 quantifies the rescue at Fact = 20.
At
κb = 100 [Fig. 7(a)], the single-filament MSDref drops
from ∼5×104 to ∼103 between Mh=1 and Mh=20, while
the multi-filament system maintains MSDref ≈2 × 107
across this range—two to three orders of magnitude en-
hancement. At Mh=30 the rescue weakens, indicating
limits at low stiffness. At κb = 1000 [Fig. 7(b)], the res-
cue is dramatically stronger: the single filament collapses
by a factor of 50,000 while the multi-filament system
holds at ∼108 even at Mh=40, yielding approximately
five orders of magnitude enhancement at Mh=30.
A striking result is the qualitative inversion of the
role of stiffness:
κb = 1000 produces worse single-
filament transport but better multi-filament transport
than κb = 100, because the same rigidity that stabilizes
the coiled spinning state in isolation promotes aligned
bundled propulsion in the multi-filament system.
B.
Conformational rescue: SACF
The SACF reveals the structural basis of the res-
cue.
For a single filament at Mh=20, the SACF dips
to min Cs(s) ≈−0.75 at both stiffnesses [Fig. 8(a,b)],
confirming strong helical coiling. As Nf increases, this
negative dip is progressively suppressed, but the two stiff-
nesses behave qualitatively differently [Fig. 8(c)].
At κb=1000, the minimum SACF crosses zero near
N ∗
f ≈3 and rises to +0.75 by Nf=7—the chains form
a rigid aligned bundle with coiling fully eliminated. At
κb=100, the coiling depth is reduced by more than 80%
but saturates near min Cs(s) ≈−0.13 for Nf ≥5, never
crossing zero. Flexible filaments retain sufficient confor-
mational freedom to partially coil even when sterically
constrained, whereas stiff filaments are geometrically in-
compatible with simultaneous coiling.
This asymmetry has an important consequence:
at
κb=100, the transport rescue is already complete by
Nf ≈5 (Sec. V A) despite the persistence of residual
coiling. The conformational and transport rescue thus
decouple at low stiffness but coincide at high stiffness—a
distinction whose physical origin is revealed by the TTAF
analysis below. The underlying mechanism is steric frus-
tration: multiple filaments anchored to the same head
cannot all simultaneously adopt the coiled conformation
required for spinning, and the excluded-volume interac-
tions force asymmetric configurations that generate net
translational thrust.

D.
Multi-filament conformational regimes
The multi-filament assembly exhibits distinct confor-

These arise from the competition between bending rigid-

directly to the rescue mechanisms identified above.
In the disordered regime [Fig. 10(a)], the flexible fila-

ture. This regime corresponds to the decorrelation rescue
pathway: steric interactions destroy spinning coherence
without imposing inter-filament alignment.

niscent of a bacterial flagellar bundle. The rigid back-

VI.
DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS
We have demonstrated that multi-filament architec-
tures rescue active transport from inertia-induced spin-
ning arrest through steric frustration of the coiled confor-
mation. Three independent diagnostics—MSD, SACF,
and TTAF—reveal two distinct rescue mechanisms: res-
cue by coordination (high stiffness, persistent bundled
propulsion) and rescue by decorrelation (low stiffness,
elimination of orientational coherence).
At κb=1000,
conformational and transport rescue coincide at N ∗
f ≈3:
coiling is fully eliminated and replaced by a rigid bun-
dle.
At κb=100, the conformational rescue is incom-
plete—residual coiling persists (min Cs(s) ≈−0.13) even
at Nf = 7—yet transport is still rescued by two to three
orders of magnitude. This decoupling demonstrates that
disrupting the coherence of spinning, not eliminating coil-
ing per se, is sufficient for motility rescue.
Relation to prior work.—The spinning arrest is consis-
tent with spiral states reported by Karan et al. [19] and
Isele-Holder et al. [16], extended here to three dimensions
with an explicit heavy head. The closest precedent for
the rescue is the density-dependent spiral destabilization
by Janzen and Matoz-Fernandez [44]; our mechanism is
qualitatively different, operating through the architec-
tural constraint of the star topology for a single isolated
molecule. The active star polymer work of Buglakov et
al. [45] shares the multi-arm topology but did not address
spinning suppression or inertia.
Steric frustration, not force accumulation.—The res-
cue cannot be attributed to trivially having Nf times
more active force. Force scaling (MSDref ∝N 2
f ) accounts
for at most 1.5 orders of magnitude (N 2
f = 25 for Nf=5),
far short of the observed five orders. Moreover, the phase
diagram (Fig. 3) shows that increasing activity on a single
filament deepens the spinning trap—the multi-filament
rescue operates against the force trend. The conforma-
tional evidence (elimination of the SACF negative dip
and cessation of TTAF zero crossings) confirms that the
motility mode itself has changed, not merely the thrust
magnitude.
Applicability and limitations.—Our framework, ex-
cluding hydrodynamic interactions, is directly applica-
ble to dry and quasi-dry settings: in vitro motility as-
says [42, 43, 58], macroscopic active chains [21, 59], glid-
ing bacteria [60], and the hydrodynamically screened in-
tracellular environment [4]. For multi-filament systems in
fluid, HI would add coupling that mediates flagellar syn-
chronization [56, 61] and bundling [57, 62]; our results
establish the baseline that excluded-volume interactions
alone achieve. Additional limitations include equal fila-
ment lengths and fixed anchor geometry.
Outlook.—The two rescue mechanisms suggest design
