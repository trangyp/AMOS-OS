I. Introduction
Surface roughness plays a substantial role in many ﬂows for which Reynolds averaged prediction is needed [1].
Even surfaces that are nearly smooth geometrically, may be hydrodynamically rough at high Reynolds number. If 푟is
the roughness height, the ﬂow is aﬀected when 푟+ ≡푟푢∗/휈= 푅휏푟/훿is not small. If 푅휏is a few thousand, and 훿is a
centimeter, roughness can be signiﬁcant when 푟is on the order of a few 10’s of microns.
Because the viscous wall layer is very thin in high Reynolds number turbulence, it can be disturbed by turbulent
eddies created by small surface irregularity [2].
The mechanism by which these eddies are created is not well
understood. The dominant eddies are larger than the scale of the roughness [3]. They are generated on local shear
layers, not directly by the roughness elements.
Whatever the generation mechanism, for the purposes of RANS
modeling, their only property is that they disrupt the wall layer, which alters the mean ﬂow.
An operational approach is adopted in RANS modeling: the surface is characterized by an equivalent sandgrain
roughness height. This roughness height can be quite diﬀerent from the geometrical roughness height. It is treated as
a hydrodynamic property of the surface. It is determined from a data correlation. That correlation was measured long
ago by Nikuradse, and correlates an oﬀset of the mean velocity in the log-layer to sand grain size. A convenient curve
ﬁt to the correlation was provided by Ligrani and Moﬀat [4], and recited in eq. (2). Similar roughness correlations are
reviewed in Kadivar et al. [2, §9.2].
With an eddy viscosity model, the log-layer velocity oﬀset is determined by the distribution of eddy viscosity
between the wall and the log-layer. Thus, roughness enters as a modiﬁcation to the turbulence model within that region:
such modiﬁcation is the approach adopted herein.
The purpose of the present paper is to adapt the 푘−휔0 model [5] to rough surfaces. Methods to adapt 푘−휔-type
models for surface roughness began with Wilcox [6], who introduced roughness via a boundary value of 휔; he left the
푘boundary condition as 푘= 0. A 푘boundary condition that interpolates between zero and a fully rough value was
introduced in [7], along with a treatment of roughness by adding an eﬀective origin to model formulas. Knopp et al.
[8] developed a rough-wall 푘−휔model using similar methods. These and other approaches to modeling roughness
are reviewed in Durbin [9]. The present approach is to incorporate roughness via an eﬀective origin.

VIII. Discussion
The idea of accommodating roughness by introducing an eﬀective origin is attractive for its simplicity and has
a physical motivation. The irregular surface is represented by a smooth surface, at a location that depends on the
height of the surface asperities. This artiﬁcial, smooth surface could be regarded as a mean line through the actual,
irregular surface. Alternatively, it has been proposed in the literature that the eﬀective surface should be at the center
of force on the asperities. The requirements of turbulence modeling allow this to be vague. One could describe the
present approach as prescribing the artiﬁcial surface to be at height ℓ, above the bottom of the asperities. However,
that geometrical interpretation is misleading. The eﬀective origin is used to prevent the eddy viscosity from becoming
zero at the lower boundary, because the roughness creates shear layers and turbulence near the surface. ℓis determined
11


## Page 12


by turbulent mixing, not by geometry.
The 푘−휔0 model was developed as a means to avoid computing a singular function. The regular solution for
휔0 is mapped onto a singular function, because the 1/푦2 singularity is an essential property of the 푘−휔model. The
1/푦2 singularity is derived from the balance between dissipation and molecular diﬀusion. That balance is upset by
roughness; roughness introduces turbulence near the wall, overwhelming molecular diﬀusion. The present method of
representing this is to alter the near-wall behavior of 휔to 1/(푦+ℓ)2. Also, 푘is no longer 0 at the wall. In combination,
the model is able to extrapolate from smooth to fully rough conditions. The roughness modiﬁcation is developed for
two possible 휔0-boundary conditions. The 휔0(0) = 0 variant oﬀers a simpler implementation.
In experiments, a virtual origin is needed to ﬁt measurements of 푈to the log-law. This is distinct from the eﬀective
origin, added to the turbulence model. The virtual origin is determined empirically. In the present formulation, it is
possible to derive a formula for the virtual origin, under fully rough conditions.
