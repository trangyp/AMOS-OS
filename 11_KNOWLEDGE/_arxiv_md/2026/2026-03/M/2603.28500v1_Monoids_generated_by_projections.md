Abstract
We define and explore semireflection monoids on a finite-dimensional vector space. These are
monoids generated by semireflections: linear maps fixing a subspace of codimension 1. We mostly
focus on the case of projection monoids (where the generating semireflections are non-invertible).
After exploring some general theory, we give some important examples, and give classification
results for projection monoids on C2 and R3. We then briefly introduce affine projection monoids.

Introduction
The theory of finite reflection groups (both real and complex) is one of the cornerstones of twentieth-
century mathematics, with applications in numerous areas, and continues to inspire a great deal of
research. We begin by summarising the essentials.
Throughout this paper we let V be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field F. We define a
reflection on V to be an invertible linear map r : V →V whose fixed-point space has codimension 1
(or equivalently, for which the space {(r −1)v | v ∈V} has dimension 1). A reflection group on V is
a subgroup of GL(V) generated by reflections. We are interested primarily in finite reflection groups.
Obviously in a finite reflection group each reflection r has finite order, so has eigenvalues 1, . . . ,1, ζ
for a root of unity ζ ∈F. Moreover, if char(F) = 0 then ζ ̸= 1, so that r is diagonalisable. (What we
have called a reflection is often called a pseudoreflection, with the term ‘reflection’ being reserved for
the case where ζ = −1.)
A finite reflection group G on V is irreducible if there is no subspace 0 < W < V which is preserved
by G. It is completely reducible if for every W1 ⩽V which is preserved by G there is another subspace
W2 which is preserved by G such that V = W1 ⊕W2. In this case G decomposes as a direct product
G1 × G2, where Gi is the pointwise stabiliser of W3−i, and acts as a completely reducible reflection
group on Wi. Iterating this decomposition shows that for any completely reducible finite reflection
group G on V there is a direct sum decomposition V = W1 ⊕· · · ⊕Wr and a corresponding direct
product decomposition G = G1 × · · · × Gr, where Gi acts as an irreducible reflection group on Vi and
fixes Vj pointwise for each j ̸= i.
It is essentially a consequence of Maschke’s Theorem that if char(F) = 0 then every finite reflection
group is completely reducible, so to classify finite reflection groups it suffices to classify irreducibles.
Over R this was done by Coxeter [C1], who showed [C2] that the irreducible finite real reflection
groups are precisely the finite Coxeter groups. Over C the irreducible finite reflection groups were
classified by Shephard and Todd [ST]; these consist of the infinite family of groups G(m, p, n), to-
gether with 34 exceptional groups G4, . . . , G37. Finite reflection groups in prime characteristic have
been considered in a series of papers by Zaleskii and Serezhkin [ZS1, ZS2, ZS3].
Now we introduce the generalisations of reflection groups that we study in this paper. Suppose
V is a finite-dimensional vector space over F. We define a semireflection to be a (not necessarily invert-
ible) linear map r : V →V whose fixed-point space has codimension 1. So a semireflection is either
a reflection or a projection onto a subspace of dimension n −1. For the rest of this paper, we use
the term ‘projection’ to mean a projection onto a subspace of codimension 1; or in other words, an
idempotent linear map of nullity 1.
We define a semireflection monoid on V to be a submonoid of EndF(V) generated by semireflections.
A semireflection monoid is a projection monoid if it is generated by projections. We will mainly restrict
attention to projection monoids in this paper; we are particularly interested in finding finite projection
monoids. We will concentrate on irreducibility and classification, leaving the study of the algebraic
structure of these monoids to future work.
In the next section we will explore some basic theory of semireflection monoids and projection
monoids, particularly relating to irreducibility and complete reducibility. In Section 3 we introduce
two important examples and give come of their properties. In Sections 4 and 5 we classify irreducible
projection monoids on C2 and on R3, and in the final section we introduce affine projection monoids.

If r : V →V is a semireflection, then so is the dual map r∗: V∗→V∗. So if M is a semireflection
monoid on V, then there is a dual semireflection monoid M∗on V∗. Unlike in the case of finite
reflection groups, a finite semireflection monoid need not be equivalent (or even isomorphic an an
abstract monoid) to its dual.
Now we show how to decompose a semireflection monoid into a ‘reflection part’ and a ‘projection
part’. Suppose M is a semireflection monoid. Then we define M0 to be the submonoid generated by
all the projections in M, and M1 the submonoid generated by the reflections in M. Then M1 is simply
the set of invertible elements of M and is a reflection group. Moreover, M0 ∩M1 = {1}, M0M1 = M,
and M1 normalises M0. (In many cases M0 comprises all the non-invertible elements of M, but this
is not true in general.)
Given a projection monoid M0, we define its normalising reflection group to be the group generated
by all the reflections that normalise M0. Now to construct a finite semireflection monoid, we can
proceed in stages:
⋄construct a finite projection monoid M0;
⋄let G be the normalising reflection group of M0;
⋄let M1 be a finite subgroup of G, and let M be the semireflection monoid M0M1.
In this paper we will mainly be concerned with the first step, though for many of the projection
monoids that we construct we will find the normalising reflection group.
We remark that one can consider instead projection semigroups instead of projection monoids, with
no practical difference; a projection semigroup is the same as the corresponding projection monoid
but with the identity element removed. The choice is largely a matter of taste; our choice to work with
monoids simplifies some statements (e.g. the expression M0M1 above would need to be M0M1 ∪M1
when working with semigroups, and references to direct products below would need modification)
while complicating others (in Section 3 below we repeatedly make reference to the non-invertible
elements of a projection monoid, which are simply the elements of the corresponding projection
semigroup).
2.2
Irreducibility and complete reducibility
Suppose M is a projection monoid on V. We say that M is reducible if there is a subspace 0 < W < V
which is mapped to itself by all elements of M. We call such a subspace invariant for M. We say that
M is irreducible if it is not reducible.
We begin with some basic theory which parallels the corresponding theory for reflection groups.
We leave the proof of the following simple lemma to the reader.
Lemma 2.1. Suppose M is a projection monoid on V, and 0 ⩽W ⩽V. Then W is invariant for M if
and only if for every projection p ∈M either ker(p) ⩽W or W ⩽im(p).
This lemma allows us to further examine the structure of a reducible projection monoid. Suppose
M is a projection monoid and W is an invariant subspace for M. For each projection p ∈M, we
consider the two possibilities in Lemma 2.1. If ker(p) ⩽W, then p yields a projection on W and the
identity on V/W; on the other hand, if W ⩽im(p), then p acts as the identity on W and yields a
projection on V/W.
So we naturally obtain a projection monoid MW on W, generated by the restrictions to W of the
projections in M, and a projection monoid MV/W on V/W, generated by the projections induced by
the projections in M that fix W. The direct product MW × MV/W is naturally a quotient of M.
Now say that M is a completely reducible if for every invariant subspace W there is another invari-
ant subspace X with V = W ⊕X, or equivalently, if the sum of the minimal invariant subspaces for
M is V.

In the case where V is the direct sum W1 ⊕W2 of two invariant subspaces, we can refine the above
discussion. By Lemma 2.1 we can partition the set of projections in M as P1 ⊔P2, where ker(p) ⩽Wi
and W3−i ⩽im(p) for each p ∈Pi. Then each projection in P1 commutes with each projection in P2.
If we let Mi be the monoid generated by Pi for i = 1,2, then every m ∈Mi fixes W3−i pointwise. So
M1 ∩M2 = {1} and M ∼= M1 × M2. Each p ∈Pi acts as a projection on Wi, so we can view Mi as a
projection monoid on Wi. If M is completely reducible, then so is Mi (as a projection monoid on Wi).
Iterating this, we find that if M is a completely reducible projection monoid on V, then there is a
direct sum decomposition V = V1 ⊕· · · ⊕Vr and a direct product decomposition M = M1 × · · · × Mr,
where each Mi is an irreducible projection monoid on Vi (and acts as the identity on Vj for each j ̸= i).
So to classify completely reducible projection monoids, it suffices to classify irreducible ones.
Example. In this example we describe all reducible projection monoids in dimension 2 (for any F). As
a consequence, we observe that (in contrast to the situation with reflection groups) finite projection
monoids need not be completely reducible in characteristic 0.
Suppose dim(V) = 2 and let L be any line in V. We consider the projection monoids on V having
L as an invariant subspace. By Lemma 2.1 any projection p in such a projection monoid must have L
as an eigenspace (that is, L must be either the kernel or the image of p).
Let P be any finite set of projections all having L as an eigenspace, and let M be the monoid
generated by P. Then we claim that M is finite. To see this, suppose p, q ∈P, and observe that:
⋄if L = ker(p) = ker(q), then pq = q;
⋄if L = im(p) = im(q), then pq = p;
⋄if L = im(p) = ker(q), then pq = 0.
As a consequence, any expression in the elements of P reduces to an expression of length at most 2
or to 0, so M is finite.
M is reducible (because L is invariant for M), but generally not completely reducible.
2.3
Images and kernels
In this subsection we describe necessary and sufficient conditions for complete reducibility of a
projection monoid M; here the theory diverges from the theory of finite reflection groups.
First we set out some definitions which will be used throughout the rest of the paper. Suppose
M is a projection monoid on V. Given subspaces K, L < V with dim(K) = codim(L) = 1 and with
K ⩽̸ L, we write prj(K, L) for the projection with kernel K and image L. We Say that a line K is a kernel
of M if prj(K, L) ∈M for some L, and we say that a hyperplane L is an image of M if prj(K, L) ∈M
for some K. We write Ker(M) for the set of kernels of M, and Im(M) for the set of images of M.
We say that M is complete if the following condition holds: whenever K ∈Ker(M) and L ∈Im(M)
with K ⩽̸ L, the projection prj(K, L) lies in M.
Now we can give the main theorem of this section. We define the trivial projection monoid on
V to be the monoid containing just the identity element; clearly this is irreducible if and only if
dim(V) = 1.
Theorem 2.2. Suppose M is a non-trivial finite irreducible projection monoid on V. Then:
⋄the sum of the kernels of M is V;
⋄the intersection of the images of M is 0; and
⋄M is complete.

We end this section by considering semireflection monoids and complete reducibility. Recall that
for a semireflection monoid M we define the projection submonoid M0 and the reflection subgroup M1.
Corollary 2.5. Suppose M is a finite completely reducible semireflection monoid on V. Then M0 is
completely reducible.
Proof. It suffices to prove that V is the sum of its minimal M0-invariant subspaces. Since M is com-
pletely reducible, V is the sum of its minimal M-invariant subspaces. So we just need to show that
any minimal M-invariant subspace W is a sum of minimal M0-invariant subspaces. Certainly W is
M0-invariant, so it contains a minimal M0-invariant subspace U. Because M1 normalises M0, the set
of M0-invariant subspaces is preserved by the reflection group M1, so the images of U under M1
are also minimal M0-invariant subspaces. So the sum X of these images is both M0-invariant and
M1-invariant, so is M-invariant. Clearly X ⩽W, because W is M1-invariant. So the minimality of W
means that W = X, and therefore W is a sum of minimal M0-invariant subspaces.

4.1
The trace group
In this subsection we work over an arbitrary field F. Suppose M is a projection monoid on F2,
take two non-identity elements p, q ∈M, and suppose im(p) ̸= ker(q). Then pq equals Tr(pq) times
the projection prj(ker(q),im(p)). So if a ∈N, then (pq)a equals Tr(pq)a times prj(ker(q),im(p)). Since
M is finite, this means that Tr(pq) is zero or a root of unity in F; we will call this the “trace condition”.
If we define the trace group of M to be the group G generated by all the non-zero traces of the non-
invertible elements of M, then G is a finite subgroup of F×.
The next result shows how the trace group enables us to find the cardinality of an irreducible
projection monoid on F2.
Proposition 4.1. Suppose M is an irreducible projection monoid on F2. Then the number of non-
zero elements in M is kl|G| + 1, where k = |Ker(M)|, l = |Im(M)| and G is the trace group of M.
Furthermore, 0 is an element of M if and only if Ker(M) ∩Im(M) ̸= ∅.
Proof. We prove two claims.
Claim 1. If K ∈Ker(M) and L ∈Im(M), then there is an element of M with kernel K and
image L.
Proof. In the case where K ̸= L this follows from Lemma 2.2: M must contain prj(K, L). Even
in the case where K = L, the argument from the proof of Lemma 2.2 still works to show the
existence of an element with kernel and image both equal to K.
Claim 2. If m is a non-zero non-invertible element of M and g ∈F×, then gm ∈M if and only
if g ∈G.
Proof. For each projection p ∈M, let Gp = { g ∈F× | gp ∈M}. Then Gp ⩽G. Since any non-
invertible element of M with non-zero trace t equals t times a projection, the trace group G is
the union of the groups Gp for all projections p ∈M.
To prove the claim, first consider the case where m is a projection; then we want to show that
Gm = G. If Gm < G, then there is an element g ∈G \ Gm and another projection p such that
g ∈Gp. Because M is irreducible, it has at least two images and at least two kernels, so we can
choose a kernel K ̸= im(p) and an image L ̸= ker(m). By Claim 1 we can find an element r ∈M
with kernel K and image L. Similarly, we can find a non-zero element s with ker(s) ̸= im(m)
and im(s) ̸= ker(p). This means that the element mrpsm is non-zero, and hence has image
im(m) and kernel ker(m), so equals hm for some h ∈Gm. Additionally, M contains the element
mr(gp)sm, which equals ghm. But then gh ∈Gm, so that g ∈Gm, a contradiction. So Gm = G.

6
Affine projection monoids
6.1
Definitions
In this final section we broaden our definitions, and consider monoids generated by affine projec-
tions: that is, idempotent affine-linear maps whose image is an affine subspace of V of codimension
1. The projection monoids considered in preceding sections (whose elements are all linear maps) will
be called linear projection monoids in this section. Affine projection monoids are analogous to affine
reflection groups, which play an important role in Lie theory. However, while properly affine reflec-
tion groups (i.e. those which are not equivalent to linear reflection groups) are necessarily infinite
in characteristic 0, properly affine projection monoids can be finite. In this section we will mainly
restrict attention to definitions and examples, leaving a more comprehensive exploration of affine
projection monoids to a future paper.
We start by setting out some definitions and basic theory. If p : V →V is an affine projection,
then its image im(p) is an affine subspace of V. We abuse notation and define the kernel of p to be
ker(p) = { p(v) −v | v ∈V}. Thus ker(p) is a (linear) subspace of V, of dimension 1 (but is not the
kernel in the usual sense of being the preimage of the zero vector under p).

If m : V →V is an affine-linear map, then we can define a linear map m : V →V by m(v) =
m(v) −m(0). Clearly if p is an affine projection then p is a linear projection: the image im(p) is the
linear subspace obtained as a translation of im(p), and the kernel ker(p) equals ker(p).
For any affine-linear maps l, m, it is clear that lm = lm. Thus if M is an affine projection monoid,
then the monoid M = {m | m ∈M} is a linear projection monoid, naturally isomorphic to a quotient
of M. We call M the linear projection monoid underlying M.
The notion of completeness introduced in Section 2.2 naturally extends to affine projection mon-
oids: M is complete if whenever K is a kernel of M and L is an image of M such that K + L = V (or
equivalently, K is not contained in any translate of L) the affine projection prj(K, L) lies in M. It is
easy to see that if M is complete, then so is M; a partial converse is given by following.
Proposition 6.1. Suppose M is an affine projection monoid, and that the underlying linear projection
monoid M is complete. Suppose that one of the following holds.
1. M does not have two parallel images.
2. char(F) = 0 and M is finite.
Then M is complete.
Proof. First suppose (1) holds. Suppose K is a kernel of M and L an image of M with K + L = V, and
let L be the linear subspace obtained by translating L. Then prj(K, L) ∈M, because M is complete,
which means that M must contain an affine projection with kernel K and image parallel to L. But (1)
tells us that the only image parallel to L is L itself, so prj(K, L) lies in M.
Now suppose (2) holds. First we note that if p, q are projections in M with distinct but parallel
images, then ker(p) = ker(q). To see this, suppose that the kernels are distinct, and consider the
effect of pq on im(p). Because im(p) and im(q) are parallel and distinct, there is a non-zero vector
w ∈ker(q) such that q(v) = v + w for all v ∈im(p). Similarly, there is a non-zero vector x ∈ker(p)
such that p(v) = v + x for all x ∈im(q). So pq acts on im(p) as translation by w + x. The assumption
that ker(p) and ker(q) are not parallel means that w and x are linearly independent, so that w + x ̸= 0.
So pq acts on im(p) by a non-zero translation, so has infinite order, contradicting (2).
Now suppose that K is a kernel of M and L an image of M with K + L = V. Then, as in the first
part of the proof, M must contain prj(K, L′) for some L′ parallel to L. If L′ = L, then we have the
desired projection prj(K, L) in M. So suppose L′ ̸= L. Let p be a projection in M with image L. Then
from the paragraph above, ker(p) = K; so p = prj(K, L) ∈M.
We extend the notion of irreducibility to affine projection monoids: M is irreducible if there is no
proper affine subspace of dimension greater than 0 which is mapped to itself by every element of M.
(Note that if there is an invariant affine subspace of dimension 0, i.e. a point fixed by every element
of M, then M is equivalent to a linear projection monoid.)
It is clear that if the underlying linear projection monoid M is irreducible, then so is M. However,
the converse need not hold. For example, take V of dimension 2, and let K and L be any two (linear)
lines in V. Choose affine lines L1, . . . , Lr (with r ⩾2) parallel to L, and let J1, . . . , Js be any affine lines
not parallel to L. Let M be the affine projection monoid generated by
⋄the projections prj(K, Li) for i = 1, . . . ,r, and
⋄the projections prj(L, Ji) for i = 1, . . . , s.
Then it is easy to check that M is finite, and it is irreducible (unless s = 1 and J1 is parallel to K). But
the underlying projection monoid M is reducible, because it has L as an invariant subspace. Note
also that (provided at least one of J1, . . . , Js is not parallel to K) M is not complete, so we do not have
an analogue of Lemma 2.2 for affine projection monoids.
