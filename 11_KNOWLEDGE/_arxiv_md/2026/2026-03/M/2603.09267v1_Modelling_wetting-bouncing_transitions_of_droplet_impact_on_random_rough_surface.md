Abstract
Droplet impact on rough surfaces is of critical importance to various appli-
cations, yet remains incompletely understood. The present work aims to un-
cover droplet impact dynamics on random hydrophobic surfaces using volume
of fluid simulations. Random fractal surfaces with RMS roughness ranging
from 2 to 50 µm were generated using the Weierstrass–Mandelbrot function.
Three identifiable impact outcomes including no bouncing, complete bounc-
ing, and bouncing with breakup have been identified as Weber number varies
between 5.7 and 12.9 and RMS roughness varies between 0 and 50 µm. We
examine the spreading, retraction, re-spreading, and breakup stages of the
impact events under different velocity and surface morphologies conditions.
Numerical simulations show that the maximum spreading factor decreases
linearly as surface roughness increases. Two scaling laws have been proposed
for droplet impact on surfaces with small and large roughness values, re-

1. Introduction
Droplet impact is ubiquitous in a wide range of applications, e.g., inkjet
printing [1], spray cooling [2], coating [3], integrated circuits [4] and binder jet
3D printing [5]. Within the past decades, droplet impact dynamics have been
investigated extensively, and different impact outcomes, for instance, droplet
deposition, partial or total rebound, receding breakup, and splashing have
been reported to elucidate the mechanisms of impact phenomena [6, 7, 8, 9].
Droplet impact dynamics are governed by droplet properties (e.g., density,
viscosity, and surface tension), surrounding air characteristics, impact pa-
rameters (e.g., impact velocity and angle) and surface properties that govern
droplet wettability [6, 10, 11, 12]. Focus on surface morphologies as a re-
search gap. For instance, a recent study has shown that wettability on rough
surfaces affects the dynamic contact angle of droplets; namely, the maximum
dynamic contact angle of an impacting droplet increases with an increase
of the surface roughness at the micrometer scale [13]. Gao et al. (2025)

conducted two-dimensional simulations to understand how surface skewness
and kurtosis affect droplet impact behaviour [15]. They pointed out that a
droplet impacting on a rough surface with skewness = 0.2 and kurtosis = 3
shows superior rebound performance, and, notably, the overall droplet con-
tact time on such a rough surface shows a reduction by 16.15% compared to
that on a smooth surface.

U0 is the impact velocity, D0 the droplet diameter, and ρ, µ and σ are
droplet density, dynamic viscosity and surface tension coefficient, respec-
tively. Spreading dynamics of high-viscosity droplets have been found to be
mainly affected by the Reynolds number Re, as the viscous dissipation is
dominant in this scenario [14]. An impacting low-viscosity droplet spreads
and reaches its maximum spreading diameter Dm. Then it enters a phase
of repeated receding and spreading until it finally sits on a substrate with
an equilibrium contact angle θ0 [16]. The maximum spreading diameter is
of great importance to control the printing resolution and to minimize the
void defects in inkjet printing technologies [1]. A low-viscosity droplet im-
pacting on a superhydrophobic surface has been investigated experimentally
in the range of 2 < We < 900, and it shows that the maximum spread-
ing diameter Dm scales as D0We1/4 [17]. A crossover regime between the
viscous and capillary regimes has been proposed for droplet impacting on
hydrophobic surfaces, and the scaling law Dm/D0 ∼Re1/5f(WeRe−2/5) has
been validated by experimental and numerical results [18, 19]. Liu et al.

quite different from the scenario when impacting on a patterned surface with
the same surface roughness [25]. The root-mean-square (RMS) roughness
has negligible influence on droplet splashing velocity when the roughness is
minor (< 3 −4 µm), and the splashing velocity decreases with increasing
roughness when the roughness is larger than the above threshold [26]. Bao
et al. (2023) experimentally investigated the influence of the random sur-

found that the wetting-bouncing transition can be triggered by increasing
We [9]. Wang et al. (2024) investigated droplet dynamics when impact-

and they demonstrated that a higher surface roughness produces a thicker
liquid film on a rough surface [27]. Tong et al. (2025) found that the critical
We required for a transition from droplet complete rebound to fragmenta-
tion splashing decreases monotonically with increasing surface roughness [28].
Various roughness parameters have been utilized to study the effect of surface
roughness on droplet impact dynamics, for instance, the root-mean-square
roughness (Rq), the arithmetic average roughness (Ra), the maximum height
of the profile (Rz), the average peak to peak feature size (Rp), and the mean
width of the surface feature (Rm) [9, 13, 27]. Roisman et al. (2015) found
that the absolute length scales of the substrate roughness, e.g., Ra and Rz,
have negligible influence on the splash threshold [12].
In the aforementioned experimental and numerical investigations, droplet
dynamics when impacting on smooth, patterned, tailored rough, and random
rough surfaces have been extensively investigated. It is clear that surface
morphology, micro-structures, and roughness affect droplet dynamics. How-

3.2. Droplet impact dynamics
The impact dynamics during droplet spreading and receding phases are
discussed in this section. For each given Weber number, we investigate the
influence of surface roughness on the droplet spreading factor. As shown in
Figure 3, we present the temporal evolution of the spreading factor up to
12 ms. Spreading factors of seven cases with different surface roughness at
a given We gradually increase, and then reach their maximum spearing fac-

leads to a slower increase in the spreading factor during the spreading phase.

phase. As We continues to increase, more kinetic energy is transferred from

droplets during their receding phase, as shown in Figure 3c. This scenario
evidences that droplets completely rebound from surfaces.

in dissipating kinetic energy and suppressing the recovery of droplet shape.
The complete bouncing regime is confined to a band of moderate Weber
numbers (6.5 ≤We ≤11.2) and low-to-moderate surface roughness (Rq ≤
20). In this region, droplet inertia is sufficient to overcome adhesion and
viscous dissipation, but not so high as to induce interfacial breakup. The
onset of breakup defines the upper boundary of this regime, while increased
roughness values suppress its lower boundary. The bouncing with breakup
regime emerges at high Weber numbers (We ≥11.2) and on surfaces with low
roughness (Rq ≤20). At a given high We (e.g., We=11.2), increasing surface
roughness appears to stabilise the droplet, and thus leads to a transition
from bouncing with breakup to complete bouncing. It implies that bouncing
with breakup requires two concurrent conditions. First, a moderately high

4. Conclusions
In this work, volume of fluid simulations have been employed to under-
stand how surface roughness affects droplet impact dynamics on hydrophobic
surfaces with an equilibrium contact angle of 100◦. By varying the root-mean-
square roughness Rq and Weber number We, we examined 35 numerical
cases. The impact outcomes of the 35 cases have been classified into three
categories: no bouncing, complete bouncing, and bouncing with breakup.
The main conclusions and contributions are summarized as follows:
(1) Droplets spread more slowly on rough surfaces than on smooth ones,
as evidenced by the temporal evolution of spreading factors. Re-spreading
appears during the receding phase for droplet impact on rough surfaces with

Rq ≥30 µm at each given Weber number ranging from 5.7 to 12.9. The
maximum spreading factors βm exhibit approximately linear reduction as Rq
increases from 0 to 50 µm. βm = 1.14(1.38 + We)0.46 and βm = 1.11(1.55 +
We)0.40 have been proposed for slightly rough surfaces (Rq ≤20 µm) and
rough surfaces (Rq ≥30 µm), respectively.
(2) Surprisingly, droplet contact time τc on hydrophobic surfaces has been
proved to be independent of both Weber number and RMS roughness, and

(3) The wetting-bouncing transition is triggered by increasing We and
decreasing Rq. Droplet impact on rough surfaces (Rq > 10 µm) results in
larger triple contact line length as compared to impact on smooth surfaces.
On smooth surfaces, droplets manage to maintain their spherical triple con-
tact line, whereas on rough surfaces, the triple contact line becomes more
irregular, as evidenced by decreased circularities.
(4) Additionally, as Rq increases, more air bubbles are entrapped inside
droplets and thus significantly affect droplet wetting dynamics. Visualiza-
tions of internal flow and velocity vectors during droplet impact uncover that
the velocity field around triple contact line shows disordered asymmetry when
impact on rough surfaces. Random surface morphology significantly alters
internal flow dynamics and induces instabilities, especially when impact on
surfaces with larger Rq.
The wetting-bouncing transition identifies surface morphology as another
dominant factor governing droplet impact outcomes, providing a simple yet
efficient way to manipulate droplet impact modes by tailoring surface mor-
phologies. The linear scaling of maximum spreading factor with RMS rough-

ness serves as a simple predictive tool for droplet spreading dynamics on
both ideally smooth surfaces and real-world rough substrates. The invari-

water-repellent and anti-icing surfaces, optimizing spray coating, and regu-

between surface morphology, internal flow dynamics, and macroscopic im-
pact behavior, this work advances a foundational framework for the optimal
design of surfaces with tailored wetting and bouncing characteristics, relevant
to a wide range of colloidal and interfacial applications.
In future work, droplet impact on superhydrophobic surfaces will be of
particular interest. Special attention will be given to droplet dynamics on
moving surfaces, whether in translational or rotational motion.
