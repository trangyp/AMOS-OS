ABSTRACT
AstroSat/LAXPC and SXT observed the persistent neutron star low-mass X-ray binary 4U 1820-
30 between 2016 and 2022.
During these observations, the hardness-intensity diagram (HID) and
color-color diagram (CCD) indicated that the source was in the banana state. We divided the CCD
into 11 segments for spectral and timing analyses. For each segment in the CCD, we modeled the
spectral data using two distinct approaches over the 0.7-20.0 keV band. A combination of a multi-
color-disk component with an inner disk temperature of around 0.6 keV and Comptonized emission
from the boundary layer (BL)/ hot corona provided the best description of the X-ray spectral data of
this source. The truncation radius was found to be in the range of ∼19-40 km. The Comptonized
component has an optical depth in the range of ∼7 −13 with electron temperature in the range of
∼2.5 −3.8 keV. The optical depth of the corona varies significantly along the position on the CCD,
while ∼80% of the X-ray flux comes from the Comptonized component. We discuss possible physical
scenarios to explain the relationship between the spectral evolution and motion of the source along
the CCD. The timing analysis revealed kHz QPOs peaks at ∼710 Hz and ∼740 Hz in the lower left
banana branch. An energy-dependent study indicates that these QPOs are stronger in the high-energy
band.
Keywords: High energy astrophysics – Low mass X-ray binary – Accretion, accretion disk – Neutron
star X-ray binary: individual: 4U 1820-30
1. INTRODUCTION
A neutron star (NS) accretes matter from a low-mass (≤1M⊙) companion star via Roche-lobe overflow in NS low-
mass X-ray binaries (LMXRBs), and the accreted material forms an accretion disk (Bahramian & Degenaar 2022).
NS-LMXRBs host a weakly magnetized (B ≤108−9G) NS and provide ideal laboratories to understand the physical
behavior of accretion processes in the vicinity of a dense compact object and in the strong gravity regime.
NS-
LMXRBs are categorized into two classes: Z-type and atoll-type sources, based on their X-ray spectral and temporal
characteristics as well as the pattern they trace in their color-color diagram (CCD) or hardness-intensity diagram
(HID) (Hasinger & van der Klis 1989; Di Salvo et al. 2023). Z-type and atoll-type sources differ in luminosity due to
their varied accretion rates (Lin et al. 2009; Homan et al. 2010; Ng et al. 2024). Z-type sources are the most luminous
NS-LMXRBs, and sometimes they radiate at a rate close to the Eddington luminosity (LEdd), whereas atoll-type
sources are generally less bright (∼0.001 −0.5 LEdd) than Z-type sources (Done et al. 2007). Based on luminosity,

atoll-type sources are further classified into two categories: ordinary atoll sources (0.01-0.3 LEdd) and bright atoll
sources (0.3-0.5 LEdd) (Yao et al. 2021). The spectral states of atoll sources are broadly categorized into: hard state,
termed as the ‘extreme island state’ (EIS), the intermediate state, referred to as the ‘island state’ (IS), and the soft
state, designated as the ‘banana state’ (BS) (van der Klis 2010). BS is further subdivided into the lower banana (LB),
lower-left banana (LLB), and upper banana (UB) branches (Wang 2016). Atoll-type sources exhibit a “C-shaped”
track in the CCD/HID and move along this track on timescales of days to months (Hasinger & van der Klis 1989;
van der Klis 2010; Fridriksson et al. 2015; Wang 2016). Along this track, the mass accretion rate ( ˙m) increases as the
source moves from the IS to the UB state (Falanga et al. 2006).
In the IS state, sources exhibit lower luminosities with harder spectra, while in the BS they show higher luminosities
with softer spectra (Barret 2001). The X-ray spectra of these sources have two major components: a soft thermal
component and a hard Comptonized component. To model the X-ray spectra of atoll sources, various approaches
have been proposed in the literature. According to one viewpoint, the hard Comptonized component originates from
the boundary layer (BL), while the soft thermal emission component is characterized as a multi-color disk (MCD)
blackbody that originates from the cold accretion disk (Mitsuda et al. 1984; Barret 2001; Di Salvo et al. 2002; Agrawal
& Sreekumar 2003; Agrawal & Misra 2009; Agrawal et al. 2018). In an alternative approach, the emission component
originating from a hot inner disk is responsible for the hard Comptonized component, and the BL radiates a soft
single-temperature blackbody (BB) (Di Salvo et al. 2001; Sleator et al. 2016). In certain cases, the X-ray spectra can
be modeled using two thermal components: one from the accretion disk (MCD) and one from the BL (BB) (Lin et al.
2007, 2010; Agrawal et al. 2018).
NS-LMXRBs show fast time variability features in their power density spectrum (PDS) (Di Salvo et al. 2023). They
exhibit two types of noise features in the PDS: a very low-frequency noise (VLFN) component modeled using a power
law function and a band-limited noise (BLN) component modeled using a Lorentzian function. If the BLN centroid
frequency is > 10 Hz, it is called a high-frequency noise (HFN); otherwise, it is called low-frequency noise (LFN)
(Jonker et al. 1998, 2000; Homan et al. 2002; Agrawal et al. 2020). Occasionally, narrow peak-like features known
as quasi-periodic oscillations (QPOs) are also observed in the PDS (Strohmayer et al. 1996; van der Klis et al. 1996;
Peirano & Méndez 2021). QPOs with frequencies ranging from 1 Hz to 70 Hz are named low-frequency QPOs (LF-
QPOs) (Homan et al. 2015). However, the high-frequency peak (≥200 Hz) is generally termed a kilohertz (kHz) QPO
(van der Klis 2000; Wang 2016). In most atoll-type sources, kHz QPOs are mostly observed in the LLB branch (Wang
2016). kHz QPOs are often detected in pairs referred to as lower and upper kHz QPOs (van der Klis et al. 1997;
Belloni et al. 2002; Wang 2016). Their frequencies vary with ˙m. At low ˙m in atoll sources, the PDS is dominated by
BLN, correlated with the source position on the CCD. In the lowest ˙m regime (IS), the BLN fractional rms is large
(∼10 −20%), falling to ≤2% as ˙m increases, and this is the region on the CCD where kHz QPOs appear (Wijnands
& van der Klis 1999; Psaltis et al. 1999; Di Salvo et al. 2001; Di Salvo et al. 2003). At the highest ˙m, when the kHz
QPOs are no longer observed, VLFN dominates below ∼1 Hz, and BLN components are sometimes detected with a
characteristic frequency of 10–20 Hz (Hasinger & van der Klis 1989; Di Salvo et al. 2001).
4U 1820-30 is a persistent ultra-compact (UC) NS-LMXRB (Giacconi et al. 1974; Stella et al. 1987; Armas Padilla
et al. 2023) consisting of an NS as the primary and a He white dwarf star (0.06−0.08M⊙) as its companion (Rappaport
et al. 1987; Smale et al. 1997). It is located at 0.66′′ from the center of the NGC 6624 globular cluster (Rappaport
et al. 1987; Smale et al. 1997; Shaposhnikov & Titarchuk 2004; Mondal et al. 2016; Di Marco et al. 2023). Grindlay
et al. (1976) confirmed this source as an NS based on the detection of a type-I thermonuclear burst. Stella et al. (1987)
determined its orbital period to be 11.4 min, the shortest orbital period known for any XRB. This source is classified
as a bright atoll-type source (Hasinger & van der Klis 1989; Tarana et al. 2007; Iaria et al. 2020), which mainly exhibits
BS, with occasional transitions to IS (Tarana et al. 2007). This source also shows a 170-day super-orbital accretion
cycle. 4U 1820-30 also shows an intrinsic luminosity modulation, possibly due to the tidal effects of a third object. As
a result, 4U 1820-30 oscillates between low and high luminosity modes, which have distinct spectral features connected
with the variation of accretion flow (Marino et al. 2023; Chou & Jhang 2023). Güver et al. (2010) estimated the mass
and radius of the NS at nearly ∼1.6M⊙and 9.11 km, respectively. Anderson et al. (1997) estimated the inclination
angle in the range of 35◦−50◦. Baumgardt & Vasiliev (2021) reported the distance of the source as D = 8.0 ± 0.1 kpc
using GAIA EDR3.

The observation of 4U 1820-30 by the Rossi X-ray Timing Explorer (RXTE) has revealed the presence of two simul-
taneous kHz QPOs (Smale et al. 1997). Zhang et al. (1998) observed the highest QPO frequency at 1060 ± 20 Hz,
indicating that the disk extends close to the marginally stable orbit. Later, Titarchuk et al. (2013) observed LF-QPOs,
BLN, VLFN, and high-frequency QPOs in this source using RXTE. Interestingly, LF-QPO around 6 Hz was occasion-
ally observed in this source during BS, similar to the normal branch oscillation in the Z-type sources (Wijnands et al.
1999; Titarchuk et al. 2013). Numerous studies have investigated the spectral behavior of 4U 1820-30 with different
X-ray instruments (Stella et al. 1987; Smale et al. 1994; Christian & Swank 1997; Piraino et al. 1999; Kaaret et al.
1999). Titarchuk et al. (2013) found that the photon index (Γ) remains constant at ∼2 during the transition from
BS to IS. They suggested that the stability of Γ is associated with the spectral properties of an accreting NS source.
Based on spectral analysis using NuSTAR, Swift, and Suzaku data, Mondal et al. (2016) suggested that seed photons
from the geometrically thin accretion disk are Comptonized by the BL region, which covers a large portion of the
NS surface. Marino et al. (2023) found stability in disk parameters and substantial evolution in the Comptonization
component using spectral analysis of NuSTAR, NICER, and AstroSat data. Their results indicated that a varying
region that provides the seed photons for the Comptonization spectrum was responsible for the observed X-ray flux
modulation. 4U 1820-30 is also known as a radio emitter (Migliari et al. 2004; Russell et al. 2021; Marino et al. 2023;
Di Marco et al. 2023). Recently, Di Marco et al. (2023) detected X-ray polarization using IXPE and suggested that
the polarization may occur in an optically thick outflow emanating from the inner part of the accretion disk.
Spectral and temporal studies of the NS-LMXRB 4U 1820-30 have shown variability over different time scales. In this
work, we focused on the investigation of the long-term evolution of spectral and temporal properties along the CCD
using available AstroSat data. The paper is organized as follows. In section 2, we provide observational details and
data reduction techniques. In section 3, we present the analysis and modeling methods of the temporal and spectral
data. In section 4, we present the results. In section 5, we interpret and discuss our results and conclude in the last
section.

2. OBSERVATIONAL DETAILS AND DATA REDUCTION
AstroSat is the first Indian multi-wavelength satellite (Agrawal 2006; Singh 2016) with four co-aligned instruments
that can study astrophysical sources from ultraviolet to hard X-rays. The broadband X-ray energy range of 0.3-80.0
keV is covered by the Large Area X-ray Proportional Counter (LAXPC; (Yadav et al. 2016; Antia et al. 2017)) and
the Soft X-ray Telescope (SXT; (Singh et al. 2017)). AstroSat conducted 10 pointed observations (termed Observation
ID) of the source 4U 1820-30 between 2016-20221. We carried out a detailed analysis using the data collected during
these observations, whose details are listed in Table 1. Simultaneous observational data from LAXPC and SXT are

2.1. SXT
SXT is capable of observing the X-ray sources in the energy range of 0.3-8.0 keV. The effective area of SXT is 90 cm2
at 1.5 keV, and it has a focal length of 2 meters. SXT has observed the source in photon counting (PC) mode and
fast window (FW) mode. The readout time is 2.37 seconds and 278 ms for PC mode and FW mode, respectively. We
have considered level-2 data. Using SXTMerger.jl2 package, we produced an exposure-corrected merged clean event
file. Utilizing this event file, we first extracted the source image. In the PC mode, if the source count rate is greater
than 40 counts/sec, it indicates pile-up. To avoid this pile-up, we extracted a circular region of radius 12 arcmin, and
a region of radius 4 arcmin was excluded from the center of the image. For FW mode, we considered a circular region
of 12 arcmin to extract the spectra and light curve. We use background file, and redistribution matrix files (RMF)
provided by the SXT team at the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR) 3. To generate vignetting corrected
ancillary (ARF) files compatible with the source region, an SXT ARF generation tool sxtARFModule4 was used.
2.2. LAXPC
The LAXPC instrument consists of three proportional counter detector units named LAXPC10, LAXPC20, and
LAXPC30 with an effective area of 6000 cm2, which can detect X-rays in the energy range of 3-80 keV. We have used
the data only from LAXPC20 because LAXPC10 and LAXPC30 showed anomalous behavior, including low gain and
gas leakage. Using the laxpcsoftv3.4.3_07May2022 5, we reduced the Level 1 data to generate Level 2 products.
Subsequently, source light curves, background light curves and energy spectra were extracted using standard routines

3. ANALYSIS
3.1. Light curve and CCD
We have plotted MAXI count rate (2-20 keV) versus the hardness ratio to build the HID, as shown in Figure 1. The
hardness ratio is defined as the ratio between Swift/BAT count rate (15-50 keV) and MAXI 6 count rate (2-4 keV).
The AstroSat observations are highlighted as colored points. From Figure 1, it is clear that all AstroSat observations
used in this work belong to the BS. We extracted the background-subtracted light curve from LAXPC20 event mode
data in the 3.0-50.0 keV energy band for each epoch with a bin size of 256 sec. Epoch A has an average count rate of
∼754 cts/s, Epoch B ∼842 cts/s, and Epoch C ∼956 cts/s. We have created the CCD using background-subtracted
lightcurves in the 3.0-5.0 keV, 5.0-7.0 keV, and 7.0-20.0 keV energy bands. To show the CCD, we have plotted the
hard color against the soft color. Here, the ratio of the count rates in the energy band 7.0-20.0 keV and 3.0-5.0 keV
is defined as hard color, and the ratio of the count rates in the energy band 5.0-7.0 keV and 3.0-5.0 is defined as soft
color. The light curves and CCD for the three Epochs are shown in Figure 2. To study the spectral and temporal
evolution along the “C”-track, we have divided the track into 11 segments. BA1, BA2, and BA3 correspond to the
Epoch A data, while Epoch B is divided into six segments: BB1, BB2, BB3, BB4, BB5, and BB6. Moreover, Epoch C
is divided into two segments, BC1 and BC2. Each segment is marked in the CCD. For each segment, we have created
the X-ray spectra and PDS, which are used for further spectral and temporal analysis.
3.2. Timing Analysis
First, we have generated the light curves with a bin size of 0.0003 s in the 3.0-50.0 keV energy band. Light curves are
divided into intervals of 8192 bins. For each interval, we created the PDS. The PDS corresponding to each segment of
the CCD is averaged and then rebinned with a factor of 1.05. The binned PDS are normalized to obtain the fractional
rms spectra in the units of (rms/mean)2 Hz−1 (Miyamoto et al. 1991). Dead time-corrected Poisson noise level is
subtracted from all the PDS (Zhang et al. 1995; Agrawal et al. 2018; Agrawal et al. 2020). We have fitted the PDS in
the frequency range 0.8-1500 Hz. The PDS is modeled with a combination of power-law and/or Lorentzian functions.
Thus, we have found the presence of two types of noise features in the PDS: a VLFN modeled using a power-law
function (Aν−β) with index β and normalization A, and a BLN component modeled using the Lorentzian function
defined as (Belloni et al. 2002),

3.3. Spectral Analysis

using ftgrouppha. The grouped spectra were fitted simultaneously using the spectral fitting package XSPEC version
12.12.1 (Arnaud 1996) in the energy range of 0.7-20 keV. For SXT, we have utilized an energy range of 0.7-7 keV and
5–20 keV for LAXPC20, ignoring data beyond 20 keV because of background domination. To account for the response
matrix’s uncertainty, we added 3% systematic during the fitting process (Bhattacharya 2017). We have computed the

We have attempted multi-component phenomenological spectral models to fit the spectra. Initially, we modeled the
spectrum of each segment using Model 1: constant*TBabs*(diskbb+bbodyrad). Here, constant is used to handle
the relative normalization between the two instruments: SXT and LAXPC20. TBabs is an absorption model with the
abundances given by Wilms et al. (2000) and the cross-section as in Verner et al. (1996). For our entire analysis, we
fixed the hydrogen column density (NH) value at ∼0.14×1022cm−2 (Di Marco et al. 2023). We applied the gain fit
command to adjust the gain shift in the SXT. Our Model 1 is the combination of a blackbody emission component
from the BL/NS surface (bbodyrad in XSPEC) and a MCD model (diskbb in XSPEC) (Mitsuda et al. 1984; Makishima
et al. 1986). The best-fit parameters are reported in the Table 3. Then, we replaced the bbodyrad with nthComp. The
nthComp model takes care of the emission from the thermally Comptonized corona (Zdziarski et al. 1996). The model
becomes Model 2: constant*TBabs*(nthcomp+diskbb). The best-fit parameters for the segments corresponding to
Epoch A and Epoch C are tabulated in Table 4. For Epoch B, all parameters are reported in Table 5. The spectra
could be well described by the MCD model along with the thermally Comptonized emission model from the corona,
which is confirmed by the improved χ2 obtained by the Model 2 as compare to Model 1. To gain deeper insight into the
physical properties of the BL/corona, we further replaced nthcomp with the more physically motivated model comptb,
and model becomes Model 3: constant*TBabs*(comptb+diskbb). Here, comptb model comprises two components:
the direct soft seed photons that are not up-scattered, and the Comptonized emission (Farinelli et al. 2008). During
this fitting, the blackbody seed photon index (γ) and bulk eﬀiciency (δ) are kept fixed at 3 and 0, respectively. Since
the logarithm of the illumination parameter (log A) saturated at 8, we kept it fixed at this value. Although both
Model 2 and Model 3 provides comparable reduced χ2 values, we consider Model 3 for all future considerations and
estimation of the physical parameters because it offers insight into the BL geometry and inflow dynamics near the
NS surface. Figure 4 shows the unfolded spectra along with the residuals for segments BA1 and BB1. The best-fit
parameters obtained using Model 3 are listed in Table 6 and Table 7. We did not observe Fe emission line and reflection
features, or Compton hump from any segment.

temperature (kTe), the power-law photon index (Γ), and the seed photon temperature (kTs), and they exhibit certain

5. DISCUSSION
In this work, we performed a detailed long-term spectral and temporal analysis of the atoll source 4U 1820-30 using the
SXT and LAXPC-20 instruments on-board AstroSat. The HID showed that all AstroSat observations of this source


## Page 15


15
are part of BS, as shown in Figure 1. To conduct the spectral and temporal analysis, we have divided the complete set
of data into three different Epochs. During all three Epochs, the source is found in the banana state. Subsequently,
we divided the data set into 11 segments based on the source’s position on the CCD (see right panel of Figure 2). Our
complete observations did not reveal any type-I thermonuclear X-ray bursts. From the fast timing analysis, the PDS
of each segment is modeled by two components: power law and Lorentzian in the 3.0-50.0 keV energy range using
LAXPC 20 data. This PDS are mainly composed of LFN, HFN, and VLFN. In addition to this, the timing analysis
revealed the presence of the kHz QPO (see Figure 3) and its evolution from ∼710 Hz (in BB1) to ∼740 Hz (in BB2).
Broadband spectral data acquired with SXT and LAXPC 20 were fitted with the two widely accepted approaches in
the 0.7-20.0 keV energy range for each segment. The details of spectral analysis are described in section 3.3. Among
these approaches, the spectral data were best described by a combination of the MCD (Mitsuda et al. 1984) and a
thermal Comptonization model (Farinelli et al. 2008). The MCD model explains the emission from the accretion disk,
while the thermal Comptonization model accounts for the emission from the corona/BL. The variation of the spectral
parameters and their association with the kHz QPO properties are also studied.
Based on analysis of the broadband spectra, we found that Γ spans ∼1.72 to 2.38, indicating that 4U 1820-30 was
likely in a BS or soft state. When the source was in the soft state, Titarchuk et al. (2013), Mondal et al. (2016), and
Marino et al. (2023) found a very close Γ value, consistent with our outcomes. Conversely, Agrawal et al. (2018) found
that Γ < 2.0, when the source 4U 1705-44 was in UB. We have found that the electron temperature of the corona
varies between ∼2.58 and 3.83 keV, which is lower than the value when the source was in IS (Titarchuk et al. 2013).
During BS, the value of the electron temperature of this source is consistent with the previously reported results (see,
Titarchuk et al. (2013); Mondal et al. (2016); Di Marco et al. (2023); Marino et al. (2023)). To better understand the
coronal properties, we have estimated the optical thickness of the corona, which has been found to vary from ∼7 to
13 and aligns well with earlier studies (Kaaret et al. 1997; Piraino et al. 1999). The optical thickness of the corona
depicted an increasing trend as the source moved in the BS during three distinct Epochs (see Figure 5). Ultimately, it
was determined that the corona is cool and optically dense by presuming the seed photon to originate from blackbody
radiation at the NS surface. The values kTe and τ are also the same in other atoll-type sources during BS, such as 4U
1608-52 (Bhattacherjee et al. 2024) and 4U 1705-44 (Agrawal et al. 2018).
We found that the inner accretion disk was truncated and ranged from ∼19 km to 40 km. The inner disk temperature
varied between ∼0.54 keV to 0.64 keV, which is very close to the previously reported results (see Marino et al. (2023);
Di Marco et al. (2023); Mondal et al. (2016)). This truncation of the inner rim of the accretion disk may be caused
by the magnetic field strength (Illarionov & Sunyaev 1975). In 4U 1705-44, Agrawal et al. (2018) suggested that an
increase in optical depth along the banana branch is caused as radiation pressure drives material from the accretion
disk onto an extended corona. This interpretation aligns well with our findings. On the other hand, Mondal et al.
(2016); Russell et al. (2021); Marino et al. (2023); Di Marco et al. (2023) suggested the presence of BL between the
inner rim of the accretion disk and the surface of the NS. Agrawal et al. (2018) discussed that as the accretion disk
radius approaches the NS surface, the BL/central corona becomes optically thick. So, we assumed the presence of
an optically thick BL or a cold central corona present in the space between the NS surface and the inner rim of the
accretion disk. For further understanding, we have estimated the thickness and the radial extent of the BL. The
thickness of the BL ranged between ∼7 km to ∼28 km and the radial extent of the BL ranged between ∼16 km and
∼38 km. In addition, we have also observed that nearly 80% of the total flux was dominated by the BL/central corona.
As shown in Figure 5, the variation in physical parameters along the CCD’s position reveals that, in Epoch A, as the
Rin drops, the optical thickness and electron temperature remain constant. The corona is found to be optically thick
(τ ∼13) and cool (kTe ∼2.7 keV). This suggests that the photons undergo a large number of scatterings and hence
it become cooler. On the other hand, as Rin decreases, the thickness of the cold central corona/BL also decreases,
but the radial extent of the BL is very close to the Rin. Similarly, in Epoch C, as Rin slightly decreases, the optical
thickness of the central corona was found to be around 10.2 with an electron temperature of around 2.74 keV. Our
analysis reveals that the BL extends radially close to the accretion disk’s inner edge, except BB1 and BB2 segments.
This may be attributed to variations in magnetic strength, which leads to disk truncation and results in the BL or
central corona cooling down. To understand the cause behind the disc truncation due to the magnetic field associated
with the NS, we further calculated the upper limit Alfven radius (RA ≡(

6. CONCLUSION
In this section, we present the main findings derived from the X-ray analysis of 4U 1820-30 using the combined data
from AstroSat instruments SXT and LAXPC 20.
• A long-term comprehensive study of atoll type NSXRB 4U 1820-30 using AstroSat’s observations was performed.
The source was found in the banana state during these observations.
• The spectral parameters generally align with what has been documented in prior studies. The spectra primarily
involve two components: one originating from the accretion disk and the other from the Comptonization mech-
anism. The spectrum is predominantly influenced by the Comptonized component. As the inner edge of the
accretion disk nears the surface of the NS, the optical thickness of the corona/boundary layer increases. The
primary cause for this truncation of the disk’s inner rim is the magnetic field strength.


## Page 17


17
• Detection of kHz QPO in BB1 and BB2 segments presents fascinating insights. Particularly in the context of the
evolution of kHz QPOs. The radial extent of the boundary layer from spectral observations closely matches the
radius predicted by the RPM model. This finding implies that the region between the inner edge of the accretion
disk and the neutron star’s surface might harbour an optically thick BL or central corona, whose oscillations can
produce these kHz QPOs.
