Abstract
Let ®𝐺= (𝑉, 𝐸+ ∪𝐸−) be a bidirected graph whose underlying undirected graph 𝐺= (𝑉, 𝐸) is 2-edge-connected.
A strongly connected orientation (SCO) is defined as a subset of arcs that contains exactly one of 𝑒+, 𝑒−for every
𝑒∈𝐸and induces a strongly connected subgraph of ®𝐺. Given a family F of proper subsets of 𝑉, we call an SCO
tight if there is exactly one arc entering 𝑈for every 𝑈∈F . We give a polynomial-time algorithm to construct a set
B consisting of tight SCO’s which forms an integral basis for the linear hull of tight SCO’s. That is, B is a linearly
independent subset of tight SCO’s, and every integer vector in the linear hull of tight SCO’s can be written as an
integral combination of B. This extends the main result of Abdi, Conu´ejols, Liu and Silina (IPCO 2025), who gave
a non-constructive proof of the existence of such a basis in an equivalent setting. While the previous proof uses
polyhedral theory, our proof is purely combinatorial and yields a polynomial-time algorithm. As an application of
our algorithm, we show that parity-constrained tight strongly connected orientations can be solved in deterministic
polynomial time. Along the way, we discover appealing connections to the theory of perfect matching lattices.

Our main motivation for studying tight strengthenings is the following conjecture by Woodall [49]. In a digraph, a
dicut is the set of arcs leaving a nonempty proper vertex subset with no incoming arcs, i.e., an arc set of the form 𝛿+(𝑈)

The terminology of bricks and braces is inspired by the theory of matching lattices by Lov´asz [28], and Edmonds,
Pulleyblank and Lov´asz [17]. Interestingly, in a tight dijoin-covered digraft (𝐷, 𝑆𝑡) with |𝑆| = |𝑇|, a subset 𝐽⊆𝐴is a
tight dijoin if and only if it is a perfect matching (see Section 6). So, our definition of braces is identical to the one for
perfect matchings in bipartite graphs [28]. This is not the case for bricks. Nevertheless, we reuse the terminology due
to its crucial role in determining the dimension of the lattice (Theorem 1.10). Similar to the perfect matching setting
[17], we show that the results of any two tight dicut decomposition procedures on a digraft (𝐷, F ) are the same list of
bricks and braces up to the multiplicities of the arcs (Theorem 3.9). In particular, the number of bricks after applying
tight dicut decomposition to (𝐷, F ) is invariant, which we denote as 𝑏(𝐷, F ). The following is our main theorem for
digrafts:
Theorem 1.10. Let (𝐷= (𝑉, 𝐴), F ) be a tight dijoin-covered digraft. Then, we can construct an integral basis B for
lin(J (𝐷, F )) that consists of tight dijoins in polynomial time. Moreover, |B| = |𝐴| −|𝑉𝑡| −𝑏(𝐷, F ) + 2.
From now, we may assume the digraft is either a brick or a brace. The case of braces is identical to the setting of
bipartite matchings so we omit it. We sketch the main steps in our proof on how to deal with bricks. The first step
is to reduce the digraft to robust ones, and then to elementary ones. We present how to construct integral basis for
lin(J (𝐷, F )) in the bottom-up order: elementary, robust, and non-robust digrafts.
Elementary digrafts
For the base case of the proof we define a new class of digrafts.
Definition 1.11 (elementary digraft). A digraft (𝐷, 𝑆𝑡) is elementary if it is tight dijoin-covered and |𝑆𝑡| = |𝑆| −1.
In an elementary digraft, every tight dijoin has a fixed degree on every 𝑣∈𝑉. In particular, when |𝑆| = |𝑇|, a tight
dijoin is a perfect matching. Thus, it includes the case where (𝐷, 𝑆𝑡) is a brace. We generalize the ear decomposition
for bipartite matchings to elementary digrafts (see e.g. [39] Ch 4.1). We build a sequence of elementary digrafts by
removing one ear at a time. The addition of each ear will increase the dimension of the lattice by exactly one, so we will
add an appropriate tight dijoin to the basis. This gives us an algorithm to construct an integral basis for lin(J (𝐷, 𝑆𝑡)).
Non-elementary robust digrafts
An edge cover is a subset of edges that has degree at least 1 on every 𝑣∈𝑉. We

Definition 1.12 (robust digraft). A digraft (𝐷, 𝑆𝑡) is robust if it is tight dijoin-covered and every tight edge cover is a

We now elaborate on the algorithm of finding a good dicut. Recall that if a digraft is not robust, we can use
Theorem 1.14 to find a separating dicut. This dicut will be a starting point of a search for a good dicut. We show that

Abdi, Cornu´ejols, Liu and Silina [2] initiated the study of the lattice of tight
strengthenings. They proved a non-constructive version of Theorem 1.2. Their argument can be summarized as
follows. First, they use the same reduction to the digraft setting (𝐷, F ). They closely work with the polyhedron of
tight dijoins, DIJ(𝐷, F ) := {𝑥∈R𝐴
≥0 : 𝑥(𝛿+(𝑈)) ≥1 ∀dicut 𝑈; 𝑥(𝛿+(𝑈)) = 1 ∀𝑈∈F }, which is given by [31].
They reduce to basic digrafts by contracting tight dicuts. This step is non-constructive. Observe that DIJ(𝐷, F ) of
the basic digraft does not have implicit equalities except those defined by trivial tight dicuts induced by tight nodes.
If all facet-defining inequalities (FDI) are defined by trivial dicuts or nonnegativity constraints, the digraft is robust
(this is indeed equivalent to Theorem 1.12 because the bipartite edge cover polytope is integral, see e.g. [43] Ch 19.5).
Using the integer decomposition property of tight edge covers and a result of Gerards and Seb˝o [22], they show that an
integral basis consisting of tight dijoins exists. Otherwise, there is a nontrivial dicut 𝐶= 𝛿+(𝑈) that induces an FDI.
They set 𝐶to be tight, which reduces the dimension of lin(J (𝐷, F )) by one. Using the Jump-free lemma, they find
one additional tight dijoin 𝐽with |𝐽∩𝐶| = 2 to add to the basis.
To make their proof constructive, the step of finding an FDI defined by a nontrivial dicut must be made algorithmic,
which is highly nontrivial. For a general polyhedra, the problem of FDI recognition is 𝐷𝑝-hard [38]. Even for well-
structured polyhedra such as perfect matching polytope, the similar question of finding an FDI defined by a nontrivial
odd set is open. We discuss this in detail in the next paragraph. We overcome these difficulties by first, providing a
recognition algorithm for robust digrafts and second, finding a good dicut that is, in fact, an FDI in polynomial time.
Perfect matching lattices
Some results of this work are reminiscent of the setting of perfect matchings. In an
undirected graph 𝐺= (𝑉, 𝐸) a perfect matching is a set of edges incident with each 𝑣∈𝑉exactly once. A graph
is matching-covered if every edge 𝑒∈𝐸belongs to some perfect matching. Denote the set of perfect matchings in
𝐺by M(𝐺). A well-celebrated result due to Lov´asz [28] in the theory of matching covered graphs is that for every
𝑥∈lin(M(𝐺)) ∩Z𝐸we have 2𝑥∈lat(M(𝐺)). Moreover, if 𝐺has no Petersen minor, lat(M(𝐺)) = lin(M(𝐺)) ∩Z𝐸.
Abdi and Silina [5] recently gave a short proof of Lov´asz’s result. In a series of follow-up works of Carvalho, Lucchesi,
and Murty, it was shown that the lattice of perfect matchings lat(M(𝐺)) contains a basis consisting of perfect matchings
[8, 10, 9, 12, 13] (see the excellent survey book [32]). In proving these results, the authors leverage many structural
results on matching-covered graphs, such as tight cut decomposition established by Edmonds, Pulleyblank and Lov´asz
[17]. They define the notions of barrier cuts and 2-separation cuts which are crucial for the existence of nontrivial
tight odd cuts.
We give a characterization of when a digraft has a tight dijoin that has an interesting resemblance to Tutte’s
theorem for perfect matchings [47]. This allows us to identify similar notions of barrier dicuts and 2-separation dicuts
in digrafts. However, there are important distinctions between perfect matchings and tight dijoins in digrafts. For
example, a well-studied problem in matching theory is testing whether a graph is Birkhoff-von Neumann (BvN), i.e.,
the perfect matching polytope is characterized solely by nonnegativity and degree constraints [7, 11, 15, 16]. It is
open whether recognition of BvN graphs is in NP. In contrast, we are able to recognize robust digrafts in polynomial

constraints. This distinction allows us to greatly simplify the proof of Carvalho, Lucchesi, and Murty, and bypass the

Congruency-constrained optimization
The literature of congruency-constrained combinatorial optimization is
quite rich, e.g., [6, 19, 24, 27, 33, 34, 36, 37, 41]. A central open question in this area is the exact perfect matching
problem: given a graph 𝐺= (𝑉, 𝐸), a subset of edges 𝑅⊆𝐸that are colored red, and a number 𝑘, find a perfect
matching with 𝑘red edges if it exists. A randomized polynomial algorithm exists by [35], but whether a deterministic
algorithm exists is open. As a relaxation, El Maalouly, Steiner, and Wulf [33] gave an algorithm to find a perfect
matching with the correct parity in deterministic polynomial time using the theory of perfect matching lattices. Tight
strengthenings can be captured by submodular flows [18], whereas tight strongly connected orientations can be captured
by matroid intersection [21]. H¨orsch et al. [24] show that finding a common basis with an odd (even) number of red
elements for two general matroids requires exponentially many independence queries. Our result on parity strongly
connected orientations provides a new class of matroid intersection whose parity-constrained problem can be solved
in polynomial time, generalizing the result on perfect matchings for bipartite graphs.

In this section, we deal with non-robust bricks, which is the most challenging case. The proof puts together everything
we established so far. To start with, we define a partial order over all non-tight dicuts. Let 𝐶, 𝐶′ be non-tight dicuts of
