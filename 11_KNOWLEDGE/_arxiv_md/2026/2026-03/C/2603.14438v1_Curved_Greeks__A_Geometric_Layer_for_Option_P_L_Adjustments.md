Abstract
Short-horizon option book management relies on P&L expansions in a small set of risk
factors.
In practice, the quadratic term and common desk adjustments (smile corrections,
execution cost add-ons) depend on the chosen factor coordinates, so predicted second-order
P&L can change when moving between spot, forward, and log-forward parameterizations. We
propose a local, model-agnostic framework that makes the quadratic term coordinate invari-
ant. The usual Hessian is replaced by a covariant Hessian defined by an aﬀine connection,
yielding an invariant quadratic predictor. The connection is calibrated to match a desk tar-
get for quadratic P&L—Vanna–Volga for smile effects or, in principle, a local fit to realized
P&L—while leaving first-order hedge Greeks unchanged. Execution frictions enter through a
quadratic cost model for hedge trades. Combined with hedge ratios, this induces an equivalent
quadratic penalty on factor moves, makes portfolio netting of costs explicit, and provides local
liquidity-aware second-order sensitivities and rebalancing directions. Calibration reduces to
small linear systems with clear identifiability conditions. Two FX barrier case studies (EURUSD,
USDTRY) illustrate the workflow, and we briefly sketch extensions to other quadratic penalties
(risk normalization, scenario/gap terms, and xVA/capital add-ons).
Keywords: Curved Greeks; Covariant Hessian; Aﬀine connection; Coordinate invariance; Vanna–
Volga; Riemannian metric; Geodesic hedging

Introduction
Short-horizon option book management relies on local P&L predictors built from first- and
second-order sensitivities in a small set of risk factors. A standard baseline is the Black–Scholes
(BS) delta-gamma expansion in coordinates such as spot/forward and implied volatility. In practice,
desks rarely use the raw BS quadratic predictor: they incorporate smile effects (via an explicit smile
model or via local corrections such as Vanna–Volga, Castagna and Mercurio (2007); Wystup (2006))

The framework separates desk second-order inputs into two classes of local quadratic data.
First, one specifies a target quadratic form for the second-order P&L term (for example a smile-
implied correction such as Vanna–Volga, or, in principle, a local fit to realized P&L). This target
is used to calibrate the correction term in the covariant Hessian. Second, one specifies one or more
local quadratic penalties that represent costs or constraints of rebalancing (execution costs treated
in detail, and further penalties such as risk normalization or scenario/capital terms outlined later).
Multiple penalty terms can be combined within the same predictor, and the resulting quadratic
P&L remains comparable across common reparameterizations by construction.

Related Work
This paper relates to four strands of the literature: (i) smile corrections and short-time asymp-
totics for implied volatility, (ii) geometric and heat-kernel methods for option pricing, (iii) liquidity
and market impact models that motivate quadratic execution costs, and (iv) covariance-based and
information-geometry metrics. Our aim is complementary rather than competitive: we recast fa-
miliar desk adjustments in a coordinate-invariant second-order language and calibrate the resulting
local objects directly to market- or data-based targets. Beyond smile and liquidity, the same local
quadratic viewpoint can encode other desk features (e.g. risk normalization from factor covari-
ances, scenario or gap penalties, and internal funding/margin/capital weights). We outline such
extensions in Sec. 9, while keeping the present discussion focused on the two instances developed
in full detail.
On Smile corrections and short-time asymptotics, practitioners and the academic liter-
ature go beyond Black–Scholes (BS) to accommodate smiles and skews. In FX, the Vanna–Volga
(VV) recipe is widely used (see Castagna and Mercurio (2007); Wystup (2006)). On the model side,
local volatility and stochastic volatility frameworks lead to short-time implied-volatility expansions,
including Dupire’s local volatility mapping Dupire (1994), the SABR asymptotics of Hagan, Ku-
mar, Lesniewski, and Woodward (2002), and small-time expansions for more general diffusions
(see Berestycki, Busca, and Florent (2002); Lorig, Pagliarani, and Pascucci (2015)). Our contribu-
tion is model-agnostic and pragmatic: rather than committing to a specific diffusion or replication
scheme, we calibrate a local second-order correction term so that the covariant Hessian matches a
chosen quadratic target (VV, or in principle an empirical local regression), and we recover the BS
predictor when the smile is locally flat.

Regarding liquidity and market impact, quadratic execution costs are standard in optimal
execution and inventory models, starting with Almgren and Chriss (2000), and have been extended
to transient and cross-impact effects (see, e.g., Benzaquen, Mastromatteo, Eisler, and Bouchaud
(2016); Bouchaud, Farmer, and Lillo (2009); Gatheral and Schied (2013)). In our setting, quoted
widths and typical sizes per bucket define a symmetric positive definite (SPD) impact matrix Λ in
hedge-trade units. A chosen rebalancing rule x 7→q(x) links small factor moves δx to hedge trades
δq. Locally, δq ≈M(x) δx. Combining this linear response with a local quadratic model for trade
costs (as used later in the paper) yields a quadratic cost on factor moves, gℓ(x) = M(x)⊤Λ M(x).
The Levi–Civita connection of gℓgives second-order sensitivities adjusted for liquidity, and gℓalso
defines distances and least-cost rebalancing paths. The construction is deliberately simple and
data-driven. More refined impact models (e.g. transient kernels or factor cross-impact) can be
reflected either in Λ or in the rebalancing rule without changing the coordinate-invariant layer.
Finally, on covariance and information-geometry metrics, choosing g = Σ−1, with Σ
an empirical covariance of factor increments, connects to the Mahalanobis distance Mahalanobis
(1936). When a likelihood is available, it also connects to the Fisher information metric viewpoint
developed in information geometry, cf. Amari (2016). In practice, covariance-based metrics provide
a simple normalization when execution data are scarce, and they encode the relative scale and
correlation of typical factor moves.
Putting these strands together: heat-kernel methods deliver a model-implied local geometry at
short maturities. Our approach addresses a different question: it builds a local quadratic descrip-
tion for P&L and hedging directly from market or data inputs, by calibrating the second-order term
to a target and incorporating costs and constraints through additional quadratic penalties. VV pro-
vides a practical calibration target, and empirical local P&L regressions are a drop-in alternative
when available. Liquidity enters through the induced quadratic penalty gℓ, and the coordinate-
invariant formulation makes predictors and backtests robust to common coordinate choices, e.g. S,
F, or log F.

The Constant-Λ Regime
We analyze the practically important regime in which the impact matrix Λ is taken constant
in the state x = (S, σ) within each time bucket (while still allowing Λ to change between buckets).
Operationally this matches how liquidity inputs are managed: dealers stream firm widths and firm
sizes by venue and product bucket and keep those settings fixed until a risk toggle, a time refresh
or an event. Using vol points with vega notionals for vanilla options and pips with cash clips for
spot further reduces any residual dependence of per-unit costs on small moves in S or σ. In short,
in this section Λ is piecewise constant in time and treated as independent of x within a bucket.
Within a bucket, Λ is fixed, but the induced state-space penalty generally varies with x through
the rebalancing sensitivities M(x). As in Sec. 6, with δq ≈M(x) δx and M(x) ∈Rm×2, the local
quadratic execution-cost proxy L(δq) is given by Eq. (6.2) and implies a quadratic penalty L(δx)
for a factor move δx given by Eq. (6.1), with gℓ(x) = M(x)⊤Λ M(x) ∈R2×2. Since Λ is constant
in x within the bucket, Eq. (6.7) becomes
∂igℓ= (∂iM)⊤Λ M + M ⊤Λ (∂iM),
i ∈{S, σ}.

Further Applications and Extensions
The previous sections develop two constructions in detail: (i) a market-implied second-order
correction to quadratic P&L, encoded through a calibrated aﬀine connection, and (ii) execution
frictions, captured by a local quadratic trade-cost model that induces a quadratic penalty on risk-
factor increments. The same local quadratic interface can incorporate other desk features whenever
they admit a quadratic approximation over the hedging horizon. In this section we sketch a few
practically relevant extensions (such as risk normalization via factor covariances or scenario/gap
add-ons), and we do not claim empirical validation beyond the case studies already presented.

which modify the scalar being expanded (e.g., using an xVA-adjusted value) and/or specify an
additional target quadratic form for the covariant Hessian, and (ii) penalty-side extensions, which

Conclusion and Future Research
We have introduced a geometric short-horizon framework for option and portfolio P&L in
which second-order effects are treated in a coordinate-invariant way. The key step is to replace the
Euclidean Hessian in the quadratic expansion by a covariant Hessian,
eHij = ∂ijV −Ck
ij ∂kV,
so that the quadratic predictor f

(up to numerical error). This removes spurious dependence on coordinate choices (e.g. (S, σ) versus
(log F, σ)) and provides adjusted second-order sensitivities that are comparable across common
parameterizations.
We showed how to identify the contracted correction term Ck
ij ∂kV by matching a chosen target
quadratic form built from observables. Targets can come from Vanna–Volga or, in principle, from a
local regression of realized short-horizon P&L when such data are available. This recasts common
desk second-order smile adjustments as a correction to the quadratic term and reduces to the BS
predictor when the smile is locally flat. In practice, identification reduces to small linear systems
whose stability requires that calibration instruments span the relevant gradient directions; we state
the resulting conditioning requirements and discuss stabilization when the system is ill conditioned.
We also incorporated execution frictions through a local quadratic model for hedge-trade costs,

factor moves,

and the associated Levi–Civita connection provides liquidity-adjusted second-order sensitivities as
well as cost-based distances and least-cost rebalancing paths (geodesics). Crucially for practice,
this construction is book-level: execution costs apply to the net hedge trade, so internal crossing
and netting effects arise mechanically rather than from deal-by-deal add-ons. Two FX barrier case
studies (EURUSD and USDTRY) illustrate the workflow and highlight regimes where smile corrections
are small and regimes where liquidity costs are material.
The framework is deliberately local and model agnostic: it does not replace a full pricing model,
but provides a coordinate-invariant interface for organizing short-horizon second-order corrections
(smile and execution frictions) and for adding further features when they admit a local quadratic
approximation. Several limitations are inherent to this perspective. The approximation can de-
grade near kinks or under genuinely nonlocal effects (large gaps and jumps). Second derivatives
and local regressions can be noisy, and calibration becomes unstable when hedge instruments do
not span the state directions robustly. Finally, a market-implied connection inferred from a target
quadratic form need not coincide with the Levi–Civita connection of any smooth metric, so the
metric-based viewpoint should be interpreted as a modeling choice when one wishes to represent
second-order structure through costs.
Beyond the smile and liquidity constructions developed in detail, Sec. 9 sketches further appli-
cations that fit the same local quadratic template. Overall, moving from Euclidean to intrinsic
aﬀine/Riemannian tools provides an implementable upgrade to short-horizon second-order P&L
and hedging: it clarifies practical desk corrections, enforces coordinate invariance, and offers a
disciplined way to incorporate additional quadratic targets and penalties.
