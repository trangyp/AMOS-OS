Composition of random functions and word reconstruction
Guillaume Chapuy∗
Guillem Perarnau†
April 1, 2026
Abstract
Given two functions a : [n] →[n] and b : [n] →[n] chosen uniformly at random, any word
w = w1w2 . . . wk ∈{a, b}k induces a random function w : [n] →[n] by composition, i.e. w =
ϕwk ◦· · · ◦ϕw1 with ϕa = a and ϕb = b. We study the following question: assuming w is fixed
but unknown, and n goes to infinity, does one sample of w carry enough information to (partially)
recover the word w with good enough probability?
We show that the length of w, and its exponent (largest d such that w = ud for some word u) can
be recovered with high probability. We also prove that the random functions stemming from two
different words are separated in total variation distance, provided that certain “auto-correlation”
word-depending constant c(w) is different for each of them. We give an explicit expression for c(w)
and conjecture that non-isomorphic words have different constants. We prove that this is the case
assuming a major conjecture in transcendental number theory, Schanuel’s conjecture.

1.1
Presentation of the problem
The problem studied in this paper is motivated by the study of random automata, random permuta-
tions, and random trees.
Throughout this paper, n ≥1 is an integer that will eventually tend to infinity. We consider two
functions a, b : [n] −→[n] chosen independently and uniformly at random, where we write [n] :=
{1, 2, . . . , n}.
One can picture this data as a deterministic finite automaton (DFA), in which the
elements in [n] are states and the two functions a and b represent the transitions associated to the
letters a and b, respectively. In graph theory language, this object can be represented as a directed
graph with two types of arcs (labelled with a or b), such that each vertex has exactly one outgoing arc
of each type. Choosing the functions independently and uniformly at random is equivalent to choosing

The random function w is a priori non-uniform. The goal of this paper is to study it and understand
how it depends on the associated word w. We defer a detailed discussion of the connections with
random automata and random permutations to Section 1.3 at the end of the introduction, beginning
instead with our main questions and results.
Problem A (Main question, general form). What are the properties of the random function w,
and to what extent does it encode information about the word w used to construct it?
A more precise variant of this problem is as follows:
Problem B (Properties). Which properties of the word w can be guessed with probability close to
one in one sample of the random function w? and in a bounded number of samples?
A natural way to attack this question is to find statistics, measurable observables of the function
w, that behave differently for different words w.
Problem C (Statistics). Identify observables for the random function w with characterizable asymp-
totic behaviour that depends on w or uniquely determine w up to isomorphism.
For example, we will see that the proportion of leaves of the function w (see below for a precise
definition) satisfies an almost sure convergence theorem, with a deterministic limit only depending on
the length of the word w. Thus the word length can be guessed, with high probability, in one sample.

1.3
Random automata, random permutations, and comments
As mentioned above, the study of the function w appears naturally, although maybe not as explicitly
stated as in this paper, in the field of random automata, which is a classical subject at the intersection
of random graphs and computer science [Nic14, CD17, QS22, ABBP20]. In a recent work [CP25],
motivated by the study of the shortest synchronisation word for random automata [KKS13, Nic19], we
introduced the notion of w-trees, which are automata in which the w-transitions induce a cycle-rooted
tree. A key element in that work is to show that a random automaton is a w-tree with probability
asymptotic to |w|
n , uniformly for all w of at most logarithmic length. In a sense, the bulk of [CP25]
shows that, for the function property of being a cycle-rooted tree, the random function w behaves like a
uniform random function, in some qualitative and quantitative sense. The upper bound on the shortest
synchronizing word in [CP25] was later slightly tightened by Martinsson in the preprint [Mar23], with
no references to w-trees, but using w-transitions for some “structured” words w.
The common difficulty in all these papers comes from the fact that it is difficult to analyze the
w-transitions of elements in [n] simultaneously, due to their lack of independence. In some way, they
all have to do with the question of understanding the random function w for various choices of the
word w, which led us to formalizing the question in this paper.
Apart from random automata, another natural motivation for this work comes from random per-
mutations. Our main question can be asked similarly if the uniform random functions a, b are replaced
by uniform elements σ, π in the symmetric group Sn. Note that in this context, it is also natural to
6


## Page 7


allow the symbols σ−1 and π−1 in the word w. The distribution of the number of short cycles in
such compositions is well understood [Nic94, HP20, KM20, KM22] with a limit law depending only on
the exponent of the underlying word. This problem has deep connections to free probability; see the
introduction of [KM22]. As it turns out, the limiting distribution of short cycles in our setting is the
same as for random permutations, and in particular some of our results also apply to the latter case;
see Remark 2.
In the context of random permutations, the question of the TV-distance to the uniform measure
on the symmetric or alternating group could be studied by means of representation theory, at least
for certain simple words w which have some symmetries related to conjugacy invariance. We refer
to F´eray’s formulation of the problem [F´er], who mentions that the smallest word for which no such
property is known is w = στσ2τ 2.
Note that the references above often consider the case of an alphabet of arbitrary fixed size rather
than {a, b}: we leave the reader to check that all our proof techniques and results are easily adapted
to this context. Note also that considering an alphabet of size growing with n would not make sense in
our setting, since we only work with words w of fixed length — however, this could lead to interesting
new questions.
To conclude this discussion, let us also mention that random functions [n] →[n] are a standard and
very well studied object in discrete probability, with deep relations to random trees, see e.g. [FO89,
Pit06, CP24]. A natural model interpolating between uniform random functions and uniform random
permutations is to consider a random function f chosen with probability proportional to the weight
xc(f), where c(f) denotes the number of vertices belonging to a cycle in f, and x ≥0 is a real parameter.
In this distribution of “Boltzmann” type, x acts as a fugacity parameter controlling the “cyclicity” of
the function. The classical ensembles are recovered as limiting cases of this parameter: if x →0, the
measure concentrates on functions with minimal cyclic content (only one single fixed point), yielding
uniform rooted Cayley trees; if x = 1, the weight is uniform for all f, yielding uniform random functions;
and if x →∞, the measure concentrates on functions with maximal cyclic content (i.e. where every
vertex is cyclic), yielding uniform random permutations. All the questions asked in this paper for
uniform random functions could be studied for this general model, which might lead to interesting new
phenomena.
1.4
Plan of the paper
In Section 2, we recall basic and standard facts about BGWTs and how they approximate local in-
neighbourhoods of uniform random functions. This section also establishes the convergence of the
expected number of leaves in Theorem 1 and sets the necessary notation and toolbox for the next
sections where more involved local approximations are studied, both at the first and second order. In
Section 3 we study the 1
n-correction to the leaf-probability by counting the contribution of cyclic local
in-neighbourhoods. This requires to introduce variants of BGWTs with self-replication properties. In
Section 4, we compute the variance and a shifted second moment of the number of leaves of w, which
are both of linear order with leading constants c(w) and ˜c(w) respectively, to prove Theorem 5. In
order to determine these constants we combine finite-volume approximations with the study of coupled
BGWTs, and use the results in the previous section on self-replicating BGWTs. At the end of the
section we provide a self-contained explicit description of all the constants involved in these leading
terms (Section 4.7), and we make the link with the Schanuel conjecture (Section 4.8). In Section 5,
we focus on higher-order shifted moments of the number of leaves. Our main result here bounds the
q-th shifted moment, which hints a Gaussian-type behaviour. In particular, this gives us control on
fourth moments and enables us to translate differences in variance into non-trivial TV-separation,
proving Corollary 6. Finally, Section 6 is devoted to the short cycle distribution of w and the proof of
Theorem 3.

Definition 8 (Maximum self-overlap set). Given w ∈{a, b}k, define M as the set of pairs (i, j)

same position.
