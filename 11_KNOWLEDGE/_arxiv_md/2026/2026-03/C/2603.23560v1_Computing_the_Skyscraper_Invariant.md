Computing the Skyscraper Invariant
Marc Fersztand and Jan Jendrysiak
March 2026
Abstract
We develop the first algorithms for computing the Skyscraper Invariant [FJNT24].
This is
a filtration of the classical rank invariant for multiparameter persistence modules defined by the
Harder-Narasimhan filtrations along every central charge supported at a single parameter value.
Cheng’s algorithm [Cheng24] can be used to compute HN filtrations of arbitrary acyclic quiver
representations in polynomial time in the total dimension, but in practice, the large dimension of
persistence modules makes this direct approach infeasible. We show that by exploiting the additivity
of the HN filtration and the special central charges, one can get away with a brute-force approach.
For d-parameter modules, this produces an FPT ε-approximate algorithm with runtime dominated
by O(1/εd · Tdec), where Tdec is the time for decomposition, which we compute with aida [DJK25].
We show that the wall-and-chamber structure of the module can be computed via lower envelopes
of degree d −1 polynomials. This allows for an exact computation of the Skyscraper Invariant whose
runtime is roughly O(nd · Tdec) for n the size of the presentation of the modules and enables a faster
hybrid algorithm to compute an approximation.
For 2-parameter modules, we have implemented not only our algorithms but also, for the first
time, Cheng’s algorithm. We compare all algorithms and, as a proof of concept for data analysis,
compute a filtered version of the Multiparameter Landscape for biomedical data.

Introduction.
Multiparameter Persistence Modules (MPM) provide a way to capture topological
features of multifiltered data. Unlike the one parameter case, the decomposition of an MPM is, in general,
neither easy to interpret nor robust to noise [4]. To use MPM in practice, one needs invariants that are
informative, stable, computable, and easy either to interpret or to integrate into a statistical or Machine
Learning pipeline. A first example is given by the rank invariant [14, 15] and its vectorisations [19, 64],

Given a d-parameter module V , θ ∈R and parameters α ≤β ∈Rd, the value sθ

and carries a more discriminating erosion-distance, which makes it stable with respect to the interleaving
distance [27]. The Skyscraper Invariant induces filtrations of all the aforementioned vectorisations of the
rank invariant.
We will describe multiple algorithms to compute the Skyscraper Invariant and also touch on the
computation of Harder-Narasimhan Filtrations with respect to other stability conditions.
Contributions.
In Section 1, we give an elementary introduction to Harder-Narasimhan (HN) filtrations
and a post-hoc motivation for their utility in Persistence Theory. We also briefly explain that, up to an
ε-error in the erosion distance, it suffices to compute the HN filtration at each point α on an ε-grid in the
parameter space.
For quiver representations, this was recently made possible in polynomial time via Cheng’s algorithm
[18], described in Section 4. We present the first implementation of Cheng’s algorithm1 using the random
method introduced in [30] to compute shrunk subspaces.
The obtained algorithm computes with high probability the Skyscraper Invariant in O(t(V )19ε−11d)
time, up to poly-logarithmic factors, where t(V ), the thickness of V , is the maximal pointwise dimension.
We optimized Cheng’s algorithm to our setting, leading in Proposition 4.15 to an improvement of a factor
t(V )2ε−2d compared to the general version of the algorithm (Corollary 4.5). Building on ideas from [38],
we propose further optimisations that yield considerable empirical speed-ups.
A priori, this method is generally not directly applicable to the large data considered in TDA
applications. In Section 3, we introduce a strategy which significantly cuts the computation time of
the Skyscraper Invariant: Algorithm 2 reduces the computation of the HN filtration at α ∈R to
indecomposable submodules uniquely generated at α. It blends the decomposition algorithm aida and
kernel computation with mpfree2.

Related Work.
Many informative invariants of MPM have been proposed in the literature, including,
among others, [11, 9, 43, 52, 57, 2], and for some, there are also implemented algorithms [24, 64, 19, 16,
50, 48, 66]. All of these are either not stronger than the rank invariant, not (yet) practically computable
for large modules, or unstable in interleaving distance, demonstrating the advantage of our work. The
idea of computing filtrations has also appeared in [8, 56]. An algorithm for the Skyscraper Invariant
has been implemented in the special case of ladder persistence modules [39]. The computation of HN
filtrations of quiver representations is an active topic in complexity theory [38, 30].

1.1
Persistence Modules
Filtrations of simplicial complexes.
Topological Data Analysis seeks to compute topological in-
variants from geometric data. In a typical setting, one constructs a simplicial complex filtered by d ≥1

1.2
Harder-Narasimhan Filtrations
The Skyscraper Invariant arises from the Harder-Narasimhan filtration [31] of persistence modules for a
specific set of stability conditions. We will explain how one could arrive at considering HN filtrations
even without prior knowledge of geometric invariant theory [58]. For persistence modules, this theory was
originally developed in [28] and [27].
Lifetime.
Let V be a multiparameter persistence module. A sensible idea to extend the barcode is the
following: search for an element with a "maximal lifetime" (an idea developed further in [56]). By lifetime
we mean for any element v ∈Vα, with α ∈Rd, the set
Lv := {β ∈Rd | α ≤β and Vα→β (v) ̸= 0}
of parameter values under which v persists and "maximal" refers here to its volume. To assure that every
lifetime has a finite volume, we will assume that every module is bounded, which can always be realised
by restricting the module to a bounded subset of Rd.

Preliminaries.
In the previous section, we have touched on many notions without properly defining them. The reader
should view this section as a way to read further on this material if they are not familiar with it or,
alternatively, skip it and come back to it if necessary.
In Subsection 2.1 we review bifiltrations as used in Example 1.1 and the important Conjecture 1.9. In
Subsection 2.2, we introduce notation for multiparameter persistence theory, and in Subsection 2.3 review
discretization and restriction via Kan extensions. Our definition of the HN filtration at α ∈Rd, given in
the previous section, is actually not consistent with the classical one [31, 45]. Instead of a filtration of
⟨Vα⟩, one really considers a filtration of V . In Subsection 2.4, we will give a small overview of this theory.
2.1
Filtrations of simplicial complexes
We denote by SCpx the category of finite simplicial complexes and fix d ≥1. We see Rd as a poset endowed
with the product partial order. A Rd-filtered simplicial complex is given by a functor K• : Rd →SCpx
such that for α ≤β ∈Rd, the simplicial map Kα→β is injective. Depending on the application, the
construction of a filtered simplicial complex from the data can take many forms. We now present several
standard constructions.
Scale parameter
In one-parameter persistence (d = 1), we often build a scale-indexed filtration of
simplicial complexes from a finite metric space (X, dX). Given r ∈R, let Nr(X) be the graph whose
vertex set is X and whose edges are all the {x, y} ⊂X such that dX(x, y) ≤r. The Rips filtration of X is
the R-filtered simplicial complex VR(X) given for r ∈R by the clique complex of Nr(X). Given x ∈X,
we denote by Vor(x) the cell of x ∈X in the Voronoi diagram of X. Moreover, for all r ∈R, we write
B(x, r) for the closed ball of radius r around x. The alpha filtration of X is the R-indexed simplicial
complex D(X) given for r ∈R by the nerve of the sets (B(x, r) ∩Vor(x))x∈X.

(X, dX). We now describe ways to add a density parameter to VR(X) and obtain a R2-filtered simplicial
complex that can deal with noisy pointclouds like the one in Example 1.1.

To store the Hilbert function of a module V , we will use a collection of intervals whose indicator

which we store via their graded Betti numbers.

cutting it up into intervals. The advantage of our approach is that over R2, the relations of an interval
form an anti-chain by ordering with respect to x or y coordinate. Point-location in the interval then
becomes logarithmic in the number of relations.

6.5
Parallel Grid Scanning
In practice, where one wants to compute filtered vectorisations of the Skyscraper Invariant, it is not a
problem to restrict the module to an ε-grid εZ2 and use Algorithm 2. The real power of Theorem A and
Algorithm 6 lies in the fact that it is a recipe to avoid recomputation: For any direct summand Vi of
the input module, the induced grid Grid(Vi) is coarser than εZ2 in many places, especially when ε is
small. Since, as described in Remark 6.11, the slope subdivision of a cell typically consists of a single
region, we mostly need to compute a single HN filtration per cell of the induced grid and not one for
every point on εZ2. We therefore overlay the two grids and use Algorithm 6 only for those cells in each
Grid(Vi) that intersect εZ2. We then store the Hilbert functions of all factors in an array. This enables
the computation of the Skyscraper Invariant at practically arbitrary resolution.

Applications and Open Problems
Conclusion.
Our algorithms, together with the filtered Landscapes give researchers a new invariant for
topological data analysis that is robust to noise, finer than the rank invariant, efficiently computable, and
directly interpretable from a visual representation.
To develop fast algorithms, we studied the wall-and-chambers structure on skyscraper stability
conditions for persistence modules and obtained a full description in Theorem A.
Our experiments with Cheng’s algorithm empirically show that higher blow-up sizes are necessary for
finite fields compared to infinite ones, confirming the intuition behind the results of [35].

Therefore, we can filter the Landscape by replacing the rank with the Skyscraper Invariant. As an
example, we will use the IHC stained sample LargeHypoxicRegion2 of a tumour6, for which the authors

Open Problems.
Improving the runtime.
Figure 8 and Figure 13 make it clear that, in practice, we are still computing
the HN filtration too often. In Remark 6.4, we outline a strategy to prove that it is enough to compute
the slope subdivision only for the equivalence classes of ∼t,V .
These are coarser than the grid by
Proposition 6.3 (ii), but we would probably need a condition that is easy to compute and that guarantees
that two points in Grid(V ) are related by ∼t,V to experience a significant speed up.
Differentiability.
To incorporate invariants of MPM into Machine Learning pipelines, the authors
of [62] define a framework, which needs to be extended to include the Skyscraper Invariant. Instead
of Landscapes, one could also filter other vectorizations of the rank invariant. There are qualitatively
different HN filtrations of MPM via changing the definition of the slope [27]. Can our methods be adapted
to this general setting?
Other Stability Conditions.
Remark 2.12 points towards a possible generalisation to another stability
condition. Consider a positive locally integrable function f : Rd →R. One can replace the real part of the
stability condition
R
Rd dim V with
R
Rd f · dim V . For each J ⊂[d], we can compute the slopes µ(⟨Vα⟩|FJ)
and construct the slope polynomial with these coefficients. Using Proposition 6.7, this polynomial will
approximate the slope. If f is Lipschitz or has other bounds on its growth, then we can bound the error
of this approximation. If for some parameter value α, one slope polynomial is not just minimal, but even
minimal up to this error, we have again found the unique submodule of highest slope for the stability
condition at α.
Möbius Inversion.
In [52], McCleary and Patel Möbius-inverted the rank invariant ([61]) to represent
it as a signed set of rectangles. For each θ ∈R the same can be done with sθ : (Rd)op ×Rd →Zop, yielding
a path in the space of signed d-cells in Rd, Hom(Int(Rd), Z). What does it look like?
For a specific θ, sθ is not always represented by a persistence module, but it sometimes is. An example
was given in Example 1.11 and we can see that these modules are not finitely presented anymore, but they
are finitely encoded over a semi-algebraic set in the sense of Miller’s work [54]. If sθ can be represented
36


## Page 37


by a module, we conjecture that this is always the case. In general, this implies that the Möbius inversion
will not be finite. The question is therefore: Is there a signed semi-algebraic barcode or persistence
diagram [44, 11] that represents each sθ and can this be used to construct an efficient data structure to
store the Skyscraper Invariant?
