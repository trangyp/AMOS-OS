Combinatorial Sparse PCA Beyond the Spiked Identity Model
Syamantak Kumar∗
Purnamrita Sarkar†
Kevin Tian‡
Peiyuan Zhang§
Abstract
Sparse PCA is one of the most well-studied problems in high-dimensional statistics. In this
problem, we are given samples from a distribution with covariance Σ, whose top eigenvector
v ∈Rd is s-sparse. Existing sparse PCA algorithms can be broadly categorized into (1) combi-
natorial algorithms (e.g., diagonal or elementwise covariance thresholding) and (2) SDP-based
algorithms. While combinatorial algorithms are much simpler, they are typically only analyzed
under the spiked identity model (where Σ ∝Id + γvv⊤for some γ > 0), whereas SDP-based
algorithms require no additional assumptions on Σ.
We demonstrate explicit counterexample covariances Σ against the success of standard com-
binatorial algorithms for sparse PCA, when moving beyond the spiked identity model. In light
of this discrepancy, we give the first combinatorial method for sparse PCA that provably suc-
ceeds for general Σ using s2 · polylog(d) samples and d2 · poly(s, log(d)) time, by providing a
global convergence guarantee on a variant of the truncated power method of [YZ13]. We provide
a natural generalization of our method to recovering a vector in a sparse leading eigenspace.
Finally, we evaluate our method on synthetic and real-world sparse PCA datasets.

Introduction
Principal component analysis (PCA) is a classical and widely-used tool for dimensionality reduction
that identifies directions in which the data exhibit the most variance. Interestingly, the classical in-
tuition that the leading eigenvectors of the sample covariance reliably estimate the population-level
principal components can fail dramatically in high dimensions [Pau07, JM09, JL09a]. Even when

eigenvalue threshold at which the top empirical eigenvalue separates from the Marchenko–Pastur
bulk; below this threshold no outlier emerges, and the leading empirical eigenvector is asymptotically
uninformative, exhibiting vanishing correlation with the population eigenvector [Pau07].
Sparse PCA addresses this inconsistency by positing additional structure: that the leading eigenvec-
tor v1 of the population covariance Σ is s-sparse, reflecting the hypothesis that only a small subset
of variables drives the dominant variation. The canonical finite-sample optimization problem is

Models of sparse PCA. Existing sparse PCA algorithms can be grouped by the type of covariance
model assumed. The first is the well-known spiked identity covariance model.1
Model 1 (Spiked identity model). Fix (s, d, n) ∈N3 with s ∈[d], γ ∈(0, 1).
In the spiked
identity model, there is an unknown unit v ∈Rd with nnz(v) ≤s. We obtain {xi}i∈[n] ∼i.i.d. D, a
sub-Gaussian distribution with covariance Σ = 0.9(Id −vv⊤) + vv⊤.
Model 1 has inspired elegant yet simple combinatorial principles for solving sparse PCA. Notable ex-
amples are the diagonal thresholding method [JL09b, AW08], which simply keeps the top s elements
of the marginal variances (the diagonal of the empirical covariance). Another line of work thresholds
the sample covariance matrix itself to establish consistency [BL09, DM16, Wai19, Nov23]. While
these combinatorial methods are computationally light and interpretable, they typically require
structural assumptions, e.g., working under the spiked identity model (Model 1).
Our focus is combinatorial algorithms for the sparse PCA problem in full generality (Model 2), i.e.,
assuming only that the top eigenvector of Σ is sparse, with no other assumptions about Σ.
Model 2 (General model). Fix (s, d, n) ∈N3 with s ∈[d], γ ∈(0, 1). In the general model, there
is an unknown unit v ∈Rd with nnz(v) ≤s, the top eigenvector of an unknown Σ ∈Sd×d

Model 2 generalizes Model 1 by making only the minimal well-posedness assumptions for sparse
PCA: that λ2(Σ) ≤0.9λ1(Σ), and that Σ has a sparse top eigenvector.
Our starting point is the surprising observation that under the more general Model 2, standard com-
binatorial algorithms (many of which have provable recovery guarantees under Model 1) fail with
constant probability under the conjectured minimum sample complexity n ≳s2 log d for polynomial-
time algorithms to exist [BR13]. Indeed, in Section 3, we give counterexamples against diagonal
thresholding, entrywise covariance thresholding, and a planted clique-style greedy correlation rank-
ing suggested recently by [BBKS24]. Together, these counterexamples rule out robustness of typical
≈d2 · poly(s log d)-time combinatorial methods beyond Model 1.
Under Model 2, most provable methods use heavy hammers from convex programming, most promi-
nently semidefinite programming (SDP)-based formulations [dBEG08, AW08, VL12, BR13, WL16,
dBEG08, VCLR13, STL07, ZX18, DMMW17, AW08]. These algorithms are computationally bur-
densome and memory-intensive in high dimensions: for the standard ℓ1-constrained sparse PCA
SDP with ≈d2 constraints and a d × d decision variable, state-of-the-art SDP solvers run in time
Ω(d2ω) ⊆Ω(d4.5) in theory (where ω > 2.371 denotes the current matrix multiplication constant)
[HJS+22, ADV+25], and may incur even larger overheads in practice.
To our knowledge, the
fastest end-to-end implementation (i.e., without initialization assumptions or additional structure)
of the SDP-based approach under Model 2 was given by [WLL14]. Their algorithm requires time
Ω(d4n−0.5) (see the discussion in their Section 4.4), which in the typical sublinear sample regime
n ≪d is still much larger than the input size of nd.
This state of affairs begs the following question, central to this work.
Is there a lightweight combinatorial method that solves sparse PCA under Model 2?
Concretely, our goal is to design an algorithm succeeding under Model 2 with poly(s log(d)) samples
and d2 · poly(s log(d)) time, competitive with existing combinatorial methods for Model 1. Despite
the rich body of work on sparse PCA and many attempts to answer the above question [YZ13,
QLR23, KS24], this fundamental problem has remained open.
The semi-random lens.
Model 2 has connections to a recent line of work on semi-random
models from theoretical computer science, whose goal is to investigate the robustness of statistical
algorithms to their modeling assumptions. Briefly, a (monotone) semi-random adversary changes
a standard statistical model by strengthening the desired signal, in a way that breaks an original
modeling assumption. The underlying philosophy is that if a statistical algorithm fails under such
a “helpful” modification, then the design of the algorithm was overfit to the model.
Our setting is reflective of this philosophy, as compared to Model 1, Model 2 can only amplify the
signal in the v direction: it posits that all other eigenvalues are at most 0.9λ1(Σ), whereas Model 1
posits that they are all exactly 0.9λ1(Σ).
It is thus potentially troublesome from a robustness
standpoint that existing combinatorial heuristics fail under a more general model, that can only
amplify the signal towards v from an information-theoretic point of view.
The semi-random philosophy has been applied to a wide range of statistical problems, starting from
seminal work by [BS95, FK01] on graph coloring. We are particularly inspired by a line of recent
work investigating the brittleness of lightweight, fast algorithms to semi-random models (as opposed
to less efficient convex programming counterparts). This investigation has been applied to (sparse)
linear regression [CG18, JLM+23, KLL+23, GC23, KLL+24], learning [BGL+24, CKST24], and
clustering [BJK+24, BBKS24]. Notably, the work of [BBKS24] designed fast combinatorial algo-
rithms for a semi-random variant of the planted clique problem (building upon [BKS23], which used

Towards sparse subspace recovery. We complement our results by considering the more general
problem of recovering a k-dimensional leading eigenspace, whose components all share a support
of size s. Existing SDP-based algorithms for sparse 1-PCA also solve this more general problem of
sparse subspace recovery, as we recall for completeness in Appendix B (following [VCLR13]).
Several works in the literature, [Mac08, GWS20] have proposed a deflation-based strategy for reduc-
ing the sparse k-PCA problem to iteratively solving sparse 1-PCA problem on a deflated matrix (the
original target, with all previously-learned approximate principal components projected out). We
provide pseudocode for such a reduction in Algorithm 5. Recently, the approximation tolerance of
this strategy was rigorously analyzed in the non-sparse PCA setting by [JKL+24]. Unfortunately,
despite decades of research, to our knowledge no theoretical analysis of deflation-based k-sparse
PCA reductions currently exists under Model 3, the natural generalization of Model 2.
We investigate this lack of theoretical guarantees for deflation-based sparse PCA methods in Sec-
tion 4.2. We provide a formal barrier against such reductions for sparse PCA: namely, that Model 3
does not reduce to an instance of itself after deflation, under mild approximation error. Our coun-
terexample (Lemma 11) constructs Σ ∈Sd×d
⪰0

s = 2 coordinates, such that after projecting out a 3-sparse vector that is arbitrarily well-correlated

leverages the generality of Model 2, again highlighting the potential brittleness of existing sparse
PCA frameworks to their modeling assumptions.
Nevertheless, we show in Theorem 2 that under Model 3, RTPM does successfully approximate a
single sparse eigenvector in the leading eigenspace. This opens the door to its use in any potential
future deflation-based sparse PCA frameworks that can bypass the barrier in Lemma 11.

Related work
In addition to the work cited in the introduction, we provide a brief sketch of more related work on
sparse PCA in this section, as the literature has studied several variants of the problem.
Existing algorithms for Model 1. A large body of work [Ma13, CMW12, YX15, WL16, BPP18,
GWS20, CXY25] develops algorithms with provable convergence guarantees for the spiked identity
model (Model 1) with either 1 or k spikes. The strategies therein range from using marginal variances
for providing a warm start, a thresholding-based variant of power method, a reduction to sparse
4


## Page 7


PCA, and using random projections with a deflation-style approach. While these algorithms tend
to be lightweight and combinatorial in nature, none provably extend to Model 2 (and indeed, we
provide counterexamples to several prominent ones in Section 3).
Existing algorithms for Model 2. As discussed earlier, existing provable algorithms for solving
sparse PCA under Model 2 using poly(s log(d)) samples are based on solving SDPs. Here we outline
several computationally-cheaper alternatives in the literature and their guarantees.
Most SDP-free algorithms for the general covariance model are spectral in nature. The truncated
power method as proposed and analyzed in [YZ13] was previously only known to succeed under
a suitable local initialization. Recently, [QLR23] proposed a single-pass streaming algorithm and
claim it is the first to provably reach the global optimum without local initialization under a general
Σ. The method requires O(d2) memory, O(nd2) time, but achieves error O( d2
√n), so it requires
e.g., poly(d) samples for constant error. A follow-up work by [KS24] introduced a computationally
lightweight, linear-time streaming algorithm, though its statistical guarantees again require a larger
sample size (Ω(poly(d))) than is information-theoretically optimal.
Optimization and approximation viewpoints. Several works study sparse PCA primarily as a
deterministic optimization problem by developing exact or approximation algorithms, and geometric
characterizations. These do not provide statistical recovery rates as a function of the sample size
n. Representative examples include [CDWZ20, BK23, CR24, PZZ25, LX25].
Computational-statistical gaps. Building on the foundational work of [BR13, WBS16], where a
conjectured computational-statistical gap for sparse PCA was shown via a reduction to the planted
clique problem, recent work has studied sparse PCA in intermediate computational regimes, in-
cluding certification hardness for constrained PCA [BKW20, PR22, DKWB23]; closely related com-
putational barriers also appear in sparse CCA [GMZ17]. However, unlike the more fine-grained
computational-statistical tradeoffs that our work focuses on, these works primarily examine the gap
between the statistical properties of polynomial-time algorithms vs. exponential-time algorithms.

Experiments
This section presents a series of empirical tests over both synthetic and real-world datasets to
compare the efficiency and performance of our new method with heuristic and SDP-based methods.
Sample-splitting ablations. In reporting all other experiments than those in the current discus-
sion, whenever we describe an algorithm as RTPM, we formally mean a variant of Algorithm 1 that
we call RTPM-full for disambiguation. This variant reuses the full-sample empirical covariance bΣ
at every iteration, as opposed to Algorithm 1 which splits the sample into independent batches for
use in each iteration. We similarly call the variant described in Algorithm 1 RTPM-disjoint.
We choose to report our experimental results for RTPM-full because we believe this better reflects
the use of our strategy in practical sparse PCA instances. The sample complexity of RTPM-full
automatically saves a T factor over the corresponding RTPM-disjoint counterpart, which discards
samples at every iteration. While our proofs only apply to RTPM-disjoint due to our use of inde-
pendent sub-exponential concentration via Fact 4, here we conduct an ablation study comparing
the effect of sample-splitting on a challenging sparse PCA instance.
We specifically perform our ablation on the GreedyCorr counterexample construction (Lemma 4 in
Section 3). We compare two variants that are identical in all algorithmic choices: they use the
same truncation level (set to r = 5s), the same set of restarts (all standard basis vectors {ei}i∈[d],
processed in restart batches), the same number of iterations T, and the same final selection rule
based on maximizing the Rayleigh objective. The only difference is how the empirical covariance is
formed across iterations. We report the resulting sin2 error as a function of T. As Figure 1 shows,
both algorithms achieve similar sin2 error, which justifies the use of RTPM-full for our experiments.
We believe that our theoretical analyses for the batched variant, RTPM-disjoint, could potentially
be extended to RTPM-full, which we leave as an interesting open direction.
