1. Introduction
Complex cells, introduced in [4], are holomorphic analogs of the cells of
o-minimal geometry (e.g. semialgebraic or subanalytic geometry). A classical
o-minimal cell is, roughly, a “product” I1 ⊙· · · ⊙In of intervals {Ij}, where
the fibers are translated and scaled by continuous functions on their respective
bases. A complex cell F1 ⊙· · · ⊙Fn is composed in a similar manner from basic
fibers {Fi} such as discs and annuli in the complex plane, where the radii of
the fibers are determined by holomorphic functions of the relevant bases (see
Section 2.2).

In [4], Binyamini and Novikov develop the theory of complex cells in the
context of the structures Ralg and Ran. They obtain uniform parameterization
and preparation theorems for families of semialgebraic or globally subanalytic
sets and functions. In the case where the relevant sets or functions are semial-
gebraic, their results are effective and depend polynomially on the degrees of
the polynomials defining the given sets.
The constructions in the algebraic and analytic settings differ in several
points, with the following consequences. First, in the case of sets which are, for
example, sub-Pfaffian but not necessarily semialgebraic, the arguments of [4]
give parameterizations whose size and complexity might be exponential in the
degrees of the parameterized sets. Second, it is not clear that applying the
constructions in the more general analytic setting to sets definable in some
reduct of Ran yields cells definable in the same reduct — these constructions
depend on the definability of coefficients in Laurent expansions of definable
functions.
In this paper, we extend the results of [4] to the setting of a sharply o-minimal
expansion of the real field, as defined by Binyamini, Novikov, and Zak in [6]
(see Section 2.1). An example is the structure RrPfaff generated by restricted
Pfaffian functions.
We obtain effective preparation and parameterization theorems (Theo-
rems 2.38 and 2.40), which are uniform in families, and which depend poly-
nomially on the degrees of the relevant sets and functions. This resolves [5,
Conjecture 21]. As an added benefit, our constructions are definable in the
sense that applying them in any reduct of Ran (ignoring questions of effectivity)
yields uniformly finite covers which are definable in the same reduct. The
precise statements require some technical set-up which we review in Sections 2.1
and 2.2. The following are informal versions one may keep in mind.
Theorem A (Sharp Cellular Parametrization Theorem, #CPT). Let C =
F1 ⊙· · · ⊙Fℓ⊂Cℓbe a complex cell and let Z1, . . . , Zk be a finite collection of
analytic hypersurfaces of C, all of them definable in an o-minimal structure S.
Then there is a finite collection of complex cells Ci and holomorphic maps
fi : Ci →Cℓ, all of them definable in S, such that the sets fi(Ci)∩C cover C and
each of them is either disjoint from or contained in each of the hypersurfaces Zj.
Assume now that S is sharply o-minimal with FD-filtration {SF,D}. If the
hypersurfaces {Zj} as well as the radii functions determining the fibers {Fj}
are in SF,D, then the number of maps {fi} may be taken to be polyF (D, k),
and the radii functions determining the fibers of the cells Ci may be taken to be
in SOF (1),polyF (D).

We note that, similarly to [3], the application of our results to real analytic

the complexity of the resulting parameterizations depends on the complexity of
the graph of such a holomorphic continuation, rather than that of the original
real sets.
We defer the discussion of the applications of Theorems A and B to real sets
to [8], where we define the appropriate subclass of sharply o-minimal structures,
called analytically generated structures (similar to those described in [5, Section
3.3]), and obtain sharp versions of the Yomdin–Gromov lemma and of the
subanalytic preparation theorems of Parusinski and Lion–Rolin [13, 12] (see
also [18]), as well as establishing Wilkie’s conjecture on polylogarithmic point
counting for such structures.
In a subsequent paper [2], we use the preparation theorems of [8] to obtain
sharp versions of the logarithmic–exponential preparation theorems of [12, 18].
We deduce from these the sharp o-minimality of certain structures, includ-
ing Rexp, as well as establish Wilkie’s conjecture for such structures. In the
case of Rexp, this conjecture was recently resolved in [7] by Binyamini, Novikov,
and Zak.

Appendix A. The real setting
We recall the notion of a real complex cell and of real cellular maps (see [4,
Section 2.2.2]).
Definition A.1. The real part RC of a complex cell C of length ℓis the
intersection of C with Rℓ. The positive real part R+C of C is similarly obtained
by intersecting each coordinate of C which is not of type ∗with the positive real
numbers R+ = (0, ∞). A holomorphic function f : C →C is called real if it is
real valued on RC. Finally, a complex cell C is called real if all radii defining
its components are real holomorphic functions over their corresponding bases.
We note that a real complex cell of length ℓis a subset of Cℓand not of Rℓ.
Definition A.2. Let C be a complex cell and let Z ⊂C. We will call Z
symmetric if it is invariant under the conjugation z 7→z.
Thus real complex cells and the zero-sets of real holomorphic maps defined
on real complex cells are symmetric.
Definition A.3. Let Cδ be a real complex cell. A cellular map f : C →
Cℓis real if each component of f is a real holomorphic function. A finite
collection {fj : Cδ′
j
→Cδ} of real cellular maps is a real cellular cover if
RC ⊂∪j(fj(R+Cj)).
As in [4], the statements of the sharp refinement theorem, the #CPT and
the #CPrT (Theorems 2.30, 2.38 and 2.40, respectively) all have real versions,
where we replace all complex cells, cellular maps and cellular covers in the
hypotheses and conclusions by their real counterparts (and in the case of the
#CPT, assume that the sets Zi are symmetric).
The small necessary modifications to the proofs are the same as in [4]. We
note that in Section 3.2.1 of the proof of the #CPT, when intersecting the sets
Zi with graphs of the constant functions p ∈εZ2 ⊂C, we may group these
graphs by conjugate pairs in order to ensure that the resulting intersections
and their projections are symmetric.
