Abstract
We revisit the complexity of verifying basic identities, such as associativity and distributivity,
on a given finite algebraic structure. In particular, while Rajagopalan and Schulman (FOCS’96,
SICOMP’00) gave a surprising randomized algorithm to verify associativity of an operation
⊙: S × S →S in optimal time O(|S|2), they left the open problem of finding any subcubic
algorithm for verifying distributivity of given operations ⊙, ⊕: S × S →S.
Our results are as follows:
1. We resolve the open problem by Rajagopalan and Schulman by devising an algorithm
verifying distributivity in strongly subcubic time O(|S|ω), together with a matching con-
ditional lower bound based on the Triangle Detection Hypothesis.
2. We propose arithmetic progression detection in small universes as a consequential algo-
rithmic challenge: We show that unless we can detect 4-term arithmetic progressions in a
set X ⊆{1, . . . , N} in time O(N 2−ϵ), then (a) the 3-uniform 4-hyperclique hypothesis is
true, and (b) verifying certain identities requires running time |S|3−o(1).
3. A careful combination of our algorithmic and hardness ideas allows us to fully classify a
natural subclass of identities: Specifically, any 3-variable identity over binary operations
in which no side is a subexpression of the other is either: (1) verifiable in randomized
time O(|S|2), (2) verifiable in randomized time O(|S|ω) with a matching lower bound from
triangle detection, or (3) trivially verifiable in time O(|S|3) with a matching lower bound
from hardness of 4-term arithmetic progression detection.
4. We obtain near-optimal algorithms for verifying whether a given algebraic structure forms
a field or ring, and show that counting the number of distributive triples is conditionally
harder than verifying distributivity.

Introduction
We consider fundamental questions about a given algebraic structure – which consists of a ground
set S together with one or more operations ⊕, ⊙, ⊗, etc. over S – such as: Does (S, ⊕) form a
group? Does (S, ⊕, ⊙) form a field? Does (S, ⊕, ⊙) form a ring? Here, we consider the case that S
is a finite set and each operation is binary, represented by its Cayley table.
Answering such questions essentially boils down to deciding whether (a collection of) certain
identities hold for given Cayley tables, such as the associative law (ab)c = a(bc) or the distributive
law a(b + c) = ab + ac. Interestingly, already verifying associativity of a given operation turns out
to be non-trivial: It was not until 1996 that Rajagopalan and Schulman [49] showed how to beat
the trivial O(n3)-time baseline by a surprising randomized algorithm that checks associativity with
constant probability in time O(n2), where n = |S|. (Note that since the Cayley table of a binary
operation on an n-element set S has size Θ(n2), this algorithm is optimal, albeit randomized.)
In particular, this leads to a randomized O(n2)-time algorithm to check whether (S, ⊕) forms a
group1. Using a different approach, a recent breakthrough result due to Evra, Gadot, Klein, and
Komargodski [25] obtains a deterministic O(n2)-time algorithm for this task (see Section 1.1.3
below for further details).
Rajagopalan and Schulman’s algorithm for verifying associativity generalizes to verifying any
read-once identity in optimal time, where a read-once identity is any identity with an arbitrary
number of operations and variables in which on each side of the equation, each variable appears
exactly once. However, the complexity of verifying non-read-once identities (most importantly,
distributivity) remains wide open.2 Note that verifying the distributive law is a key task, e.g., for
verifying whether a given structure (S, ⊕, ⊗) forms a field or a ring. We thus ask the following
driving question:
Is there a complexity dichotomy of “easy” identities, checkable in time O(n2),
“hard” identities, for which naive running time is (conditionally) best possible?
In light of Rajagopalan and Schulman’s work, the set of “easy” identities must contain all read-once
formulas. However, we are not aware of published superquadratic conditional lower bounds for any
identity.
1.1
Our Results
We focus on identities with three variables, but remark that some of our results can be generalized
to identities with four or more variables.

As one of our most interesting contributions, both conceptually and technically, we justify the
apparent hardness of this and related identities via a reduction from an algorithmic problem in
additive combinatorics: Specifically, let 4-AP Detection denote the problem of deciding whether a
set A ⊆{1, . . . , N} contains a non-trivial 4-term arithmetic progression a, a+b, a+2b, a+3b (where
b ̸= 0). We show that an algorithm verifying (2) in time O(n3−ϵ) would give an O(N2−ϵ′)-time

Arithmetic Progression Detection as a Fine-Grained Hypothesis.
k-term arithmetic pro-
gressions, or k-APs for short, are central objects in additive combinatorics. Many fascinating results
revolve around the study of k-AP-free sets, such as Roth’s and Szemer´edi’s seminal theorems [53].
We consider the same question from a computational perspective: How efficiently can we decide if
a given set A ⊆{1, . . . , N} is k-AP-free?
The naive algorithm runs in time O(N2) for any fixed k: Each combination of the first two
terms a1, a2 ∈A uniquely determines the remaining terms a3, . . . , ak (via ai = a1 +(i−1)(a2 −a1))

This jump in complexity from k = 3 to k = 4 is not a coincidence, but is actually mirrored in

intricate “higher-order Fourier analysis” [33]. We view this as one of many reasons (to be described

Hypothesis 1.3 (k-AP). There is no algorithm to detect a k-AP in a given set A ⊆{0, . . . , N} in
time O(N2−ϵ) (for any constant ϵ > 0).
In Section 5 we further justify the relevance of the k-AP Hypothesis for understanding hardness
in P, and argue that it deserves to be viewed as a new fine-grained hypothesis. In particular, we
establish k-AP Detection as an “arithmetic analogue” of the well-established Hyperclique Hypoth-
esis, review why current algorithmic approaches fail to break the k-AP Hypothesis, and point out
further connections to central problems like 4-SUM. We also develop a web of fine-grained reduc-
tions from 4-AP Detection (detailed in Sections 4 and 6) to interesting intermediate problems that
connect to verifying identities, our core interest of this paper.
Main Classification Theorem.
Equipped with the techniques of the results discussed above,
we are able to prove a classification theorem for a natural set of identities, specifically, all identities
in which neither side is a subexpression of the other.
Theorem 1.4 (Classification Theorem, informal version). Consider an identity f(a, b, c) = g(a, b, c)
where f is not a subexpression of g and vice versa. Each such identity falls into one of three regimes:
1. it can be checked in randomized time O(n2), or
2. it can be checked in randomized time O(nω); any O(nω−ϵ)-time algorithm would violate the
Triangle Detection Hypothesis;
3. it can (trivially) be checked in time O(n3); any O(n3−ϵ)-time algorithm would refute the 4-AP
Detection Hypothesis.
This theorem successfully answers our driving question for a large and interesting class of
identities. Curiously, what we initially expected to become a dichotomy turned out to be a natural
trichotomy.
In fact, we obtain more detailed results: Specifically, we can describe the set of identities in
the first regime as all formulas where either side can be written as H(G(a, b), c) for appropriate
choices of H, G and permutation of the variables.
This generalizes the result by Rajagopalan
and Schulman [49] which remained restricted to read-once identities. E.g., (c((ab)(ba)))(c + c) =
(ba) + (ab) lies in Regime 1, but is far from read-once.
Our results show that verifying any identity in Regime 3 in strongly subcubic time necessitates a
strongly subquadratic algorithm for 4-AP Detection. This means that either: (1) 4-AP Detection is
indeed quadratic-time hard, and we have fully settled the complexity for the most natural identities,
or (2) 4-AP Detection can be solved in strongly subquadratic time – in this case, the techniques
behind such an algorithm might lead the way to obtaining strongly subcubic algorithm for the
4-AP-hard identities. Ultimately, the authors remain agnostic about which case is more likely.

1.1.3
Further Results on Ring and Field Verification and Counting
A corollary of our O(nω)-time randomized algorithm for distributivity verification together with the
quadratic-time algorithms for group verification [49, 25] immediately yield O(nω) time algorithms
for verifying whether (S, ⊕, ⊙) forms a field (or ring, respectively). We improve over this baseline
by obtaining (almost-)optimal algorithms for these tasks:
Theorem 1.5. Given a set F consisting of n elements, together with binary operations ⊕, ⊙, there
exists a deterministic algorithm deciding if (F, ⊕, ⊙) forms a field in time O(n2).
Theorem 1.6. Given a set R consisting of n elements, together with binary operations ⊕, ⊙, there
exists a randomized algorithm deciding if (R, ⊕, ⊙) forms a ring in time O(n2).
We make use of a breakthrough result of Evra, Gadot, Klein, and Komargodski [25] which
constructs a basis for S of size O(
p
|S|) in time O(n2), where a basis of S is a set B such that
B2 = S (i.e., each element in S can be written as the product of two elements in B). In order to
find this basis, Evra et al. make use of the classification of finite simple groups.
Prior to this result, F.W. Light made a simple observation for the associativity checking problem
(see [21]). If we are given a smaller set of generators R ⊆S, it suffices to check associativity for all
a, c ∈S but only for b ∈R. We use a similar approach, leveraging the basis construction of Evra
et al., to show our results for field and ring verification.
Counting Variant.
We remark that our algorithms for verifying distributivity and related iden-
tities avoid (explicitly or implicitly) representing the full search space, fundamentally exploiting
that the identity needs to hold for all choices of a, b, c ∈S. We give evidence that this is a crucial
aspect, by showing that counting all distributive triples requires cubic running time assuming the
Strong Zero Triangle Hypothesis. This adds distributivity verification to the currently rather small
list of decision problems in P for which a fine-grained separation from its natural counting version
is known.
Theorem 1.7. Given a set S of n elements, together with binary operations ⊕, ⊙, if there exists
an algorithm that counts the number of distributive triples, i.e., the triples x, y, z ∈S such that
x ⊙(y ⊕z) = (x ⊙y) ⊕(x ⊙z) in time O(n3−ε) for some ε > 0, then the Strong Zero Triangle
Hypothesis is false.
1.2
Conclusion and Open Problems
Our main theorem establishes a trichotomy for a large natural subclass of identities. In particular,
the dividing line between identities verifiable in strongly subcubic time and identities requiring
cubic time is fully explained by the 4-AP Hypothesis. As such, the perhaps most pressing open
problem is to find further support or evidence against quadratic-time hardness of 4-AP Detection,
and we encourage readers to try to refute it.
Orthogonal to this, a natural question is to extend our classification to also settle the (possibly
slightly obscure) identities in which one side is a proper subexpression of the other. Furthermore,
it would be interesting to classify (subclasses of) identities involving four or more variables. Some
of our algorithmic and/or hardness ideas extend to such more complex formulas, however, a full
classification will likely require a classification into more than 3 regimes.
Finally, our hardness results on counting distributive triples suggests that a classification of the
natural counting problem over different identities will be substantially different from analyzing the
verification task, which has been the main focus of this work.

2.1
Algebraic Expressions
We consider algebraic expressions built up from variables and binary operations ⊙1, . . . , ⊙p. While
analyzing a concrete instance of such problem, we operate on a set S of n elements and operations
⊙1, . . . , ⊙p that operate on S (⊙i : S × S →S) and are provided as their Cayley tables (for each
of them we have table Ci ∈SS×S where Ci[a, b] = a ⊙i b for all a, b ∈S). All variables in the
expression can take any value from S. The algebraic expression itself is part of the description of
the problem and we assume it has constant size. Then, our algorithmic task is to take set S and all
the Cayley tables and return True or False, depending on whether the given property is satisfied
or not.
Every algebraic expression can be represented with a rooted, ordered binary tree with internal
nodes labeled with operations and leaves labeled with variables that can take values from S (see
Figure 1). We define depth(f) as the depth of the tree representing f (where a single-node tree,
e.g., a variable, has depth 0) and we say that expression f′(¯v) is a subexpression of f(¯v) iff the
tree representing f′ is a subtree of the tree representing f. For an expression f, we define var(f)
as the set of variables that f depends on (i.e., that appear as leaves in the tree representing f).
As there is a bijection between such trees and considered algebraic expressions, later we identify a
tree with its expression and vice versa. For two expressions f, g, we write f ≡g to denote that f
and g are symbolically equivalent expressions (in other words, f and g represent the same algebraic
expression). On the other hand, given a set S and the operations ⊙1, . . . , ⊙p as the Cayley tables,
we write f(x, y, z) = g(x, y, z) for some x, y, z ∈S, to denote that the evaluations of f and g on
the elements x, y, z yield the same element in S.

Technical Overview: Beyond Distributivity Checking
Beyond distributivity checking, our second main contribution is the following theorem, providing
the complexity classification of many identities into three regimes: (1) quadratic time, (2) Trian-
gle Detection time with matching upper and conditional lower bounds, and (3) cubic time with
matching lower bounds based on the 4-AP Hypothesis.
Theorem 4.1 (Trichotomy of Natural Identities). For any two expressions f(a, b, c), g(a, b, c),
such that f is not a subexpression of g and vice versa, given an algebraic structure (S, ⊕1, . . . , ⊕p),
checking if f(a, b, c) = g(a, b, c) for each a, b, c ∈S falls precisely in one of three regimes.
1. It can be checked in randomized O(n2) time if both f, g can be written as I(H(a, b), c) (or any
symmetric form), for some expressions H, I.
2. It can be checked in randomized O(nω) time if both f, g can be written as K(J(I(a, b), c), H(a, b))
(or any symmetric form), for some expressions H, I, J, K. Any O(nω−ε) algorithm for iden-
tities in this regime would refute Triangle Detection Hypothesis.
3. It can be checked (trivially) in O(n3) time. Any O(n3−ε) algorithm would refute the 4-AP
Detection Hypothesis.
In this section we give an overview of the main ideas behind this trichotomy: In Section 4.1
we quickly comment how our approach for distributivity checking extends to other identities in
regimes (1) and (2). Then, in Section 4.2, we explain how some selected identities from regime (3)
can be shown to be 4AP-hard. Finally, in Section 4.3 we show how to obtain the full classification
into the three regimes.
4.1
Subcubic Algorithms
As all identities on three variables can be verified with a brute-force approach in O(n3) time, we
need to focus only on the quadratic and triangle-time regimes.
It turns out that our approach for verifying Distributivity almost immediately generalizes to
verifying all identities of the form f(a, b, c) = g(a, b, c) where f and g are from the two sub-cubic
regimes. Again we reduce this question to verification of identity of polynomials defined separately
for f and g; and apply Schwartz–Zippel lemma. In order to evaluate the polynomials efficiently,
it suffices to put appropriate weights on edges in a 3-partite graph and count triangles or 2-paths,
which can be done in O(nω) or O(n2) time, respectively. We provide the details in Section 7.2.

Lower Bounds

Intermediate Problem: Square Detection.
The Square Detection problem is to decide if a
given 0-1-matrix M contains a square M(i, j) = M(i, j + k) = M(i + k, j + k) = M(i + k, j) = 1.
This problem was communicated to us by Adam Polak [48].
It turns out to be a convenient
intermediate problem in our reduction chain, but is arguably mathematically natural and interesting
in its own right. Square Detection can be solved in time O(n3) for n × n matrices (by enumerating
all triples i, j, k), and no faster algorithm is known. This is to be expected, as in the next paragraph
we show that it is 4-AP-hard.
Reduction from 4-AP Detection to Square Detection.
First observe that 4-AP and Square
Detection have different complexities, hence we cannot expect a reduction that transforms one 4-AP
instance into one equivalent Square Detection instance. Instead, it is reasonable to aim to reduce
a given 4-AP instance A ⊆{0, . . . , n} to N = √n Square Detection instances, each of size N × N.
If Square Detection is in subcubic time O(N3−ϵ), then such a reduction would imply that 4-AP
Detection is in subquadratic time O(N · N3−ϵ) = O(n2−ϵ/2), contradicting the 4-AP Hypothesis.
We now describe the idea to construct one such Square Detection instance. Suppose that there
is a labeling of the N × N grid, ℓ: N × N →{0, . . . , n}, which satisfies that labels of the corners
of every square form a 4-AP. Then consider the Square Detection instance M, where we place
1-entries at exactly those grid points whose labels appear in A. This construction is sound: If M is
a yes instance, then A contains a 4-AP. However, the construction is not complete-we only capture
the 4-APs in A that appear at the labels of the corners of some square.
Hence, to implement a complete reduction along these lines the crucial question is: Are there N
labelings such that every 4-AP in {0, . . . , n} appears as a square in at least one of these labelings?
To our initial surprise such labelings exist indeed, at least modulo some technical annoyances.
Though tricky, our final proof is unexpectedly (and pleasantly) short; see Section 6.

Strong Zero Triangle Hardness.
Finally, we outline how to show Theorems 1.2 and 1.7, our

Let Small-Weight Zero Triangle denote the problem of detecting a zero-weight triangle in an
n-node graph with edge weights in {−n, . . . , n}. The current best algorithm for detecting zero-
weight triangles in graphs with weights {−M, . . . , M} runs in time O(Mnω) [7], thus a reasonable
hypothesis is that Small-Weight Zero Triangle cannot be solved in subcubic time O(n3−ϵ). This is
known as the Strong Zero Triangle Hypothesis [2]. In Section Section A, we will review this and
other hardness hypotheses in detail.
Both theorems rely on the simple bookkeeping trick introduced previously. Let us first talk
about how to prove the hardness of the first identity ((a ⊙b) ⊕(a ⊙c)) ⊕(b ⊙c) = 0. The simple
idea of encoding edge weights into the operation x ⊙y works. In order to add the edge weights, we
define x ⊕y to return the sum of edge weights if they exist or some annihilator element otherwise.
We incorporate a second variable, which tracks how many times we performed the ⊕operation, so
that the final ⊕operation performs a last equality to zero check.
In order to show the small-weight zero triangle hardness of counting distributive triples, we
proceed as follows.
We want to show hardness of the task of counting x, y, z ∈S such that
x ⊙(y ⊕z) = (x ⊙y) ⊕(x ⊙z). We like to encode a zero triangle u, v, w ∈V in such a way that
xu, yv, zw ∈S form a distributive triple that is xu ⊙(yv ⊕zw) = (xu ⊙yv) ⊕(xu ⊙zw) holds. The
high level idea of the construction now consists of the left side of the distributivity equation to
equal the edge-weight sum c(u, v) + c(v, w) and the right hand side to equal −c(u, w). Clearly, this
happens when u, v, w are a zero-triangle. This can be again achieved with the bookkeeping trick,
with the drawback that we create some extra help-variables. We ensure that any triple x, y, z ∈S,
where at least one of x, y or z is a help-variable, will always be distributive. In the end it remains
to count, whether we have more distributive triples than the help-variables will inevitably produce.

The trivial O(n2)-time

5.1
Arithmetic Analogue of Hyperclique
A main argument for the plausibility of the k-AP Hypothesis is that it can be seen as the arith-
metic analogue of another well-established hardness assumption—the Hyperclique Hypothesis. To
appreciate this connection, let us take a moment to survey this analogy in a broader context.
Arithmetic versus Graph Problems.
A recurring phenomenon in fine-grained complexity is
that arithmetic problems (i.e., problems on sets or sequences of numbers) have natural counterparts
among graph or matrix problems. For example, two of the three primary fine-grained hypotheses—
the famous 3SUM and APSP Hypotheses—are part of this pattern: 3SUM4 is a fundamental
arithmetic problem [29], known to be tightly connected to the corresponding Zero Triangle5 problem
on graphs [55]. APSP6 is a fundamental graph problem, known to be fine-grained equivalent to Min-
Plus Product7, whose arithmetic analogue is called Min-Plus Convolution8 [22, 44]. This analogy
underlies many interesting fine-grained connections, which in particular led Zero Triangle and Min-
Plus Convolution to become hardness assumptions in their own right.
Many further instances
exist—for instance, between Min-Max Convolution and Min-Max Product (which is equivalent to
All-Pairs Bottleneck Paths) [54].
For all these pairs of problems the analogy is not just based on intuition, but is indeed sys-
tematic in the following sense. For one, known algorithms transfer from one setting to the other
up to mechanical changes (for instance, replacing FFT and fast matrix multiplication as the two
algebraic primitives for arithmetic and graph problems, respectively), Moreover, in all cases there
is a mechanical fine-grained reduction from the arithmetic problem to the corresponding graph
problem (that transforms sets or sequences of numbers into their Cayley graphs). This implication
is only known to be one-directional, though. In particular, arithmetic hardness assumptions are
generally (1) less plausible than their graph counterparts, but (2) have more useful implications.
Despite the missing formal equivalence, we are aware of no separation between the arithmetic
and graph variants for any corresponding pair.
Every known algorithm and lower bound syn-
chronously either applies to both variants (under mechnical adaptions) or to neither.
This is
widely regarded as heuristic evidence that corresponding pairs of arithmetic and graph problems
are at least morally equivalent.

AP Detection versus Hyperclique Detection.
The state of affairs for k-AP Detection and
(k, k −1)-Hyperclique detection is strikingly similar. First, both Hypotheses are wrong for k = 3:
3-AP Detection can be solved in near-linear time by the Fast Fourier Transform, whereas (3, 2)-
Hyperclique Detection (i.e., Triangle Detection in normal graphs) is in matrix multiplication
time O(nω). This is an instance of the natural correspondence between FFT and matrix mul-
tiplication discussed earlier.
Second, for k ≥4 both problems remain stuck at their brute-force running times. The same
algorithmic approaches fail for the same reasons, as we discuss further in Section 5.2.
Third, and most importantly, we establish a formal reduction from k-AP Detection to (k, k−1)-
Hyperclique Detection. Recall that this is in line with all other pairs of related problems, where
the arithmetic problem reduces to the graph problem.
Theorem 5.2. For every k ≥4, the k-AP Hypothesis implies the (k, k−1)-Hyperclique Hypothesis.
At a high level, this reduction follows the same mechanical idea used in other arithmetic-to-
graph reductions: transforming a set of numbers into its Cayley graph. The challenge is to find an
appropriate generalization to hypergraphs. Conceptually, our construction is inspired by Gowers’
proof [34] of Szemer´edi’s theorem.
Another notable connection is that the difficulty of both problems increases as the relevant
parameters grow: k for k-AP and h for (k, h)-Hyperclique. This is formalized in the following
theorem. Figure 2 illustrates the resulting web of known reductions.
Theorem 5.3. The k-AP Hypothesis implies the k′-AP Hypothesis for all k′ ≥k.
In summary, we have established that AP Detection is indeed the correct arithmetic analogue
of the Hyperclique Detection problem. For this reason alone the k-AP Hypothesis deserves its place
among the other fine-grained assumptions. One could even argue that it should be regarded as
morally equivalent to Hyperclique. Of course, there have been fewer explicit attempts to break the
k-AP Hypothesis, so it is fair to consider it with care.

Combinatorial Algorithms.
A very different approach is to consider purely combinatorial al-

structure that can be algorithmically exploited. This line of reasoning has led to surprising com-
binatorial algorithms for the related Triangle Detection problem [10, 12, 19, 56, 3], based on deep
regularity results in graphs [12, 3]. The fastest-known such algorithm runs in time n3/2Ω((log n)1/7).
This has direct consequences for 3-AP Detection: By the reduction from 3-AP Detection to
Triangle Detection ( Lemma 5.7), [3] implies a combinatorial n2/2Ω((log n)1/7)-time algorithm for
3-AP Detection. An alternative (marginally slower) n2/2Ω((log n)1/9)-time algorithm is to simply
run the brute-force O(|A|2)-time algorithm if |A| ≤n/2Ω((log n)1/9); otherwise the Kelley-Meka [39]
bounds for Roth’s theorem, refined in [15], imply that A must contain a 3-AP, so we can simply
report “yes”.9 In summary, currently there is no combinatorial algorithm in truly subquadratic-time
algorithms even for 3-AP Detection. Moreover, such a hypothetical subquadratic-time algorithm
could only follow along the lines of the first approach, as the Kelley-Meka bounds [39] for 3-AP-free
sets are almost tight [13].
For detecting 4-APs the state of affairs is even worse: The only improvements over the O(n2)-
time baseline are (sub-)logarithmic, either based on the simple “Four-Russians” trick mentioned
before, or similarly using Szemer´edi’s theorem [35] to avoid testing dense sets explicitly.
It is
conceivable that higher lower-order improvements can be achieved, perhaps by directly combining
the “Four-Russians” trick with “higher-order Fourier analysis” (a deep theory developed by Gow-
ers [33] to quantitatively improve Szemer´edi’s theorem, see e.g. the textbook [53]) in analogy to [12];
however, the involved quantitative bounds are still quite weak and would only lead to logarithmic
speed-ups, if any.
In summary, for a combinatorial algorithm to break the 4-AP Hypothesis one would have to
overcome two barriers: (1) To extend from 3-APs to 4-APs (i.e., from graphs to hypergraphs)
without quantitative losses, which likely involves extending the Kelley-Meka bounds to 4-AP-free
sets, and (2) to achieve truly subquadratic-time algorithms for 3-AP Detection (i.e., to improve
the quasi-polynomial speed-up to polynomial). Barrier (1) already appears hard, but (2) seems
completely out of reach for now.
A New Approach?
Currently, both algebraic and combinatorial methods fail to refute the k-
AP Hypothesis. Of course it is conceivable that a radically different (perhaps hybrid) approach
could exist. While we cannot rule out this possibility, we consider it likely that such an alternative
approach would analogously be applicable to the Hyperclique problem as well.

Algebraic Identity Checking Beyond Distributivity
In this section we aim to classify the complexity of all natural algebraic identities on three variables,
namely all algebraic identities that can be written as f(a, b, c) = g(a, b, c) such that neither f is a
subexpression of g nor g of f. Our strategy is to first consider an intermediate problem that we call
Constant Term Identity problem, that corresponds to the special case when g(a, b, c) is a constant
function and show that we can fully classify this intermediate problem. We then show how we can
use this special case to show a more general classification. In the next section we introduce the
Constant Term Identity problem and establish complexity of all possible expressions f on three
variables.
7.1
Complexity of Constant Term Identity Problem
We first recall the Constant Term Identity problem.
Definition 7.1 (Constant Term Identity problem). Consider an algebraic expression f(a, b, c) over
operations ⊙1, . . . , ⊙p. Given a set S, Cayley tables for ⊙1, . . . , ⊙p : S ×S →S the Constant Term
Identity problem is to decide if for all a, a′, b, b′, c, c′ ∈S it holds that f(a, b, c) = f(a′, b′, c′).
Throughout this section we distinguish a single element of S that is the result of evaluation of
f(a, b, c) for some triple a, b, c ∈S and we denote this element as ∞. Then the goal is to verify if
f(a, b, c) = ∞for all a, b, c ∈S and often we will use that statement of the Constant Term Identity
problem. We note that we do not assume any properties of the Cayley tables regarding ∞, i.e., it
may be the case that ∞⊙i a ̸= ∞for some a ∈S. However, for some auxiliary operations that we
introduce, we will treat ∞differently than other elements of S.
The goal of this section is to prove the following trichotomy theorem.
Theorem 7.2 (Trichotomy of the Constant Term Identity Problem). Let f(a, b, c) be an algebraic
expression over operations ⊙1, . . . , ⊙p. Then the following holds.

such that f can be written as f(a, b, c) ≡H(G(a, b), c) (for some permutation of a, b, c), then
we can solve the Constant Term Identity Problem on f in time O(n2).

Intuitively, this theorem tells us that we have three regimes in which solving Constant Term

1. Embedding subexpressions into our operations

1. We first construct a gadget that we call Subexpression Embedding Gadget. Intuitively, we embed

(i) Consistency: the embedded instance will be equivalent to the original instance.
(ii) Small size: the size of the embedded instance is (up to a constant factor) the same as the size
of the original instance.
(iii) Bookkeeping: each performed operation in the parse tree of f additionally keeps the history
of all previously performed operations.
2. We then consider a generalization of our problem, namely the Multichromatic Constant Term
Identity problem, where on top of the Cayley tables of relevant operations, our input also consists
of subsets A, B, C of our universe S, and we want to decide if f(x, y, z) evaluates to a fixed constant
for all x ∈A, y ∈B, z ∈C. We can then use the Subexpression Embedding Gadget to show that
these two problems are equivalent in a sense that a fast algorithm for one implies a fast algorithm

Definition 7.11 (Multichromatic Constant Term Identity). Let f(a, b, c) be a fixed algebraic ex-

Hardness Assumptions
In this section, we give an overview of the hardness hypotheses we use throughout the paper. We
work with the standard Word-RAM model with O(log n)-bit words.
Given a graph G with n vertices, the Triangle Detection problem is to decide if G contains a
triple of pairwise adjacent vertices. A simple reduction to matrix multiplication allows us to solve
Triangle Detection in graphs with n vertices in time O(nω) [36]. Despite extensive effort, so far no
algorithm is known that solves Triangle Detection in time O(nω−ϵ) for any ϵ > 0. This prompted
the following hypothesis which serves as a tool to prove the hardness of many related problems,
e.g., [5, 40, 20, 55] and constitutes a prominent hypothesis in fine-grained complexity theory.
Hypothesis A.1 (Triangle Detection Hypothesis). Triangle Detection cannot be solved in O(nω−ε)
for any ϵ > 0.
Another central fine-grained complexity problem is the zero-triangle problem, which essentially
asks if a given weighted graph contains a triangle whose edge weights sum up to zero.
Problem A.2 (Zero Triangle). Given a tripartite weighted graph G = (X ∪Y ∪Z, E, w) with n
vertices in each part, and weight function w : E →[−M, M], the Zero Triangle Detection problem
is to decide if G contains a triangle of weight equal to 0, i.e., do there exist x ∈X, y ∈Y, z ∈Z
such that w(x, y) + w(y, z) + w(z, x) = 0?
Zero Triangle takes a special position in the web of fine-grained reductions as a cubic-time
problem known to be harder than both the seminal 3SUM and APSP problems [55]. For graphs
with small weights bounded by M, a faster ˜O(Mnω)-time algorithm is known [7]. Note that for
weights of size M ≥n3−ω−o(1), this algorithm takes cubic time as well. For this reason, Abboud,
Bringmann, Khoury and Zamir introduced the Strong Zero Triangle Conjecture [2, Conjecture
7.5], essentially postulating that Zero Triangle requires time min{Mn2, n3}1−o(1) for essentially any
choice of M, matching the upper bound if ω = 2. For our purposes, we will use the following
formulation:12

(1) Reduction to Matrix Product Equality Verification.
Roughly speaking, in this part
we want to represent our operations ⊕, ⊙on the set S by matrices, which will allow us to construct
a matrix equation that corresponds to checking distributivity on a subset of S. To this end, we first
label each element in S uniquely by a column vector from {0, 1}n and then for each a ∈S we can
construct a linear map (represented by a matrix) that maps each vector corresponding to x ∈S to
the vector corresponding to a ⊕x (resp. a ⊙x). More formally, we first order the elements in S
arbitrarily and let λ : S →{0, 1}n be the labeling function that maps i-th element from S to the
column vector whose i-th entry is equal to 1 and all other entries are zeros. For each a ∈S define
the maps sa, pa : λ(S) →λ(S) as follows.
sa : λ(x) 7→λ(a ⊕x)
pa : λ(x) 7→λ(a ⊙x).
Since λ is an injective function, we get the following observation.
Observation B.2. Let a, b ∈S be fixed. Then for any c ∈S, the identity a⊙(b⊕c) = (a⊙b)⊕(a⊙c)
holds if and only if (pa ◦sb)(c) = (sa⊙b ◦pa)(c) holds.
This observation gives us a nice way to think about distributivity checking in terms of functions
on real vectors, rather than arbitrary algebraic structures. More precisely, in order to show that
for all a, b, c ∈S the identity a ⊙(b ⊕c) = (a ⊙b) ⊕(a ⊙c) is satisfied, it suffices to show that for
all a, b ∈S the maps (pa ◦sb) and (sa⊙b ◦pa) are the same. This is particularly useful, since we
can now prove that for each a ∈S there exists a matrix σa (resp. πa), such that for any x ∈S
σa · λ(x) = sa (λ(x)). Hence, we only need to prove that for each a, b the matrices (πa · σb) and
(σ(a⊙b) · πa) are equal. Formally, we prove the following lemma.
Lemma B.3. Let a ∈S be arbitrary. Let σa be a matrix whose i-th column is equal to λ(a ⊕x),
where x is the i-th element in the ordering of S. Define πa analogously. Then
1. For any x ∈S σa · λ(x) = sa (λ(x)).
2. For any x ∈S πa · λ(x) = pa (λ(x)).
Proof. Let x be the i-th element in ordering of S. Then λ(x) has all zeros except in row i where
it has a 1. Hence, σa · λ(x) (resp. πa · λ(x)) is precisely equal to the i-th column of σa (resp. πa),
which is by definition equal to λ(a ⊕x) = sa (λ(x)) (resp. λ(a ⊙x) = pa (λ(x))).
The following corollary now follows from the previous lemma together with the injectivity of λ.
Corollary B.4. Let a, b ∈S be arbitrary. Then the identity a ⊙(b ⊕c) = (a ⊙b) ⊕(a ⊙c) is
satisfied for all c ∈S if and only if the equality πa · σb = σ(a⊙b) · πa is satisfied.
This gives us a rather straightforward reduction to Matrix Product Equality Verification: for
every pair a, b construct the matrices πa, σb, σ(a⊙b) and test the equality of the matrix product
πa · σb = σ(a⊙b) · πa. Unfortunately however, this yields too many instances of the Matrix Product
Equality Verification problem. The next part of the algorithm is dedicated to resolving this problem
by introducing a random weight for each element of S and then instead of checking each matrix
product we sum up the weighted matrices and show that (with high probability) the answer to
the scaled version of Matrix Product Equality Verification is equal to the answer of the original
problem. This part of the algorithm is closely related to the algorithm for associativity testing by
Rajagopalan and Schulman [49].
