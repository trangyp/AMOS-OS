Why That Robot? A Qualitative Analysis of Justification Strategies for
Robot Color Selection Across Occupational Contexts
Jiangen He1, Wanqi Zhang1, and Jessica K. Barfield2
Abstract— As
robots
increasingly
enter
the
workforce,
human-robot interaction (HRI) must address how implicit social
biases influence user preferences. This paper investigates how
users rationalize their selections of robots varying in skin tone
and anthropomorphic features across different occupations.
By qualitatively analyzing 4,146 open-ended justifications from
1,038 participants, we map the reasoning frameworks driving
robot color selection across four professional contexts. We
developed and validated a comprehensive, multidimensional
coding scheme via human–AI consensus (κ = 0.73). Our results
demonstrate that while utilitarian Functionalism is the dom-
inant justification strategy (52%), participants systematically
adapted these practical rationales to align with established
racial and occupational stereotypes. Furthermore, we reveal
that bias frequently operates beneath conscious rationalization:
exposure to racial stereotype primes significantly shifted partic-
ipants’ color choices, yet their spoken justifications remained
masked by standard affective or task-related reasoning. We
also found that demographic backgrounds significantly shape
justification strategies, and that robot shape strongly modulates
color interpretation. Specifically, as robots become highly an-
thropomorphic, users increasingly retreat from functional rea-
soning toward Machine-Centric de-racialization. Through these
empirical results, we provide actionable design implications
to help reduce the perpetuation of societal biases in future
workforce robots.
I. INTRODUCTION
As robots increasingly enter the workforce across diverse
occupational sectors, the potential for human biases to influ-
ence robot selection has become a critical topic of discussion
within the human-robot interaction (HRI) community. For
HRI, fundamental questions have emerged regarding whether
human biases: (i) influence human-robot selection decisions,
(ii) shape the strategies users employ when choosing robots
for different occupations, and (iii) implicate ethical concerns
when users select robots that vary by racial characteristics
such as skin tone and anthropomorphic features.
In our previous studies exploring how occupational con-
texts shape robot selection, we examined whether societal
biases influenced choices in construction, healthcare, educa-
tional, and athletic domains [15]. Our research was guided
by Social Role Theory [10], which posits that stereotypes
emerge from observable patterns of group membership across
different occupational roles, and the Stereotype Content
Model [13], which states that social perceptions are formed
along two core dimensions: warmth (trustworthiness) and

While our previous research documented these selection

employs a qualitative approach, analyzing over 4,000 open-
ended responses to examine the reasons behind these selec-

psychological mechanisms that guide robot selection across

psychological research demonstrating that stereotypes often

coding process, we first created a scheme to identify the

concluded with design guidelines for the selection of robots

II. RELATED WORK
A. Social Categorization and Robot Racialization
Emerging literature demonstrates that humans project so-
cial categories onto robots. Research has shown that humans
display racial bias toward robots in shooter-bias tasks [1, 3],
and robot skin tone interacts with user prejudice to shape
attributions of agency [12]. Sparrow argues that race, as
a social construction, inevitably extends to robots, carrying
significant ethical implications [26]. These studies indicate
that HRI is not immune to social categorization, providing a
foundation for examining how users perceive and categorize
robots based on visual cues.
B. Occupational Contexts and Role-Based Stereotypes
Occupational roles are potent sources of stereotype ac-
tivation. Social role theory argues that stereotypes emerge
from observed occupational distributions [17], while the
Stereotype Content Model (SCM) shows systematic asso-
ciations between social categories and occupational roles
along competence and warmth dimensions [8, 22]. Empirical
evidence reveals stereotypical linkages: Latinos with manual
labor, Asians with academic competence, Blacks with ath-
letic ability, and Whites with professional roles [17, 22, 23].
In HRI, such contexts may prime users to expect certain
robotic appearances that align with these established human
stereotypes.
C. Anthropomorphism and Human-Likeness
Anthropomorphism determines how strongly social cat-
egories influence user perceptions. While anthropomorphic
design can increase trust, it also amplifies the salience of
social category cues, such as skin tone [6, 20]. Physical
human-likeness interacts with warmth and competence cues
to shape robot evaluations [4]. Specifically, anthropomorphic
features can trigger deeper social judgments, making robots
more vulnerable to stereotype-based evaluations when occu-
pational priming is present [9, 12].
D. Interpreting Appearance Cues in Human–Robot Interac-
tion
Beyond social categorization, robot appearance can also
shape how users interpret a robot’s role and capabilities
within a given context. Research in design and social
perception suggests that visual features often function as
heuristic cues that people use to infer the intended purpose
and functional properties of technological artifacts [18]. In
human–robot interaction, appearance cues such as form,
color, and material can influence perceptions of competence,
professionalism, and task suitability. For example, studies of
color perception show that darker colors are often associated
with durability and strength, whereas lighter colors are linked
to cleanliness and approachability in service contexts [11,
14]. These associations suggest that users may interpret
robot appearance not only through social categories but also
through contextual expectations about how a robot should
look in a particular occupational setting.
III. METHODS
A. Study Design and Participants
This paper analyzes open-ended response data from two
experiments described in detail in [15]. Study 1 employed
a 4 × 5 mixed factorial design (N = 421): task scenario
(four professional contexts) was a within-subjects factor, and
human-likeness level (five levels) was a between-subjects
factor. On each trial, participants read a short written scenario
and viewed a matching picture of the task setting that did
not depict people, isolating baseline contextual associations
without racial priming. They then selected one robot from the
six color options at their assigned human-likeness level and
provided an open-ended written justification for that choice.
Study 2 added a racial priming manipulation, employing
a 2 × 4 × 5 mixed factorial design (N = 617). Task scenario
and priming condition (stereotype vs. non-stereotype) were
within-subjects factors: every participant completed all four
tasks, two under Stereotype priming and two under Non-
stereotype priming, with random assignment of priming type
to task. Human-likeness level was a between-subjects factor.
The priming manipulation embedded human professional
imagery in the task scenario picture, investigating how racial
representations shape robot selection decisions. Stereotype
priming paired each task with a human professional from a
racial background commonly associated with that occupation
in popular representations (construction: Latino; hospital:
White; tutoring: Asian; sports: Black). Non-stereotype prim-
ing used less typical pairings for the same tasks (e.g., White
construction, Black hospital, Latino tutoring, Asian sports).
Participants were recruited from the United States through
Prolific, with quota sampling targeting balanced distributions
by gender and race. In both studies, participants selected
one of six robots varying in skin tone (Light, Medium,
Brown, Dark) and non-skin-tone colors (Silver, Teal) within
their assigned human-likeness level, across four professional
task scenarios: construction site, hospital, home tutoring, and
sports field. After each selection, participants provided an
open-ended explanation for their choice. The present analysis
examines these 4,146 open-ended responses.
B. Codebook Development
To systematically analyze the open-ended justifications,
we developed a multidimensional coding scheme using an
inductive thematic approach. The process followed three
iterative phases.
a) Phase 1: Stratified Sampling.: From the 4,146 re-
sponses, we constructed a representative training subset of
N = 500 (n = 250 per study). We employed stratified
sampling based on task scenario, robot color, and human-
likeness to ensure that the codebook captured the full breadth

of reasoning, including minority choices and diverse occu-
pational contexts.
b) Phase 2: Open Coding and Constant Comparison.:
The sampled responses underwent line-by-line open coding
to identify recurring concepts and descriptive labels (e.g.,
“dirt concealment,” “professionalism”). Following the Con-
stant Comparative Method [24], we iteratively compared new
data incidents with existing codes to refine definitions and
establish conceptual boundaries.
c) Phase 3: Axial Coding and Saturation.: In the final
stage, these codes were synthesized through axial coding into
higher-level thematic clusters representing distinct reasoning
strategies. This iterative process continued until theoretical
saturation was achieved—the point at which no new cat-
egories emerged from the data. The finalized codebook,
featuring precise definitions and anchor examples, served
as the framework for the subsequent multi-label coding of
the full dataset. The codebook captures a spectrum from
practical reasoning (C1: Functionalism) through affective
responses (C2: Psych/Affective) to explicit social mapping
(C5: Identity/Social). C3 (Machine-Centric) captures a dis-
tinctive response pattern in which participants actively resist
the humanization of robots, while C4 (Preference/Evasion)
captures low-effort or avoidant responses. Table I presents
the five main categories and fifteen sub-categories.
C. AI-Assisted Coding
Given the scale of the dataset (N = 4,146), we employed
an AI-assisted coding approach using Google’s Gemini API
(model gemini-3.1-pro-preview in our implementa-
tion). The system instruction, including the embedded code-
book, appears in Appendix. Each response was supplied in
a separate user message, Participant’s reason for
choosing the robot: ‘‘...’’, and the model re-
turned structured JSON with exactly one main category and
one sub-category and was set temperature=0.
D. Validation
We validated the AI coding through inter-rater reliability
analysis on a stratified sample of 100 responses coded
independently by three human coders (the authors of the
paper, two faculty members and one graduate student) and
the AI system.
Initial reliability at the main category level yielded Co-
hen’s κ values of 0.66 (Coder 1 vs. Coder 2, 78% agreement),
0.61 (Coder 1 vs. AI, 75% agreement), and 0.73 (Coder 2
vs. AI, 83% agreement). At the sub-category level, reliability
was slightly lower: κ = 0.60 (Coder 1 vs. Coder 2, 64%
agreement), 0.60 (Coder 1 vs. AI, 64% agreement), and
0.72 (Coder 2 vs. AI, 75% agreement). To establish a
definitive human baseline, discrepancies between the two
human coders were resolved by a third independent coder.
Comparing this reconciled human consensus against the AI
output demonstrated strong alignment, yielding κ = 0.73
(83% agreement) at the main category level and κ = 0.69
(72% agreement) at the sub-category level. Based on stan-

E. Analysis
We employed a mixed-methods approach to analyze the
coded justifications. For the quantitative component, we com-
puted frequency distributions and proportional breakdowns.
Statistical associations were tested using Pearson’s χ2 tests
of independence on contingency tables, with Cram´er’s V
as the effect size measure. Complementing this, the qual-
itative component involved targeted thematic analyses of
sub-categories with particular interest. By examining their
distributions across demographics, task contexts, and robot
characteristics, and supplementing the statistical findings
with illustrative participant quotes, this dual approach maps
broad statistical patterns while preserving the nuanced con-
texts of user rationalizations.
IV. RESULTS
A. RQ1: The Landscape of Justification
The distribution of reasoning frameworks across all 4,146
coded responses reveals a clear hierarchy (Table II; see
the Appendix for detailed category explanations and rep-
resentative quotes). Functionalism dominated, accounting
for over half of all justifications (52.2%). Psych/Affective
was the second most common framework (22.0%), fol-
lowed by Preference/Evasion (14.5%) and Machine-Centric
(10.2%). Identity/Social comprised only 1.2% of responses.
Machine-Centric responses skewed toward non-skin-tone
choices (27.1% skin-tone) and Identity/Social responses
skewed toward skin-tone robots (85.4%). Figure 1 presents
the frequency distribution at the sub-category level.

To probe whether the specific type of racial prime in-
fluenced reasoning, we analyzed the primed subset from
Study 2 (N = 2,464) by comparing trials based on their spe-
cific priming condition. Specifically, we compared stereotype
priming trials versus Non-stereotype priming trials. As Fig-
ure 5 (top row) illustrates, the overall category distributions
are similar between the two conditions across all four tasks.
However, the color of the robot actually selected dif-
fered markedly if we focus on the prime-aligned choices.
We defined a choice as prime-aligned when the selected
robot color matched the skin tone associated with the
primed racial group in the scenario pictures (Latino→Brown,
Black→Dark, White→Light, Asian→Medium). For exam-
ple, if the scenario depicted a Latino construction worker, a
choice of a Brown robot was considered prime-aligned. Un-
der stereotype-consistent priming, 19.2% of selections were
prime-aligned, compared with only 6.9% under stereotype-
inconsistent priming ( p < .001, V = 0.18).
In Figure 5 (bottom row), the comparison reveals a
sharp increase in prime-aligned choices under stereotype-
consistent priming across every task, which is consistent
with the companion quantitative study [15]. The effect of
stereotype priming was demonstrated in all scenarios, but the
justifications underlying these prime-aligned choices adapted
specifically to the task context. A qualitative examination of
the prime-aligned choices reveals how stereotype priming
increased the frequency of prime-aligned choices.
a) Construction: In the construction scenario, stereo-
type priming (Latino) generated more prime-aligned selec-
tions (Brown robots) than non-stereotype priming (White →
Light robots). Both groups relied heavily on Functionalism,
but their functional rationales adapted to the color chosen.
Participants in the non-stereotype condition justified their
Light robot selections through general performance metrics:
“The robot looks strong and durable” (White prime, Light
robot). In contrast, participants selecting Brown robots under
stereotype priming seamlessly linked the darker color to the
reality of construction work: “With the light brown color
it won’t look as dirty when covered in dirt and dust...” or
invoked visibility norms: “Orange is something workers will
be able to easily identify on site” (Latino prime, Brown
robot). Stereotype priming thus shifted the functional criteria
from abstract capability to material resilience and visibility.

V. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS
Although Functionalism was the primary justification for
robot selection (52%), its application was highly adaptable,
serving as a post-hoc rationalization for underlying bias.
Participants justified selecting darker robots in construction
for “hiding dirt” and lighter robots in hospitals as appearing
“sterile.” This dynamic illustrates a dual-process model:
unacknowledged stereotypes drive the selection, while the
deliberative cognitive system generates task-based excuses.
Viewed through the theories of Aversive Racism [16] and
the Justification-Suppression Model [7], Functionalism pro-
vides a non-racial pretext for stereotype-aligned choices.
Consequently, if we rely solely on explicit rationales, we
risk concluding that human-robot selections are objective,
missing the reality that structural bias often speaks the
language of practicality.
Furthermore, our findings complicate simplistic appli-
cations of Social Identity Theory [27] and racial mirror-
ing in human-agent interaction. Our intersectional analysis
reveals this is not the case for all marginalized groups.
White and Asian participants demonstrated elevated affec-
tive reasoning when selecting matching (Light or Medium)
robots, yet Black participants who selected Dark robots
justified their choices overwhelmingly through Functionalism
(75.2%) rather than affective ties. This disparity suggests
that identifying with a dark-skinned entity in a society
with anti-Black systemic biases is structurally different from
identifying with a light-skinned one. The lack of affective
mirroring from Black participants may reflect historical re-
alities where darker skin has been systematically stripped of
“warmth” in cultural narratives, forcing a heavier reliance on
“competence”.
Finally,
as
robot
human-likeness
increased,
partici-
pants often retreated from functional evaluations toward
Machine-Centric reasoning (e.g., choosing metallic/teal col-
ors). This shift highlights the tension of anthropomorphic
racialization: while utilitarian colors are easily justified
on less anthropomorphic machines (color-as-paint), highly
anthropomorphic forms render the finish inextricably hu-
man (color-as-race), making functional evaluations ethically
fraught. Experiencing racial anxiety, users attempt to circum-


## Page 8


vent social categorization by selecting non-skin-tone finishes
to socially neutralize the robot. This impulse parallels Color-
Blind Racial Ideology [5], wherein individuals attempt to
“erase” race entirely in pursuit of an artificially objective,
“neutral” machine.
These findings yield three critical implications for social
robotics. First, practitioners must critically interrogate “func-
tional” requests. Requests for practical colors (e.g., brown to
“hide dirt” in construction) often mask implicit bias, and
accepting them blindly risks hardcoding prejudice. Second,
designers face an “anthropomorphism threshold”: assigning
human skin tones to high-fidelity robots inevitably triggers
racial categorization. To achieve a universally “neutral”
agent, explicitly non-human finishes (e.g., metallic silver)
are required. Finally, our intersectional findings challenge the
“one-size-fits-all” approach to customization. While simply
offering diverse skin tones (akin to digital emojis) might
appear to guarantee universal affective bonding, systemic
biases fundamentally alter how people interact with racially
congruent machines. Our findings show that “mirroring” is
an unequal experience. Because Black participants relied
heavily on “competence/functionalism” rather than affective
warmth when choosing matching robots, systemic biases
might alter how people interact with racially congruent
machines. Therefore, simply offering a “Dark mode” robot
isn’t enough to foster trust. Designers may need to consider
the socio-historical context in which the robot operates.
Our analysis reveals that robot selection is deeply entan-
gled with societal structures, with users frequently relying on
functional reasoning to mask underlying occupational biases.
Furthermore, as anthropomorphism increases, participants
experience racial anxiety, often retreating to non-skin-tone
colors to artificially neutralize the machine. We also chal-
lenge the assumption that racially congruent robots guarantee
affective bonding, demonstrating that systemic biases struc-
turally alter how marginalized groups interpret these agents.
Ultimately, assigning appearance to a social robot is never
a purely aesthetic choice; it is a profound socio-technical
intervention requiring intentional ethical design.
