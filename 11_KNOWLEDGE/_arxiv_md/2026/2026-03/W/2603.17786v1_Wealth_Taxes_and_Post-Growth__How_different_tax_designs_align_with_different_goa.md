Abstract 
Wealth taxes are a frequently proposed policy within the post-growth literature, but evaluaƟons of 
their alignment with post-growth goals, and empirical esƟmates of their potenƟal eﬀects, are lacking. 
We contribute to this literature by examining the extent to which diﬀerent wealth-tax designs can 
contribute to four goals of a post-growth transiƟon: redistribuƟng wealth; eradicaƟng extreme wealth; 
curbing rent-seeking; and reducing CO2 emissions. The analysis is based on microsimulaƟon modelling, 
using household-level data from 18 countries of the 2017 EU Household Finance and ConsumpƟon 
Survey. Our analysis ﬁnds that taxes on net wealth are the most progressive and redistribuƟve, while 
taxes on ﬁnancial and investment property wealth tend to be more eﬀecƟve at addressing rent-
seeking. However, we also idenƟfy trade-oﬀs and conﬂicts between diﬀerent tax designs and goals. As 
a result, a broader package of policies will be necessary to navigate these conﬂicts and miƟgate the 
limitaƟons inherent in any single wealth-tax design. 
 
Keywords: post-growth; degrowth; sustainable welfare; wealth taxes; wealth inequality; welfare 
states; growth-dependence; eco-social policy.

1. IntroducƟon 
Humanity faces a polycrisis (Lawrence et al., 2024) – a set of intertwined and mutually reinforcing 
crises – including climate and ecological breakdown as well as extreme, deepening inequality. Six of 
the nine planetary boundaries have now been transgressed (Sakschewski et al., 2025), and wealth 
ownership is becoming ever-more concentrated (PikeƩy and Saez, 2014; PikeƩy and Zucman, 2014; 
European Environmental Bureau, 2024). AƩempts to tackle these tendencies from within the growth 
paradigm appear increasingly inadequate, due to insuﬃcient decoupling of GDP growth from 
environmental harm (Haberl et al., 2020; Vadén et al., 2020; Vogel and Hickel, 2023) and the failure 
of growth to reduce inequality (Hickel, 2017; Christensen et al., 2023). As a result, post-growth – the 
science of well-being within planetary boundaries (Kallis et al., 2025) – is exploring alternaƟves to the 
goal of conƟnuous economic expansion. Instead, it focuses on policies that prioriƟse the enhancement 
of human well-being and social equity within planetary boundaries (O’Neill et al., 2018), while also 
promoƟng a more emancipatory poliƟcs (Laruﬀa, 2025).  
In the current growth-oriented capitalist poliƟcal economy, social outcomes related to well-being are 
widely perceived to depend on ever-increasing GDP and producƟvity (Büchs and Koch, 2019). In 
response, sustainable welfare has emerged as a key topic within eco-social policy and post-growth 
research (Koch and Hansen, 2024), exploring how welfare states can contribute to universal and 
sustainable need saƟsfacƟon (Hirvilammi and Koch, 2020; Büchs et al., 2024a). Welfare state funding, 
however, is typically framed as relying on income and consumpƟon taxes and income-related social 
security contribuƟons. The size of these revenue sources tends to be coupled to changes in economic 
output (Raphael et al., 2024; Büchs et al., 2024a), which would likely contract in a post-growth 
scenario in the Global North. 
Post-growth welfare funding is therefore one of the ‘boƩleneck quesƟons’ in eco-social policy and 
sustainable welfare research (Bohnenberger, 2023). A range of ﬁscal and monetary proposals for a 
post-growth context has been explored (Jackson and Jackson, 2021; Olk et al., 2023), and among these, 
taxes on wealth have received growing aƩenƟon (Bailey, 2015; Hickel et al., 2022; Koch, 2022). Because 
wealth represents a stock rather than a ﬂow, some authors argue that it may be more resilient to 
economic ﬂuctuaƟons in GDP (Koch, 2022; Büchs et al., 2024a). Another prominent argument for 
wealth taxaƟon in the post-growth context is that inequality is likely to increase without growth and 
targeted intervenƟon (PikeƩy and Saez, 2014; Jackson and Victor, 2016). Wealth taxes could therefore 
be an important measure for reducing inequality in a post-growth context (Murphy, 2023; Kallis et al., 
2025; Büchs et al., 2024a). 
Depending on underlying assumpƟons about the mechanisms of government spending, wealth taxes 
are understood to either directly raise revenue for welfare spending (e.g. Buch-Hansen and Koch, 2019; 
Fitzpatrick et al., 2022; Engler et al., 2024) or – following Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) – to 
operate primarily by removing money from circulaƟon, thereby reducing aggregate demand and 
helping maintain price stability, which in turn creates macroeconomic space for public spending (Olk 
et al., 2023). In both cases, wealth taxes play an important role, and both perspecƟves recognise their 
signiﬁcance for distribuƟve jusƟce as well as for shaping incenƟves and behaviours that inﬂuence the 
allocaƟon of real resources.  
In addiƟon, ecological and post-Keynesian macro-economists argue that wealth taxes can act as 
macroeconomic stabilisers during or aŌer a post-growth transiƟon. For example, Cahen-Fourot and 
Lavoie (2016) show that posiƟve net proﬁt and interest rates are compaƟble with a zero-growth 
economy if accumulated wealth is suﬃciently recirculated – through consumpƟon or other

2. Literature review 
Recurrent taxes on net wealth were once more common in Europe. In 1990, twelve OECD countries 
levied taxes on net wealth, while today only three sƟll do so (OECD, 2018). The widespread repeal of 
wealth taxes formed part of a broader trend of tax reducƟons that began in the 1980s (OECD, 2018). 
However, rising wealth concentraƟon and perceived pressures on public ﬁnances (O’Donovan, 2021) 
have renewed calls for their reintroducƟon, both within and beyond post-growth debates. SecƟon 2.1 
summarises the exisƟng empirical research on wealth taxes, and secƟon 2.2 outlines how they could 
contribute to post-growth goals. The literature review focusses on net wealth taxes, primarily because 
the overwhelming majority of exisƟng empirical studies do the same.  
2.1 Empirical wealth tax research: eﬀects on distribuƟon, revenue, and 
emissions 
There are surprisingly few academic studies that examine how a wealth tax aﬀects the distribuƟon of 
wealth. Apostel and O’Neill (2022) model the eﬀects of diﬀerent one-oﬀ wealth tax proposals in 
Belgium and ﬁnd that the wealth share held by the top 1% could decrease by 0.16 to 0.84 percentage 
points (from 23.9%), and the wealth share held by the top 5% by 0.12 to 0.91 percentage points (from 
42.2%), depending on the tax proposal and levels of avoidance. MarƟ et al. (2023) analyse diﬀerences 
in wealth inequality and wealth tax design across Swiss Cantons and ﬁnd that a 0.1% reducƟon in top 
marginal annual wealth tax rates results in an increase of 0.9 percentage points in the wealth share of 
the top 1%, and 1.2 percentage points for the top 0.1%, ﬁve years aŌer the reform. However, the 
authors note that misreporƟng of wealth and intra-country mobility are likely to inﬂate these results.

Since wealth is unevenly distributed along gender, generaƟonal, and geographical lines (Parkes and

Some authors have esƟmated the revenue that a wealth tax could generate across Europe. Apostel

(2022) use the wealth inequality-CO2 emissions elasƟcity from Knight et al. (2017) to esƟmate that a 
one-oﬀ wealth tax in Belgium could have a posiƟve but small eﬀect – reducing emissions by between 
0.1% and 0.6%, depending on tax rates. However, further research is needed to beƩer understand the 
environmental implicaƟons of wealth taxes – in parƟcular, their impact on investment behaviour and 
associated emissions.  
2.2 How wealth taxes can contribute to the goals of a post-growth transiƟon 
Wealth taxes are widely regarded as a core component of post-growth policy (Fitzpatrick et al., 2022). 
However, scholars envision wealth taxes serving a range of purposes, which can be broadly categorised 
into four goals (in no parƟcular order):  
Goal number 1: Redistribute wealth. Achieving a more equitable distribuƟon of wealth is oŌen the 
main goal of wealth taxes in post-growth literature and is a near-universal aim across post-growth 
policy research (Fitzpatrick et al., 2022). RedistribuƟon is seen as a worthwhile objecƟve in itself, 
especially during a post-growth transiƟon, as inequality may otherwise increase without addiƟonal 
predistribuƟve, redistribuƟve, or protecƟve measures (PikeƩy and Saez, 2014; Jackson and Victor, 
2016).  
Goal number 2: Eradicate extreme wealth. Some authors take the goal of redistribuƟon further, 
arguing for the need to eradicate extreme wealth altogether (e.g. Buch-Hansen and Koch, 2019). Those 
working within suﬃciency or ‘limitarian’ frameworks (Robeyns, 2022) emphasise the importance of 
imposing ceilings on consumpƟon, producƟon, and aﬄuence (Gough, 2022; Khan et al., 2023; 
Bärnthaler, 2024). Wealth taxes are one tool that can contribute to this objecƟve. Recent work by the 
New Economics FoundaƟon explores the idea of an ‘extreme wealth line’ (Balata et al., 2025), arguing 
that such a threshold could help to idenƟfy the point at which extreme wealth becomes harmful to 
society, democracy, and the environment, and could help challenge the noƟon of limitless wealth 
accumulaƟon. However, there is no consensus on what consƟtutes extreme wealth or on where such 
thresholds should be set (Buch-Hansen and Koch, 2019). In a survey of ﬁŌeen millionaires in six middle-
to-high income countries, Balata et al., (2025) ﬁnd that the most popular absolute threshold for 
‘extreme’ wealth is $10m (equivalent to €8.9m in May 2025), while Robeyns et al. (2021) ﬁnd that the 
Dutch public primarily place the threshold between €1m and €3m. Balata et al. (2025) also survey 
poliƟcians and policy experts and report a preference for relaƟve thresholds. In our analysis, we 
therefore use both an absolute threshold of €8.9m following the ﬁndings by Balata et al. (2025), and 
a relaƟve threshold based on the 99th percenƟle of net wealth (approximately €2.4m – in line with 
Robeyns et al. (2021)), in order to explore two suitably disƟnct cut-oﬀs.

Goal number 3: Curb rent-extracƟon. The third goal involves shiŌing the economy away from rent-
extracƟng acƟviƟes to improve needs saƟsfacƟon, reduce growth dependencies, and address a key 
process through which inequality is (re)produced (Straƞord, 2020). Rent extracƟon takes many forms 
– for example, the for-proﬁt provision of housing by landlords, and the growing ﬁnancial sector, which 
increasingly “redistribute[s] money to [itself], at great cost to the rest of society” (Dietz and O’Neill, 
2013, p.104) and extracts value created elsewhere (Mazzucato, 2018; Bärnthaler and Gough, 2023). 
Such rent extracƟng acƟviƟes impede social-ecological transformaƟon (Straƞord, 2020) and should 
form as small a share of a post-growth economy as possible (Dietz and O’Neill, 2013, p.110). In this 
context, Fanning et al. (2020) introduce the concept of ‘appropriaƟng systems’, which – unlike 
provisioning systems – extract rents to saƟsfy the wants of elites at the expense of society at large. To 
target wealth associated with rent extracƟon, we focus on ﬁnancial wealth and property wealth in 
excess of the value of a household’s main residence – referred to here as investment property wealth. 
Taxing these types of wealth may contribute to reducing the power of rent-extracƟng, appropriaƟng 
systems – or, in Keynes’ famous phrase, to the ‘euthanasia of the renƟer’ (Keynes, 1936, chapter 24). 
Land wealth could also be construed as a source of rent-extracƟon, but due to data limitaƟons1, we 
are not able to include it in our metric.  
Goal number 4: Reduce emissions. Several authors also argue that wealth taxes can generate 
environmental beneﬁts, as greenhouse gas emissions and other environmental pressures are highly 
unevenly distributed along wealth lines (Gore, 2022; Tian et al., 2024; Büchs et al., 2024b). There are 
several channels through which wealth taxes could impact emission levels. ConsumpƟon and 
investment channels are criƟcal. Emissions associated with wealth holdings are substanƟal, and vary 
substanƟally across asset types and countries (Chancel and Rehm, 2025), so wealth taxes could reduce 
environmental impacts by constraining the emission-intensive investment and consumpƟon acƟvity of 
the very wealthy. However, due to data constraints, we focus only on the inequality channel, i.e. how 
lower inequality may translate into lower emissions. In this context, Knight et al. (2017) ﬁnd the 
wealth-inequality elasƟcity of consumpƟon-based CO2 emissions to be 0.795, meaning that a 1% 
decrease in wealth inequality is associated with a 0.795% decrease in per capita CO2 emissions.  
 
3. Methods and data 
3.1 Tax models and assessment criteria 
While taxes on wealth can exist in many forms, our analysis focuses on taxes on stocks of wealth, rather 
than on wealth transfers (such as inheritance or giŌ taxes) or income from wealth (such as taxes on 
capital gains, proﬁts and interest), in line with most of the wealth-tax literature. This choice is primarily 
driven by methodological and data constraints. Although the HFCS includes informaƟon on inheritance 
and giŌs, these data are widely regarded as unreliable, and no straighƞorward correcƟon method 
exists (Alvaredo et al., 2017). In addiƟon, taxes on wealth transfers or increases diﬀer in ways that 
make them less relevant to our goals and criteria. For example, such taxes are levied only when gains 
are realised, making their incidence irregular and unpredictable, with no direct link between the total 
stock of wealth and the resulƟng tax burden or eﬀects. Because we are primarily interested in how a 
tax would aﬀect outcomes related to stocks of wealth, we focus on taxes directly levied on those stocks.

There is also an inherent connecƟon between taxes on stocks of wealth and taxes on income from 
wealth. A tax on a stock of wealth must ulƟmately be paid out of income, either from asset sales or 
from the returns generated by those assets, making a net wealth tax broadly comparable to a tax on 
capital income. For example, a 1% tax on assets worth €1m with an annual return of 5% is comparable 
to a 20% tax on the resulƟng €50,000 in income. However, the two taxes are not equivalent. Revenue 
from capital income taxes ﬂuctuates with returns, whereas revenue from a tax on the stock of wealth 
can be more stable if the value of that stock varies less with changes in economic growth. If returns 
fall to 1% the following year, due to, for example, a ﬁnancial crisis, the capital income tax rate required 
to maintain the same revenue would be 100%. AlternaƟvely, if the capital income tax rate remained at 
20%, revenue would fall by four-ﬁŌhs. A tax on stocks of wealth avoids these ﬂuctuaƟons. The tax 
model with the broadest base and highest burden in our analysis corresponds to a capital income tax

5. Discussion  
In this secƟon, we place our ﬁndings in the context of the broader literature and discuss their 
implicaƟons in more detail. To put our revenue esƟmates into perspecƟve, government spending on 
social protecƟon in our country sample totalled around €3 trillion in 2017 (OECD, no date). A wealth 
tax with higher rates and lower thresholds could raise up to €605bn – around 20% of this expenditure 
(with actual revenues likely being somewhat lower due to evasion/avoidance) – or, from an MMT 
perspecƟve, create substanƟal macroeconomic space for increased spending on social and ecological 
objecƟves. Therefore, our analysis shows that wealth taxes can play an important role in enabling post-
growth welfare states.  
Our analysis regarding wealth redistribuƟon and emission reducƟon is roughly in line with the limited 
number of exisƟng studies. Our esƟmate that the share of wealth held by the top 1% could be reduced 
by 0.73% (under a net wealth tax with higher rates and a higher threshold) is within the range of 0.16% 
to 0.84% given by Apostel and O’Neill (2022). Our esƟmate that CO2 emissions could be reduced by up 
to 0.77% is also similar to the esƟmate of 0.1%-0.6% from Apostel and O’Neill (2022). 
5.1 EvaluaƟng tax designs against post-growth goals  
Our analysis shows that the most eﬀecƟve tax design varies depending on the goal of the tax. If the 
goal is to maximise revenue or the amount of wealth recirculated, reduce the share of wealth held by 
the top 10%, reduce the number of households with ‘extreme’ wealth, or reduce CO2 emissions, then 
a tax on net wealth with a lower threshold and higher rates is most eﬀecƟve. If the goal is to maximise


## Page 20


20 
 
the progressivity of the tax or reduce the share of wealth held by the top 1%, a tax on net wealth with 
a higher threshold and higher rates yields the best results. If the goal is to reduce rent extracƟon, a tax 
on ﬁnancial and investment property wealth with higher rates and a lower threshold performs best.  
5.2 Trade-oﬀs 
Our analysis also highlights conﬂicts between the goals. Figure 10 presents these visually showing the 
relaƟve eﬀecƟveness of taxes on diﬀerent kinds of wealth in relaƟon to the four goals, as well as total 
revenue. Figure 10 corresponds to the ‘higher rates, lower threshold’ models. The paƩern is similar for 
other models. Outcomes for each of the criteria are indexed against the most eﬀecƟve opƟon, which 
is indexed to 100. Where there are mulƟple criteria per goal, an average is taken. 
As ﬁgure 10 shows, a tax on net wealth would be the most redistribuƟve. A tax on ﬁnancial and 
investment property wealth would be most eﬀecƟve at shiŌing the economy away from rent-seeking 
acƟviƟes but would not be as eﬀecƟve at redistribuƟng overall wealth. However, if rent extracƟon was 
reduced, redistribuƟve taxaƟon would have less work to do. Evidence from the UK also indicates higher 
levels of public support for taxes on ﬁnancial and investment property wealth than other types of 
wealth (Rowlingson et al., 2020), based on percepƟons that this type of wealth is more likely to be 
‘unearned’. However, while a tax on ﬁnancial and investment property wealth must form part of a 
comprehensive policy package to curb rent extracƟon, it is unlikely to be an adequate response on its 
own in an otherwise unchanged system. As (Straƞord, 2020, p.1) points out, rent extracƟon – ‘an 
economic reward which is sustained through control of assets that cannot be quickly and widely 
replicated, and which exceeds proporƟonate compensaƟon for the labour of the recipient’ – is a 
structural symptom of an economy built on oŌen privaƟsed control over scarce assets. Varoufakis' 
(2024) account of technofeudalism reinforces this: because today’s dominant rents increasingly stem 
from the structural power of digital plaƞorms and cloud capital, taxaƟon alone is insuﬃcient, meaning 
that even a well-designed wealth tax must be complemented by broader reforms, such as regulaƟng 
or breaking up dominant plaƞorms, placing key digital infrastructures under public or commons 
ownership, and limiƟng the monopolisaƟon of data and algorithms.

not be the case. This highlights how trade-oﬀs between goals may become more pronounced over

the 90th percenƟle will generate more revenue and redistribute more wealth than one with an

revenue. Careful planning is therefore required to ensure that liquidity issues are not excessive, but if 
the goal is a more equal distribuƟon of assets, it may not be undesirable for the wealthiest households 
to sell some of their holdings to pay the tax bill. 
5.3 LimitaƟons 
The analysis in this arƟcle has several limitaƟons. Firstly, the analysis is staƟc. This means the results 
only represent the immediate, or ‘day aŌer’, eﬀects of a wealth tax. Our analysis is thus, in eﬀect, 
conducted as if it were a one-oﬀ wealth tax, whereas ideally research should examine the impacts of 
recurring taxes.  
Secondly, we do not consider tax avoidance or evasion in our empirical analysis because exisƟng 
esƟmates of the elasƟcity of taxable wealth in response to changes in wealth tax rates vary enormously 
– by a factor of 800 (Advani and Tarrant, 2021) – reﬂecƟng methodological diﬀerences and the strong 
inﬂuence of policy, cultural, and regulatory contexts (OECD, 2018; Advani and Tarrant, 2021). 
Meaningful esƟmates for our tax designs are therefore not feasible, as these designs would be 
unprecedented in geographical scope, rate and threshold structure, and tax base. Nonetheless, design 
choices can limit avoidance and evasion – for example, taxing on the basis of ciƟzenship rather than 
residency, or taxing worldwide assets to reduce capital mobility (OECD, 2018; Advani and Tarrant, 
2021). Improvements in informaƟon technology and internaƟonal data-sharing (Saez and Zucman, 
2022) have already reduced evasion threefold over the past decade (Alstadsaeter et al., 2024), and 
wealth tax returns could be pre-ﬁlled by the tax authoriƟes to further narrow avoidance opportuniƟes 
(Saez and Zucman, 2019). It is also worth noƟng that the social and poliƟcal-economic context implied 
by a post-growth transiƟon is very diﬀerent from the current poliƟcal-economic arrangement. 
Behavioural responses to taxaƟon, and the ability of authoriƟes to deal with such responses, may 
therefore diﬀer.  
Thirdly, there are myriad interacƟon eﬀects that cannot be fully anƟcipated. For example, a tax on 
wealth stocks may have repercussions for the revenue from other taxes, as such a tax would parƟally 
replace, rather than simply add to, the revenue from capital income or inheritance taxes (because if 
stocks of wealth have been taxed, income from wealth, and the amount of wealth transferred upon 
death, will be comparaƟvely lower). Some potenƟal interacƟon eﬀects could also be posiƟve. For 
example, Büchs et al. (2021) explore diﬀerent ways of ‘recycling’ revenues from carbon taxes in 
European countries. They show that by using revenues to equitably expand the provisioning of green 
public services such as renewable energy and public transport, greater environmental and social 
beneﬁts can be realised. The same could be achieved with wealth tax revenues.  
Finally, although their potenƟal to generate more growth-independent tax revenue is one potenƟal

6. Conclusion 
Wealth taxes are a commonly proposed policy within the post-growth literature, yet an assessment of 
their performance against post-growth goals and empirical esƟmates of their potenƟal eﬀects remain 
limited. We contribute by using microsimulaƟon to explore, for the ﬁrst Ɵme, how twelve concrete 
wealth-tax conﬁguraƟons perform against four key goals of a post-growth transiƟon: reducing 
inequality, eradicaƟng extreme wealth, curbing rent extracƟon, and reducing CO2 emissions. 
Our results show that no single tax design advances all goals equally. For example, taxes on net wealth 
are the most eﬀecƟve for redistribuƟon, extreme wealth reducƟon, and CO2 emission decreases, while 
taxes on ﬁnancial and investment property wealth are the most eﬀecƟve at reducing rent-seeking 
acƟvity. These trade-oﬀs highlight that wealth taxes cannot serve as a standalone soluƟon; instead, 
they must be embedded within broader eco-social policy packages that complement their strengths 
and miƟgate their limitaƟons.  
In an era of polycrisis, where ecological breakdown and extreme inequality are mutually reinforcing, 
there is an urgent need for policies that transform the underlying poliƟcal-economic dynamics. Our 
analysis demonstrates that well-designed wealth taxes can make a meaningful contribuƟon to this 
transformaƟon by recirculaƟng wealth, constraining harmful forms of accumulaƟon, and supporƟng 
more equitable and sustainable provisioning systems. Their transformaƟve potenƟal, however, 
depends on being coupled with wider reforms – such as regulatory measures to curb rent extracƟon, 
democraƟse ownership, and strengthen universal eco-social provisioning – that together can support 
a just and sustainable post-growth transiƟon.
