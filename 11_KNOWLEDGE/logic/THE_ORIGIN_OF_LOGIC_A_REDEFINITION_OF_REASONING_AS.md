# **Part I — The Limits of Classical Logic**
### **1 The Origin of Symbolic Reasoning**
The earliest formal treatment of reasoning, from Aristotle through Frege, conceived logic as the manipulation of symbols that stand for propositions. Its goal was syntactic validity: if premises are true, conclusions must follow. This separation of _form_ from _content_ allowed reasoning to be generalised across mathematics, law, and language. Yet every formal calculus begins with an _encoding_ —a prior act that converts continuous experience into discrete symbols. Once the encoding is fixed, logic enforces internal consistency but cannot question its own representational assumptions. Thus, classical logic explains _how_ inference proceeds within a language but not _why_ that language arose or _how_ biological observers generate the encodings that make reasoning possible.
* * *
### **2 The Cartesian Partition**
Seventeenth-century science divided existence into _res cogitans_ (thinking substance) and _res extensa_ (extended substance). Logic was assigned to the former, physics to the latter. This methodological dualism enabled precision in both domains but erased the causal bridge between neural structure and rational form. Empirical evidence now shows that reasoning capacity fluctuates with physiological variables—arousal, sleep, metabolic state, hormonal balance. Logical performance therefore cannot be divorced from biology. The Cartesian partition produced two complementary blind spots: physics ignored the observer’s contribution, and logic ignored embodiment. Both errors manifest as paradoxes when the system that measures is excluded from what is measured.
* * *
### **3 Mathematical Logic and Computation**
The twentieth century sought total formal control through axioms, proofs, and algorithms. Mathematical logic matured into proof theory, model theory, and recursion theory, culminating in computability: reasoning as mechanical procedure. These achievements established the digital age, yet their very formalism exposed new limits.
  1. **Representation dependence:** a theorem-proving machine validates only what its symbols encode; mis-encoding yields perfectly valid but meaningless results.


  2. **Computational irreducibility:** many natural systems cannot be compressed into finite derivations; prediction requires iterative feedback rather than deduction.


  3. **Semantic gap:** syntactic validity need not entail empirical truth.  
Computation therefore demonstrates that logic is executable but _not self-generating_ : symbolic systems require external, embodied agents to define and correct their encodings.


* * *
### **4 Incompleteness and the Boundary of Formal Systems**
Gödel’s incompleteness theorems and Turing’s undecidability result formalised the ceiling of self-contained logic. Any consistent, sufficiently expressive system contains true statements unprovable within itself, and no universal algorithm can decide all halting problems. These are not failures of reasoning but demonstrations that closure within a static symbol set is impossible. Progress demands _meta-reference_ : stepping outside the current formalism to redefine its premises. Biological cognition achieves this naturally—it re-encodes through perception, emotion, and learning. Living reasoning is therefore open, recursive, and adaptive, in contrast to the closed consistency of formal calculus. The boundary identified by Gödel and Turing is precisely the point at which logic becomes a biological feedback process.
* * *
### **5 The Physical Turn: Logic as Behaviour of Information**
Modern physics erodes the final assumption of detachment. Quantum measurement shows that observation alters outcome; systems and observers are entangled through information exchange. Complex-systems research reveals order emerging from recursive interaction without central command. Across scales, three invariants recur: **context-dependence, feedback, and compression**. These are the same primitives that sustain inference and memory in neural tissue. Logic, in this view, is not an ethereal calculus but the deterministic behaviour of information seeking minimal-energy stability under interaction. Symbolic logic becomes a surface manifestation of a deeper process by which matter, energy, and cognition preserve structural continuity.
