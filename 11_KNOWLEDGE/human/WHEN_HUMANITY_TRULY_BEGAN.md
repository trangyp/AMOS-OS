</style></head><body><article id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-802c-b607-d1bc55d0137e" class="page sans"><header><h1 class="page-title" dir="auto"><strong>When Humanity Truly Began</strong></h1><p class="page-description" dir="auto"></p></header><div class="page-body"><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h3 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8087-b7a4-c1bbc7496f33" class=""><strong>A factual account of emergence, constraint, and the limits of explanation</strong></h3></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8011-9847-c20d2bb17099" class=""><strong>The Beginning Was Not a Moment</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80bc-aead-de96c86520b8" class="">The question <em>“when did humanity begin?”</em> assumes that humanity is an event—something that activates once a sufficient threshold of intelligence, language, symbolism, or technology is crossed. But this assumption fails under scrutiny from every empirical discipline that can bear on it. Evolutionary biology shows no discontinuities, only gradients; population genetics shows no sudden fixation of a uniquely “human” trait; neurodevelopment shows no acceleration that would allow a rapid transition; archaeology shows accumulation, not ignition. There is no layer in the sediment, no mutation in the genome, no change in brain volume or morphology that marks a before and after. Any date offered as a beginning is a retrospective convenience, not a discovery.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8027-b8a9-e6da2ab653b5" class="">Humanity did not begin. <strong>It emerged—slowly, because it was constrained to.</strong></p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80e2-9db5-d940946c2b81" class="">From the moment the hominin lineage diverged from other great apes approximately <strong>six to seven million years ago</strong>, survival was shaped by conditions that have never changed. Upright walking reduced locomotor energy cost but narrowed the pelvis, forcing birth to occur earlier in neural development. Encephalization increased cognitive capacity but raised resting metabolic demand to roughly <strong>20–25% of total energy expenditure</strong>, an extreme outlier among mammals. These two facts alone fixed a permanent tradeoff: humans would be born neurologically unfinished and energetically expensive, requiring prolonged dependency and social buffering for survival. No technology, institution, or cultural practice has altered this constraint.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8046-89ee-ea6c6cae7465" class="">By the time anatomically modern humans appear in the fossil record around <strong>300,000 years ago</strong>, this pattern is already locked in. Brain size falls within the modern range, but maturation remains slow. The prefrontal cortex—supporting impulse inhibition, foresight, moral evaluation, and uncertainty tolerance—does not fully integrate until approximately <strong>the mid-twenties</strong>, a timeline unchanged across populations and eras. This is not a cultural lag; it is an anatomical one. Every human society that persisted across generations did so while absorbing decades of immaturity, error, and incomplete judgment into its social structure. Societies that failed to contain this developmental reality did not persist long enough to leave descendants.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8055-afd1-eb475c1feb0c" class="">Human infants, born at roughly <strong>25% of adult brain volume</strong>, require years of continuous care. Childhood dependency lasts more than a decade; social and cognitive dependency often extends longer. No human population has ever survived without cooperative caregiving, shared provisioning, and multigenerational investment. This is not an ethical preference. It is a biological requirement imposed by obstetrics and neurodevelopment. Attempts to compress, outsource, or technologically bypass this dependency reliably produce measurable deficits in executive function, emotional regulation, and adaptive judgment later in life.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-808e-8272-ef50dc7281d7" class="">The environments under which these traits stabilized were not benign. For most of <em>Homo sapiens’</em> existence, climate was volatile on timescales shorter than a human lifespan. Paleoclimate records show repeated temperature swings of <strong>5–8°C within decades</strong>, sea-level changes exceeding <strong>100 meters</strong>, and regional ecosystem collapse recurring across millennia. During the Last Ice Age alone, atmospheric CO₂ remained near <strong>180–190 ppm</strong>, primary productivity was suppressed, and habitable zones shifted repeatedly. Under these conditions, survival favored redundancy, flexibility, and restraint. Over-optimization failed. Systems that could not tolerate error collapsed.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80b8-bddc-d4cac6ce0a05" class=""><strong>Cognition</strong> evolved accordingly. Human intelligence developed under information scarcity, delayed feedback, and real consequence. Neural architecture favors energy minimization, pattern compression, and integration over time rather than sustained abstraction. When cognitive effort is consistently offloaded, neural engagement declines; when feedback is removed, error detection weakens. This is not a moral claim but a measurable one, observed in automation bias, skill degradation under assistance, and reduced transfer when external scaffolding replaces internal reasoning. The brain allocates effort where effort is rewarded. This has always been true.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-804c-ac59-e2312a7dcc92" class=""><strong>Emotional</strong> and <strong>social regulation</strong> have therefore always been primary. Across development, affective systems mature before executive ones. Across societies, norms, rituals, and sanctions arise before formal logic or codified law. These are not cultural accidents; they are stabilizing responses to a nervous system that cannot tolerate unregulated acceleration. History records the consequences repeatedly: periods of rapid informational or technological expansion without corresponding integration reliably produce instability before adaptation occurs.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8086-8138-cac6cda27e86" class="">For millions of years before writing, continuity depended on embodied practice, imitation, repetition, and social enforcement. Knowledge survived not because it was abstracted, but because it was lived. Groups that failed to transmit skills, seasonal patterns, social rules, and threat responses across generations disappeared regardless of individual cleverness. Intelligence that could not be regulated was not selected for.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80d5-9df4-dd68fa18bef1" class="">This is why the search for a single beginning fails. Humanity did not start with intelligence—cognition long predates our species. It did not start with tools—stone tools appear more than a million years before <em>Homo sapiens</em>. It did not start with culture—symbolic behaviors accumulate unevenly across tens of thousands of years. What distinguishes humanity is not the appearance of any one capacity, but the stabilization of a pattern: slow development, high dependency, metabolic constraint, social containment, and low tolerance for instability, carried forward reliably across time.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8060-9b33-fbcf189c21eb" class="">Humanity began, in the only sense the evidence allows, when organisms shaped by these constraints could preserve themselves without tearing their biological or social systems apart.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-802f-97fc-f324e98d8f68" class="">Any account that begins with civilization, language, or science begins far too late. Those are late-stage expressions layered onto an ancient organism whose defining features were fixed long before it built anything permanent.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80a0-a270-cecf6a56d493" class="">The true beginning of humanity is not found at a date. <strong>It is found in the conditions that have never changed.</strong></p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80c1-8074-f3e3eeda174b" class=""><strong>The World Humanity Had to Survive Was Not Stable</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80d7-863a-eb1b7878073b" class="">From roughly <strong>115,000 to 11,700 years ago</strong>, humanity lived through the last Ice Age—the terminal phase of the Pleistocene. This was not a marginal backdrop. It was the dominant selective environment under which modern humans consolidated their behavioral, social, and cognitive patterns.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80db-8651-edaa7d045086" class="">During this period, vast ice sheets covered northern North America and northern Europe. The Laurentide ice sheet alone reached thicknesses of <strong>three to four kilometers</strong>, reshaping atmospheric circulation and global climate. Sea levels were <strong>120–130 meters lower than today</strong>, exposing land bridges while erasing entire coastal ecosystems that humans intermittently depended on. Atmospheric carbon dioxide hovered around <strong>180–190 parts per million</strong>, less than half of modern levels, constraining plant productivity worldwide.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-800f-b0a9-e83f88877a7b" class="">The climate was not merely cold. It was unstable. Ice-core records show repeated <strong>Dansgaard–Oeschger events</strong>, in which regional temperatures rose <strong>5–8°C within decades</strong>, followed by rapid cooling. Periodic <strong>Heinrich events</strong> discharged massive volumes of ice into the North Atlantic, disrupting ocean circulation and collapsing ecosystems across continents.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8029-9eda-fd747e10fb7c" class="">These conditions imposed non-negotiable constraints. Large mammals migrated unpredictably or disappeared. Habitable zones shifted within a few human generations. Carrying capacity fluctuated sharply. In this environment, no population could survive through optimization or specialization alone.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8065-873b-e5c31fb82fdc" class="">Human survival required mobility, social coordination, and memory that extended beyond individuals. Long childhood dependency—biologically unavoidable in humans—could only be supported through cooperative breeding and intergenerational care. Knowledge had to persist across time without written records. Failure to transmit skills, migration routes, seasonal patterns, or social norms was fatal.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8038-9fa0-cd8efbf2f59c" class="">No large-scale agriculture was possible. No permanent cities could exist. These were not cultural absences. They were ecological impossibilities.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-809b-8ef6-fceab1d95f0c" class="">Crucially, <strong>humanity at this stage was already fully Homo sapiens</strong>. Anatomically modern humans predate this entire Ice Age by approximately <strong>180,000 years</strong>. The Ice Age did not create modern humans. It tested them.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><hr id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8083-884c-f12b46df918a"/></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-804e-8fd0-dfceedbe9e6d" class=""><strong>Modern Humans Existed Long Before Civilization</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8091-aad7-cab5e88d0d37" class="">The fossil and genetic record is unambiguous. <strong>Homo sapiens emerged around 300,000 years ago</strong>, with early remains found at sites such as <strong>Jebel Irhoud in Morocco</strong> and <strong>Omo Kibish in Ethiopia</strong>. These individuals possessed brain sizes within the modern human range and facial structures recognizably human, though regional variation remained high.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80fe-afd6-dc2449117883" class="">By <strong>70,000–50,000 years ago</strong>, humans occupied Africa, Europe, Asia, and Sahul (Australia–New Guinea). This expansion was not linear or uncontested. Modern humans encountered and interbred with other hominin populations, most notably <strong>Neanderthals</strong> and <strong>Denisovans</strong>.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8060-89df-c8c1e68c5219" class="">Whole-genome sequencing confirms that:</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><ul id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-802c-bab9-ca2cd5ee846c" class="bulleted-list"><li style="list-style-type:disc">Non-African populations carry <strong>~1–2% Neanderthal DNA</strong>.</li></ul></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><ul id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80d7-9bd9-cf81b7474ed4" class="bulleted-list"><li style="list-style-type:disc">Melanesian populations carry up to <strong>~5% Denisovan DNA</strong>.</li></ul></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><ul id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80b6-9700-fbae7dcf5c10" class="bulleted-list"><li style="list-style-type:disc">Introgressed genes affect immune response and environmental adaptation, including high-altitude physiology in Tibetan populations.</li></ul></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80b6-8191-e9f1abe0c7e1" class="">Modern humanity is therefore <strong>genetically composite</strong>, not isolated or pure. This is settled genomics, not speculation.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-806c-8d3a-eb9f78aaca61" class="">By the onset of the Ice Age’s most severe phases, humans already possessed the full biological equipment we recognize today. What followed was not the invention of humanity, but its endurance under pressure.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><hr id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-804f-a80b-c909f2747802"/></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8020-85ed-fa5ef8825fa8" class=""><strong>The Capacity We Recognize as Human Was Already Present</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-806e-8662-ebda3c025a0c" class="">Archaeological evidence shows that well before <strong>40,000 years ago</strong>, human populations consistently demonstrated behaviors that require planning, abstraction, and social coordination.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8072-9071-c16004840758" class="">Controlled fire use was habitual. Tools were composite, combining stone, wood, and adhesives such as resin. Clothing was tailored, with bone needles appearing by <strong>40,000–30,000 years ago</strong>, indicating foresight and symbolic marking of identity. Long-distance trade networks transported obsidian and other materials <strong>hundreds of kilometers</strong>, linking groups across landscapes.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8053-a836-e76faa871024" class="">Symbolic behavior is especially diagnostic. At <strong>Blombos Cave</strong> in South Africa, engraved ochre dates to <strong>~75,000 years ago</strong>, and shell beads to <strong>~100,000 years ago</strong>. These objects serve no subsistence function. They encode meaning, affiliation, and memory. Intentional burials with grave goods appear repeatedly in the Levant, suggesting ritualized treatment of the dead.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8035-922f-d198c2a05d75" class="">This record directly contradicts the idea of a sudden “cognitive revolution” tied to civilization. The capacities associated with modern humanity—symbolism, planning, cooperation—are <strong>deeply pre-Ice-Age</strong>.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><hr id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80c3-af4a-ffecb886491c"/></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8081-9f7b-f36e76946675" class=""><strong>Survival at the Edge of Extinction</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8065-a91a-f33b9041beba" class="">Between <strong>26,000 and 19,000 years ago</strong>, the planet entered the <strong>Last Glacial Maximum</strong>. Ice coverage peaked. Deserts expanded. Many megafaunal species collapsed or went extinct. Human populations contracted severely.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8001-8f25-e92c5d617d0b" class="">Global population estimates during this period suggest fewer than <strong>five million humans worldwide</strong>. Genetic data indicate population bottlenecks and regional isolation. Cultural traditions diverged as groups adapted locally to extreme conditions.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8094-8057-d2896d9ee386" class="">Yet humanity persisted.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80eb-8059-f717170bae76" class="">It did so <strong>without agriculture</strong>, <strong>without writing</strong>, <strong>without states</strong>, and <strong>without formal institutions</strong>. Survival depended on social cohesion, ecological knowledge, and emotional regulation under stress.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80b4-8a46-e80b03684143" class="">This fact alone falsifies any claim that civilization produces intelligence. Civilization followed survival; it did not cause it.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><hr id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8062-b365-c064f68678c8"/></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8077-890d-e27618482cee" class=""><strong>Abrupt Climate Change Repeatedly Tested Human Adaptability</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-809f-b843-f18d05b6a2cf" class="">As the Ice Age waned, the climate did not stabilize smoothly. Around <strong>14,700 years ago</strong>, the <strong>Bølling–Allerød warming</strong> rapidly expanded ecosystems and habitable zones. This was followed abruptly by the <strong>Younger Dryas</strong>, a return to near-glacial conditions beginning around <strong>12,900 years ago</strong> and lasting roughly <strong>1,200 years</strong>.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-807e-95fe-c937bd392ef1" class="">Temperatures dropped within decades. Resources collapsed. Human groups were forced to reorganize again.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80fd-8526-c1f262703ee0" class="">This period selected not for efficiency or optimization, but for <strong>flexibility</strong>. Groups that over-specialized failed. Groups that maintained diversified subsistence strategies and social resilience survived.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><hr id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8051-94d5-cf90b0ab59c0"/></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-800f-a76a-ce4fda337d89" class=""><strong>Complex Societies Emerged Before Agriculture</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8076-8cb8-f475da2adae7" class="">Well before farming, human societies displayed structural complexity. The <strong>Natufian culture</strong> of the Levant, dating from <strong>~15,000 to 11,700 years ago</strong>, built semi-sedentary villages with stone architecture, formal cemeteries, and ritual spaces. These communities relied heavily on wild cereals but had not yet domesticated plants.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-806b-8d54-ee0c5b842670" class="">Settlement therefore preceded agriculture.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8014-b901-d45165a3fdda" class="">Agriculture did not invent complexity. It stabilized it—once climate conditions allowed.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><hr id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8072-bd5b-f6839ed6020b"/></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8044-9cab-e3940b9caa38" class=""><strong>Environmental Stability Changed What Was Possible</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8001-bdb1-f7142f0835af" class="">The <strong>Holocene</strong>, beginning <strong>11,700 years ago</strong>, brought relative climatic stability. Seasonal patterns became predictable. CO₂ levels rose. Glaciers retreated. Sea levels increased, reshaping coastlines and concentrating populations.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80bf-a318-e83916c3499d" class="">These changes were environmental permissions, not intellectual breakthroughs. They allowed humans to invest in long-term land use without catastrophic risk.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><hr id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8017-984e-cb2c0129c720"/></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80fa-a3a2-f65d743f0e2f" class=""><strong>Agriculture Was an Ecological Response, Not an Intellectual Breakthrough</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80f0-b6d0-dd2e43496cbe" class="">Agriculture emerged independently in multiple regions between <strong>11,000 and 5,000 BCE</strong>: the Fertile Crescent, China, New Guinea, Mesoamerica, and the Andes. In every case, it followed climatic stabilization.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-806d-8952-f9e7cc6fc150" class="">There is no evidence that farming arose during periods of high climatic volatility. It required predictability.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><hr id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80f7-b285-eb8a227a3497"/></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8004-b0cb-cebb1e14df99" class=""><strong>Progress Came With Measurable Biological Costs</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8054-9b9d-fc564dbeac60" class="">Skeletal evidence across regions shows that early farmers experienced:</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><ul id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80cd-bc61-d3d9dccdc980" class="bulleted-list"><li style="list-style-type:disc">Reduced stature by <strong>5–10 cm</strong></li></ul></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><ul id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8044-8d6b-c4b384c2e1d7" class="bulleted-list"><li style="list-style-type:disc">Increased dental caries</li></ul></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><ul id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-800e-aa18-db9a5d96b855" class="bulleted-list"><li style="list-style-type:disc">Iron-deficiency anemia</li></ul></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><ul id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-805f-b4b3-c48e0539c621" class="bulleted-list"><li style="list-style-type:disc">Higher infectious disease loads</li></ul></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><ul id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8079-9737-eccb940c472f" class="bulleted-list"><li style="list-style-type:disc">Shorter average lifespans</li></ul></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80ae-b86b-f12af8a6494a" class="">Agriculture increased population density at the cost of individual health. It was not a biological upgrade. It was a <strong>population-support technology</strong>.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><hr id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8000-8b31-d6b2d67fa70a"/></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80ad-b3d6-e5a44bb299ae" class=""><strong>Cities Emerged to Manage Scale and Control</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-807f-a29b-e4a47b99834c" class="">Between <strong>6,000 and 4,000 years ago</strong>, cities and states emerged. Writing appeared around <strong>3200 BCE</strong> in Sumer, slightly later in Egypt, and around <strong>1200 BCE</strong> in China.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80e3-b9b5-fd65b56026bd" class="">Early writing systems served accounting, taxation, administration, and ritual authority. They did not originate as tools for philosophical reflection.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><hr id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8047-a208-ffab7725ac35"/></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80cb-8348-d4ac23c54af6" class=""><strong>Ideas Accelerated Without Changing the Brain</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8037-aee6-f1c1b60a9764" class="">Between <strong>800 and 200 BCE</strong>, major ethical and philosophical traditions emerged across Eurasia. This <strong>Axial Age</strong> represents cultural compression, not biological change. No genetic or neurological shift occurred.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><hr id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80ab-bf2e-f444ad67c7b1"/></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8014-b71e-f12ebc02d2c7" class=""><strong>Acceleration Became Exponential Only Recently</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80c1-8630-f1f928e55773" class="">The scientific method is roughly <strong>400 years old</strong>. Industrialization began around <strong>1750</strong>. Electricity reshaped societies in the 19th century. Digital systems emerged after <strong>1950</strong>.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8038-9b64-ea2d972a5aa8" class="">This entire acceleration occupies <strong>less than 0.01%</strong> of Homo sapiens’ existence.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><hr id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-809c-a954-d09d93dc97d3"/></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-801f-a24c-c87201d2a240" class=""><strong>What the Evidence Forces Us to Accept</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80ee-b2c1-c9edba2ca8c4" class="">Humanity predates civilization by hundreds of thousands of years. Intelligence evolved under constraint, not abundance. Climate stability enabled agriculture; agriculture enabled hierarchy. Technology now accelerates far faster than biology. Our nervous systems remain adapted to Ice Age conditions.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><hr id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80d3-adbd-d2f927601412"/></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8001-8850-cc7ef6e39bb4" class=""><strong>Where Measurement Reaches Its Limit</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-8099-be1a-f64d59f833e6" class="">Science can date bones, sequence genomes, and model climates. It cannot precisely locate the origin of consciousness, moral reasoning, or language. These are distributed, gradual, and non-fossilizing phenomena.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80e7-bd78-f119dd03ef82" class="">This is not a failure of science. It is a boundary of the method.</p></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><hr id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-807a-99de-faad35dc6d4f"/></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><h2 id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80d3-a158-c30b3a3c4c96" class=""><strong>Humanity Is Vastly Older Than Its Tools</strong></h2></div><div style="display:contents" dir="auto"><p id="2e6c5e6f-95bd-80fd-8e0f-df516d2dee26" class="">Homo sapiens is <strong>~300,000 years old</strong>. Agriculture is <strong>~10,000 years old</strong>. Writing is <strong>~5,000 years old</strong>.
