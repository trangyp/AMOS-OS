# **Why China Is Flooded With Unused EVs — and Why Vietnam Is the Wrong Place for Them**
## **Executive Truth (Plain)**
China does not have an EV success problem.
China has an **overproduction and misallocation problem**.
Vietnam does not have an EV demand problem.
Vietnam has a **grid, income, and infrastructure constraint problem**.
When these two realities are forced together, the result is not electrification.
It is **risk dumping**.
* * *
## **Part I — Why China Is Overloaded With Unused EVs**
### **1\. China Built EVs as an Industrial Policy, Not a Consumer Response**
China’s EV boom was not demand-led.
It was:
  * subsidy-driven,


  * capacity-driven,


  * and geopolitically motivated.


EVs were treated as:
  * a strategic manufacturing sector,


  * a battery industrial base,


  * a jobs engine,


  * and a future export wedge.


This produced **extraordinary supply** — far beyond organic domestic demand.
* * *
### **2\. The Result: Production Without Absorption**
China now has:
  * millions of EVs produced faster than they can be sold,


  * inventory accumulation,


  * price wars,


  * shrinking margins,


  * and stranded vehicles.


Many EVs exist because:
  * factories must keep running,


  * employment must be maintained,


  * capital must be utilized.


Not because consumers asked for them.
* * *
### **3\. The Structural Imbalance**
China optimized for:
  * manufacturing throughput,


  * scale,


  * cost compression.


It did **not** equally optimize for:
  * urban charging capacity,


  * grid resilience,


  * parking + charging integration,


  * long-term vehicle utilization.


So EVs accumulate:
  * in fleets,


  * in storage,


  * in low-utilization contexts.


This is how you get **unused EVs at scale**.
* * *
### **4\. Export Becomes the Pressure Valve**
When domestic absorption stalls, excess production must go somewhere.
Exports are not optional.
They are **structurally necessary**.
This creates a dangerous dynamic:
> EVs must be placed, not necessarily adopted.
* * *
## **Part II — Why Vietnam Looks Attractive (But Isn’t)**
On paper, Vietnam looks ideal:
  * fast urbanization,


  * growing middle class,


  * motorbike → car transition,


  * government interest in electrification.


But this is **surface analysis**.
* * *
## **Part III — Why Vietnam Is Structurally the Wrong Sink for China’s EV Excess**
### **1\. Vietnam’s Grid Is Not Built for Sudden EV Load**
Vietnam’s power system is:
  * growth-constrained,


  * transmission-limited,


  * distribution-fragile in cities,


  * peak-sensitive.


EVs are not neutral loads.
They are **coincident peak loads**.
Dumping EVs into Vietnam without:
  * charging coordination,


  * peak pricing,


  * grid reinforcement,


  * load authority


does not accelerate electrification.
It destabilizes the system.
* * *
### **2\. Charging Infrastructure Is Being Placed, Not Planned**
In Vietnam, charging stations are often:
  * intuitively sited,


  * real-estate driven,


  * politically convenient,


  * brand-visibility oriented.


Not:
  * transformer-headroom based,


  * feeder-capacity calculated,


  * coincidence-modeled.


This is not scaling.
It is improvisation.
* * *
### **3\. Vietnamese Households Are Price-Sensitive, Not Subsidy-Shielded**
Unlike China, Vietnam:
  * cannot subsidize EVs indefinitely,


  * has tight household budgets,


  * is highly sensitive to electricity tariffs.


When EV charging:
  * pushes households into higher tariff tiers,


  * increases grid instability,


  * causes outages or throttling,


EVs stop being “green”.
They become **financial liabilities**.
* * *
### **4\. Vietnam Does Not Have Excess Grid Slack**
China can absorb inefficiency with scale.
Vietnam cannot.
Every poorly placed charger:
  * accelerates transformer aging,


  * increases losses,


  * forces emergency upgrades,


  * socializes cost through tariffs.


Vietnam does not have margin for wasteful electrification.
* * *
## **Part IV — Why This Is Not “Technology Transfer” but Risk Transfer**
What is being exported is not just vehicles.
It is:
  * **load risk**


  * **grid stress**


  * **utilization risk**


  * **infrastructure debt**


China offloads:
  * surplus production pressure.


Vietnam absorbs:
  * operational instability,


  * grid reinforcement costs,


  * public dissatisfaction.


That is not partnership.
That is **asymmetric risk migration**.
* * *
## **Part V — The Core Misunderstanding**
> EVs are not standalone products.
> They are grid-coupled systems.
Exporting EVs without exporting:
  * grid investment,


  * charging governance,


  * peak management,


  * cost responsibility


is equivalent to exporting:
> industrial load without industrial planning.
* * *
## **Part VI — Why This Fails Long-Term**
If EVs scale faster than the grid:
  * charging is throttled,


  * reliability drops,


  * tariffs rise,


  * adoption stalls,


  * trust collapses.


EVs then become:
  * underused,


  * resented,


  * politically sensitive.


Exactly what happened in parts of China.
* * *
## **Part VII — The Only Way This Works (And It’s Hard)**
Vietnam should not import:
  * vehicles first.


It should import:
  * **charging logic**


  * **grid-integrated deployment rules**


  * **peak pricing frameworks**


  * **load authority**


  * **cost ownership models**


EVs must arrive **after** :
  * charging is governed,


  * grid limits are respected,


  * responsibility is assigned.


Otherwise, Vietnam repeats China’s mistake — without China’s buffer.
* * *
## **Final Line (Unavoidable)**
> China’s unused EVs are the result of overproduction without absorption.
> Vietnam cannot absorb them without destabilization.
EVs exported without system design are not clean technology.
They are **displaced industrial pressure**.
And grids always collect their debt.
